How it works

Enter your age, when you'd like to retire, what you've saved and what you add each month, then pick an investment mix. A worked example

Sam is 30, has $10,000 saved, puts $500 a month toward retirement, and wants to retire at 65. With a balanced mix of stocks and bonds, the calculator assumes 4.5% a year after inflation. These are its starting numbers.

  • At 65: $537,803 in today's dollars.
  • Income it could support: 4% of that is $21,512 a year, or $1,793 a month, on top of Social Security.
  • Where it comes from: Sam puts in the $10,000 plus $206,672 of contributions, in today's dollars; growth supplies the other $321,131. Over 35 years, the investments do most of the work.

Three changes Sam could make, one at a time:

  • Save $750 a month instead of $500: $783,368 at 65.
  • Invest for growth (mostly stocks, 5.75% after inflation) instead of balanced: $711,143.
  • Retire at 60 instead of 65: $405,105, about 25% less. The last five years add a quarter of the total, because they're when the balance is largest.

Questions

Why show "inflation adjusted" figures?

A million dollars in 30 years buys what about $410,000 buys today at 3% inflation. Today's-dollar figures let you compare the result with prices you know.

What's the difference between historical and Monte Carlo?

Historical runs your plan through every real stretch of markets since 1926, month by month. Monte Carlo draws thousands of random return sequences with the volatility you set, including ones history never produced.

What rate of return should I use?

The calculator starts at 8.5% before inflation, a little under the 8.7% a year that 60% stocks and 40% bonds earned from 1926 through 2025. All stocks earned 10.4%. Use less for a more conservative mix, and remember that history is a guide, not a promise.

Should I use a traditional or Roth 401(k)?

Traditional saves tax now and is taxed later; Roth is taxed now and tax-free later. Traditional usually wins if your tax rate will be lower in retirement, Roth if it will be the same or higher. Many people split contributions between the two.

What is a glide path?

A gradual shift from stocks toward bonds as retirement approaches, so a crash just before you retire does less damage. Target-date funds follow one automatically.

A worked example: when you save matters

Three savers each put away $336,000 over 30 years, starting from $10,000 and earning 8.5% a year with 3% inflation. Only the timing differs.

  • Saving more as income grows: $400 a month for ten years, $900 for the next ten, $1,500 for the last ten. The most common real-life pattern ends with $472,555 in today's dollars.
  • The same amount every month: $933 a month for 30 years ends with $642,777.

When would I use Stages instead of Advanced?

Whenever your saving will change in a planned way: a debt paid off, a promotion, part-time years, or a coast phase where you stop contributing and let the balance grow.

Does it matter when I save, if the total is the same?

A lot. Money saved early has decades longer to grow. In the example above, the same $336,000 of contributions ends anywhere from $472,555 to $817,035 depending only on the order.

Can each stage have its own investment mix?

How do I model a career break?

Add a stage with a contribution of zero for the years you expect to be out of work. The balance keeps growing during it.

Retirement withdrawal calculator: will your money last?

Test how much you can safely spend in retirement. The Drawdown Simulator runs your savings, spending, Social Security and investment mix through every real retirement since 1926, and 5,000 random ones, and shows how often the money lasted.

How to use it

Enter what you'll have at retirement, how many years it has to last, and a withdrawal rate. Add Social Security (estimated for you, or from your statement), pensions and big future expenses. The Success rate is the share of retirements where the money never ran out.

The 4% rule, and where it comes from

In 1994 the financial planner William Bengen asked a simple question: what's the most a retiree could have taken from a stock and bond portfolio in the first year, raising it with inflation every year after, and never run out in 30 years, in any period he had data for? The answer was about 4%. On $1,000,000 that's $40,000 in the first year, then a raise each year to keep up with prices, no matter what markets do.

The rule is a starting point, not a promise. It assumes you never cut spending, never earn another dollar, and hold a fixed mix of stocks and bonds. Real retirees adjust, which is why the simulator also tests five flexible strategies: Guyton-Klinger guardrails, floor and ceiling, the Yale endowment rule, a fixed percentage, and variable percentage withdrawal (VPW).

A worked example

Pat retires at 65 with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of stocks and bonds and wants the money to last 30 years. These are the calculator's starting numbers, so this is what you see when you open it.

  • At 4%: the plan survived 94.4% of the 30-year retirements since 1926. The four that failed began in 1965, 1966, 1968 and 1969, when high inflation met a flat stock market. Retiring in 1966, the money ran out in year 27.
  • The typical outcome: in the middle retirement, Pat still had about $1.6 million left after 30 years, in today's dollars. The 4% rule is sized for the worst cases, so most retirees following it end with more than they started with.
  • At 3.5%: $35,000 a year, and the plan survived 100% of the historical retirements.
  • For 40 years instead of 30: 4% survived 86.9% of them. That's why people retiring at 50 usually plan on 3.25% to 3.5%.

Flexible strategies trade certainty of income for certainty of lasting. With guardrails starting at 5% ($50,000), every historical retirement lasted 30 years, but the 1966 retiree's spending was cut as low as $17,434 a year in today's dollars along the way. The simulator shows the lowest spending each strategy would have required, not just whether the money lasted.

How to read your results

  • Success rate: above about 90% is a common comfort level for a fixed withdrawal. 100% isn't necessary if you'd be willing to trim spending in a bad decade.
  • The chart: the shaded band is the range of historical outcomes, and each faint line is one starting year. The lines that dive toward zero show what a bad sequence looks like.
  • Worst starting years: check whether you could live with the path your plan takes in 1929, 1937 or 1966. That's more useful than the average.
  • Monte Carlo: random sequences built from history, including ones that never happened. A lower success rate here than in the historical test is normal.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the withdrawal as take-home money. The amounts are before tax; traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals are taxed as income.
  • Leaving out Social Security. It covers a large share of spending for most retirees and changes the safe rate a lot.
  • Using 30 years for an early retirement, or forgetting that one spouse may live into their late 90s.
  • Holding too little in stocks. Very conservative portfolios fail more often over 30 years because they don't keep up with inflation.
  • Forgetting big one-time costs: a new roof, a car every ten years, helping a child. Add them as future expenses.

Questions

What is a safe withdrawal rate?

For a 30-year retirement with a mix of stocks and bonds, 4% has survived about 95% of historical starts. Longer retirements, like one beginning at 50, call for something closer to 3.25% to 3.5%, or a strategy that trims spending after bad years.

What is sequence of returns risk?

Losses early in retirement hurt far more than the same losses later, because you're selling investments at low prices to live on. Two retirees with the same average return can end very differently. It's why the simulator tests real sequences, like retiring in 1966 or 1929.

Are the amounts before or after tax?

Before tax. Withdrawals from traditional accounts and part of Social Security are taxed, so use the Income Tax tool's retirement mode to see what you'd keep.

Does the 4% rule still work today?

It survived about 95% of the 30-year retirements since 1926, and every one that failed began in the late 1960s. Some researchers suggest a lower rate when stock prices are high relative to earnings or bond yields are low. The simulator lets you start the record later, or test 3.5%, to see how much margin you'd gain.

What mix of stocks and bonds should I use in retirement?

Historically, 50% to 75% in stocks has supported the highest safe withdrawal rates over 30 years. Much less, and the portfolio struggles to outpace inflation; much more, and early crashes do more harm. Try a few mixes and compare the success rate and the worst years.

When should I cut spending?

Guardrail strategies cut spending by a set amount, often 10%, when the withdrawal rate rises too far above where it started, and give a raise when markets do well. Choose Guardrails as the strategy to see how often cuts would have happened and how deep they went.

Early retirement calculator: getting to 59½ without penalties

Withdrawals from a 401(k) or IRA before age 59½ usually cost a 10% penalty on top of income tax. Retire earlier and you need a plan for the years in between. This calculator runs every legal route side by side, with the real 2026 tax, penalties and ACA health premiums, and tests each against every market since 1926.

The routes it compares

  • Taxable brokerage and Roth contributions: money you can reach any time.
  • Roth conversion ladder: convert a year's spending to a Roth each year and spend it five years later, penalty-free.
  • 72(t) payments: fixed yearly withdrawals from an IRA with no penalty. See the 72(t) calculator.
  • Rule of 55: leave your job in or after the year you turn 55 and that employer's 401(k) opens up penalty-free.
  • Governmental 457(b) plans, which never carry the penalty, and simply paying the 10% as a comparison.

The calculator also builds a blended plan that mixes these, since the best answer usually uses more than one.

A worked example

Robin and Chris retire at 50 and spend $60,000 a year. They have $1,000,000 in traditional 401(k)s and IRAs, $150,000 in Roth IRAs ($90,000 of it their own contributions), and $450,000 in a brokerage account. These are the calculator's starting numbers. They have 9½ years to cover before 59½.

  • What they can reach today: the brokerage account and their Roth contributions, $540,000 in all. That's the key figure for any early retirement.
  • A conversion ladder needs a runway: money converted this year can't be spent for five years, so they need five years of spending from other sources first, $300,000. Their $540,000 covers it, which makes the ladder workable.
  • 72(t) is the alternative: at 50, the most amortization at 5% could pay from the whole $1,000,000 is $60,312 a year, locked in until 59½. Enough to live on, but inflexible, and it adds taxable income every year.
  • Taxes can be close to zero: for a couple, long-term gains are taxed at 0% while taxable income stays under $98,900. Selling brokerage shares to live on, with only part of each sale counted as a gain, often means no federal income tax at all.
  • Health premiums hinge on income: ACA credits stop above $84,600 for a household of two. Conversions and gains both count, so the ladder's size is a trade-off between tax later and premiums now. In states that expanded Medicaid, very low income moves you to Medicaid instead.

  • Holds to 59½ is the share of historical markets in which the plan paid every year's spending without running dry.
  • Tax, penalties and premiums are totals over the bridge years, in today's dollars. The lowest total isn't always best if it leaves little in the accounts.

Common mistakes

  • Retiring with nearly everything in pre-tax accounts and nothing to live on for the first five years.

  • Forgetting the five-year clock applies to each conversion separately.
  • Planning taxes without planning health insurance. Before Medicare, the two are linked through income.

Questions

How much do I need to retire early?

Enough to cover your spending for longer, typically 28 to 33 times a year's spending instead of 25, and enough outside traditional accounts, or reachable through the routes above, to cover the years before 59½.

Why do health premiums matter so much?

Before Medicare at 65, marketplace subsidies depend on your income. The way you draw money, from a brokerage account or a Roth versus a traditional IRA, changes that income and can move premiums by thousands a year. The tool prices each plan's premiums for you.

How does a Roth conversion ladder work?

Each year you convert about a year's spending from a traditional IRA to a Roth, paying income tax on it. Five years later that conversion can be withdrawn with no tax and no penalty. After the first five years, each year's conversion is ready just as you need it, like rungs on a ladder.

Can I withdraw Roth IRA contributions early?

Yes. What you contributed yourself, not earnings or conversions, can come out at any age with no tax or penalty. That's why Roth contributions count as reachable money here.

What is the rule of 55?

If you leave your employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55 (50 for many public-safety workers), withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) or 403(b) have no 10% penalty. It doesn't apply to IRAs or to plans from earlier jobs.

Is a 457(b) different?

A governmental 457(b), common for state and local government workers, has no 10% early-withdrawal penalty at all once you leave the job, at any age. That makes it the most flexible pre-tax money for an early retiree.

72(t) calculator: SEPP payments before 59½

Section 72(t) of the tax code lets you take substantially equal periodic payments (SEPP) from an IRA before 59½ without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. This calculator sizes the payment, splits off an IRA just big enough to make it, and shows whether the plan carries you to 59½ in every market since 1926, next to the other ways of reaching retirement money early.

How 72(t) payments work

Normally, taking money out of an IRA or 401(k) before 59½ costs an extra 10% on top of the income tax. A 72(t) plan is a deal with the IRS: you pick a yearly payment using one of the IRS's formulas, take exactly that every year, and in return the 10% doesn't apply.

  • You're locked in: the payments must continue for five years or until 59½, whichever comes later. Start at 50 and you're committed until 59½; start at 57 and you're committed until 62.
  • It's still taxable: each payment is ordinary income, taxed like a paycheck. Only the 10% goes away.
  • From a 401(k): it only works after you've left that employer. Most people roll the money into an IRA first, which also lets them choose how much goes into the plan.

The three ways to set the payment

  • Amortization: the balance is paid out like a mortgage in reverse, over your IRS life expectancy at an interest rate you choose. The payment is the same number of dollars every year and is usually the largest of the three. This is what most people use.
  • Annuitization: uses an annuity factor instead of a loan formula. It lands within a few dollars of amortization, so this calculator uses amortization for both.
  • RMD method: each year's balance divided by your life expectancy. It starts much lower and changes every year with the market.

Two choices set the size. Life expectancy: the IRS allows three tables; the Single Life table gives the shortest life expectancy, so the largest payment, and it's the one this calculator uses. Interest rate: for amortization, anything up to the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate for either of the two months before your first payment (IRS Notice 2022-6). A lower rate gives a smaller payment, which is a way to take less without splitting the IRA.

A worked example

Dana retires at 52 with $900,000 in a rollover IRA and wants $40,000 a year from it, before tax, to live on until 59½. The IRS life expectancy for a 52-year-old is 34.3 years.

  • The whole IRA, amortization at 5%: $55,391 a year. More than Dana needs, and she'd be locked into taking all of it.
  • The whole IRA, RMD method: $26,239 the first year ($900,000 ÷ 34.3), then recalculated each year. Not enough.
  • Split the IRA: move $649,929 into a separate IRA and run amortization at 5% on that alone. It pays exactly $40,000 a year. The other $250,071 stays in its own IRA, outside the plan, for emergencies or later.

Dana is now committed to $40,000 every year through age 59. If it's her only income and she files single, that's about $2,620 of federal income tax after the standard deduction, and no 10% penalty, which would otherwise have added $4,000 a year.

One catch: an amortization payment is a fixed number of dollars, so it buys less each year. At 3% inflation, the eighth payment is worth about $32,500 in today's dollars. The calculator counts this, so a plan that looks tight in year one shows up as tight later.

What breaking the plan costs

Changing the payment before the lock ends is called a modification. That includes stopping early, taking extra, taking less, adding money to the plan's IRA, or rolling it elsewhere. The penalty is retroactive: the 10% comes back on every payment taken so far, plus interest, all due in the year of the change. If Dana stopped after four years, she'd owe $16,000 plus interest on top of her normal tax.

  • Allowed exceptions: death, disability, and a one-time switch from amortization or annuitization to the RMD method. The switch is the escape hatch if markets fall hard: the payment drops with the balance, and the plan stays intact.
  • Running out: if the plan's IRA is used up, the payments simply stop. That isn't a modification.
  • After the lock: once you pass 59½ and five years, you can take any amount, stop, or merge the IRAs again.

When 72(t) fits, and when something else is better

  • 72(t) fits when most of your savings is pre-tax, you have little in a brokerage account or Roth, and you're retiring well before 55. It pays from day one, with no waiting period.
  • Rule of 55: leave your job in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for public-safety workers) and that employer's 401(k) opens penalty-free, with no lock-in at all. Rolling it into an IRA first gives this up.
  • Roth conversion ladder: converted money can be withdrawn penalty-free five years later. It's more flexible, but you need five years of other money to live on first.
  • A brokerage account: no rules or ages, and gains can be taxed at 0% at lower incomes. Often the cheapest money to spend first.
  • Just paying the 10%: for a gap of a year or two, the penalty can cost less than committing to years of fixed payments.

Common mistakes

  • Running the plan on the whole IRA when you only need part of it.
  • Starting payments from a 401(k) while still working for that employer.
  • Nudging the amount "just a little" in a later year. Even small changes can count as a modification, so keep it to the dollar.
  • Depositing new contributions or rollovers into the plan's IRA.
  • Starting late and forgetting the five-year rule: start at 57 and you're locked in until 62, not 59½.
  • Leaving the tax forms wrong. Your 1099-R should show distribution code 2. If it shows code 1, file Form 5329 with exception number 02 so you aren't charged the 10%.

Roth conversion calculator

When converting pays off

A conversion means paying income tax now so the money grows and comes out tax-free later. It wins when your rate now is lower than it would be later, often in the years between retiring and starting Social Security and RMDs. It also shrinks future RMDs, and helps a surviving spouse, who files single on similar income.

It loses when your tax rate later will be lower than now: a modest traditional balance, a move to a no-income-tax state, or heirs in a low bracket. The calculator doesn't assume either answer; it runs both and compares.

A worked example

Sam and Jo are both 62 and retired, with $1,500,000 in traditional IRAs, $150,000 in Roth IRAs and $400,000 in a brokerage account. They spend $100,000 a year and will claim Social Security at 67. These are the calculator's starting numbers, so this is what opens.

  • The plan: each year, convert enough to fill the 22% bracket. With no other income, that's $243,600 in the first year: the top of the 22% bracket for a couple ($211,400) plus the $32,200 standard deduction. The tax is paid from the brokerage account, so the full amount lands in the Roth.
  • How long it runs: the amount changes from year to year as the brokerage money paying the tax runs down and Social Security begins. By 70 the traditional accounts are empty, $1,288,352 converted in all. RMDs, which would have peaked at $169,099 a year, never happen.
  • Lifetime tax: $305,186 with conversions against $503,928 without. Without conversions, much of that tax comes decades later, on RMDs in their 80s and 90s, so the calculator also discounts each year's tax to today's value. Measured that way, converting saves $6,839.
  • Medicare surcharges: IRMAA totals $13,836 instead of $51,360. Big conversion years raise premiums two years later, at 65, 66 and 72, but there are no large RMDs pushing income over the IRMAA lines for the rest of their lives.
  • What's left at 92: $223,304 more after-tax wealth with conversions, counting traditional dollars at 68 cents, since they still owe tax on the way out.

Both measures favor converting here, but by very different margins. That's typical: the lifetime tax saving often looks small once discounted, while the after-tax wealth gap is large, because Roth money compounds with no tax owed on it.

Questions

How much should I convert each year?

A common approach is to fill the 12% or 22% bracket each year, stopping below the next IRMAA threshold after 63 (Medicare looks back two years). The tool shows lifetime tax and after-tax net worth for each choice.

Is there a five-year rule on conversions?

Yes. Before 59½, each conversion has its own five-year clock before it can be withdrawn without the 10% penalty. After 59½ it doesn't apply to conversions.

Should I pay the tax from the conversion?

Usually not. Paying from a taxable account moves the whole amount into the Roth; having tax withheld leaves less there, and before 59½ the withheld part is penalized too.

What is the widow's penalty?

After one spouse dies, the survivor files single from the following year, with brackets about half as wide, while income from RMDs and the larger Social Security benefit stays much the same. Is it too late to convert after RMDs start?

No, but each year's RMD has to be taken first and can't itself be converted. Conversions after 73 or 75 still shrink later RMDs and the tax heirs pay, at the cost of a higher income that year.

Do Roth conversions affect Social Security taxes?

Yes. A conversion is income, so in a year you also collect Social Security it can make more of your benefit taxable. That's one reason conversions often work best before benefits start.

RMD calculator: required minimum distributions by age

Project your required minimum distributions every year to age 100, see your largest one, and test whether Roth conversions before RMDs begin would lower your lifetime tax. Set Strategy to No conversions to see your RMDs on their own.

How RMDs work

A traditional IRA or 401(k) lets money grow untaxed for decades, but not forever. From a set age, the IRS requires you to take out a minimum amount each year, and that withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income whether you need the money or not.

  • When they start: age 73 if you were born from 1951 through 1959, and 75 if born in 1960 or later (SECURE 2.0).
  • How much: each year's RMD is your traditional balance on December 31 of the prior year divided by the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table factor for your age: 26.5 at 73, so about 3.8% of the balance, rising to about 6.3% at 85 and 11% at 95.
  • Which accounts: traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s and similar. Roth IRAs have no RMDs for the owner, and neither do Roth 401(k)s from 2024.
  • Missing one: costs a 25% excise tax on the shortfall, cut to 10% if corrected promptly.

A worked example

Sam and Jo are both 62, married, with $1,500,000 in traditional IRAs, and they expect their investments to earn 5% a year after inflation. These are the calculator's starting numbers. Born in 1964, their RMDs start at 75.

  • The balance keeps growing first: even with part of their spending coming out of it along the way, the traditional balance reaches $2,255,808 by 75, in today's dollars.
  • The first RMD: $2,255,808 ÷ 24.6, the IRS factor at 75, is $91,700, all of it taxable, on top of about $58,000 of Social Security.
  • The largest one: the factor shrinks every year, so the required share rises. Their RMDs peak at $169,099 in a single year.
  • With conversions: converting enough each year to fill the 22% bracket from 62 empties the traditional accounts before RMDs begin, so the peak RMD falls to nothing. The Roth conversion calculator page walks through what that costs and saves.

Why it matters: an RMD that size, plus Social Security, can push a couple into a higher bracket, make 85% of their benefits taxable, and cross the Medicare IRMAA line ($218,000 joint), raising premiums two years later. After one spouse dies, the survivor files single on nearly the same income, with half-size brackets.

Ways to manage RMDs

  • Roth conversions in the low-income years between retiring and 73 or 75 shrink the balance RMDs are figured on.
  • Qualified charitable distributions: from 70½ you can send IRA money straight to charity. It counts toward your RMD and isn't taxed.
  • Still working: if you're still employed and don't own 5% or more of the company, you can usually delay RMDs from that employer's 401(k) until you retire. IRAs get no such delay.
  • Spending from the IRA first in your 60s, instead of from a brokerage account, lowers the balance too, though conversions usually do it more tax-efficiently.

Common mistakes

  • Delaying the first RMD to April 1 of the next year, then taking two in one year and paying a higher bracket on both.
  • Taking the RMD from a Roth, or from the wrong account. You can combine IRA RMDs and take them from any IRA, but each 401(k) must pay its own.
  • Forgetting that RMDs raise the tax on Social Security and can trigger IRMAA.
  • Planning as if the balance will shrink. At typical returns it often keeps growing into your 80s despite the RMDs.

Questions

Can I reduce my RMDs?

Yes: Roth conversions in the years before RMDs start shrink the traditional balance they're figured on. From 70½ you can also give up to the annual limit directly to charity as a qualified charitable distribution, which counts toward the RMD and isn't taxed.

Why do large RMDs matter?

They're taxed as ordinary income, can make more of your Social Security taxable, and can push you over Medicare IRMAA thresholds, raising premiums two years later.

At what age do RMDs start?

73 if you were born from 1951 through 1959, and 75 if you were born in 1960 or later. The calculator works it out from your age.

How is my RMD calculated?

Your traditional balance on December 31 of the prior year divided by the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table factor for your age that year. At 75 the factor is 24.6, so a $1,000,000 balance means an RMD of about $40,650. If your spouse is your only beneficiary and more than ten years younger, a different table gives a smaller RMD.

Do Roth IRAs have RMDs?

Not for the original owner, and since 2024 neither do Roth 401(k)s. Heirs who inherit a Roth generally must empty it within ten years, but the withdrawals are tax-free.

Can I reinvest my RMD?

Yes, in a regular brokerage account, or in a Roth IRA if you have earned income for a contribution. You can't put it back into a traditional IRA or roll it over.

Retirement healthcare cost calculator: ACA and Medicare

Estimate what health coverage costs in retirement: ACA marketplace premiums and subsidies if you retire before 65, and Medicare Part B, Part D, Medigap and IRMAA surcharges after. Uses 2026 figures and scales premiums by your state and age.

Before 65: ACA subsidies

The premium tax credit caps what you pay for the benchmark Silver plan at a share of your income, from about 2% at low incomes to 9.96% in 2026. Above 400% of the federal poverty level the credit stops entirely, so a dollar over the line can cost thousands. Retirees can often keep income low by living on cash, Roth money or brokerage principal.

For 2026 coverage, 400% of the poverty level is $62,600 for one person and $84,600 for a household of two. The enhanced credits that removed this cliff from 2021 through 2025 have expired; the calculator also shows what you'd pay if they were restored.

A worked example

Lee and Kim retire at 62 in Illinois. At their age, the benchmark Silver plan for the two of them lists at $2,904 a month, about $34,850 a year. What they actually pay depends almost entirely on their income.

  • $40,000 of income: they pay 6.08% of it, $203 a month. The credit covers the rest.
  • $84,000 of income: just under the cliff, they pay the maximum 9.96% of income, $697 a month or $8,366 a year.
  • $85,000 of income: $400 over the line, the credit disappears and they pay the full $2,904 a month, $34,854 a year.

That extra $1,000 of income costs them $26,487 in premiums. It's why early retirees watch every source of income: a Roth conversion, a large capital gain or one extra IRA withdrawal can cross the line. Spending from savings or Roth contributions doesn't count as income, so a couple with a brokerage account can often live on $100,000 a year while reporting income well under $84,600.

To try it, set Retirement age to 62, Filing status to married, Spouse's age then to 62, State to Illinois, and Retirement MAGI to $84,000, then $85,000.

From 65: Medicare and IRMAA

The standard Part B premium is $202.90 a month in 2026. Above $109,000 of income single or $218,000 joint, IRMAA surcharges raise Part B and Part D, in tiers, based on your income from two years earlier.

  • Part B covers doctors and outpatient care; Part D covers prescriptions; a Medigap policy (like Plan G) fills most of the gaps in Original Medicare. Medicare Advantage plans bundle these, often with lower premiums but networks and different cost-sharing.
  • IRMAA is a cliff too: a couple one dollar over $218,000 pays $81.20 a month more per person for Part B, plus a Part D surcharge.
  • Enroll on time: signing up late for Part B, without other employer coverage, adds 10% to the premium for each full year you waited, for life.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that all of your Social Security counts toward ACA income, including the untaxed part.
  • Doing a Roth conversion in a year you're on the ACA without checking the cliff.
  • Budgeting only premiums. Deductibles and out-of-pocket costs can add several thousand dollars a year.
  • Missing the income two years back: a big conversion at 63 raises Medicare premiums at 65.
  • Assuming you can sign up for an ACA plan any time. You need a qualifying event, like losing job coverage, or the open enrollment window each fall.

Questions

What income counts for ACA subsidies?

Modified adjusted gross income: traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, pensions, interest, dividends, realized capital gains and all of your Social Security, including the untaxed part. Roth withdrawals and spending cash savings don't count.

Do Roth conversions affect Medicare premiums?

Yes. A conversion is income, and Medicare uses your income from two years before. Large conversions after 63 can trigger IRMAA surcharges.

What is the ACA subsidy cliff?

For 2026, premium tax credits stop completely above 400% of the federal poverty level: $62,600 for a single person and $84,600 for a couple. Just below it you pay at most 9.96% of income for the benchmark plan; just above it, the full price.

How much does health insurance cost before Medicare?

It depends mostly on your income, age and state. Without a subsidy, a Silver plan for a 62-year-old often runs $1,200 to $1,700 a month. With one, many early retirees pay a few hundred dollars a month or less.

Is COBRA better than an ACA plan?

COBRA keeps your employer plan, usually for up to 18 months, but you pay the full cost plus 2% with no subsidy. It can make sense to finish a year where you've met the deductible; otherwise an ACA plan with a credit is usually far cheaper.

Can I appeal IRMAA if my income dropped?

Yes. If income fell because you retired or stopped working, file Form SSA-44 to ask Social Security to use your current income instead of the figure from two years ago.

Alex is 30, has $10,000 invested, and spends $40,000 a year. At a 4% withdrawal rate, Alex's FIRE number is $1,000,000 in today's dollars. The calculator's starting numbers assume an 8.5% return and 3% inflation, about 5.3% a year after inflation.

2026 income tax calculator: federal, state and FICA

Estimate your 2026 take-home pay, or the tax on a year of retirement income. Uses the 2026 federal brackets and standard deduction, Social Security and Medicare tax, and the income tax rules of all 50 states and DC.

Two modes

  • Normal income: a salary, with 401(k) and HSA deductions, for net and take-home pay per month and per paycheck.

How the 2026 federal brackets work

Income tax is charged in layers. For a single filer in 2026, the first $12,400 of taxable income is taxed at 10%, the next $38,000 (up to $50,400) at 12%, then 22% up to $105,700, and higher rates above that. Taxable income is what's left after the standard deduction of $16,100 single or $32,200 for a married couple. Moving into a higher bracket only raises the rate on the dollars inside it.

What is the 2026 standard deduction?

$16,100 single and $32,200 married filing jointly. At 65 and over, an extra $2,050 single or $1,650 per spouse, plus the temporary $6,000 senior deduction for 2025 through 2028, which phases out above $75,000 single or $150,000 joint.

Your bracket is the rate on your last dollars; your effective rate is total tax divided by income, which is always lower because the first dollars are taxed at 10% or not at all.

Questions

How much house can I afford?

A common guideline keeps the house payment, including tax, insurance and PMI, under about 28% of gross income, and all debt payments under about 36%.

Is a 15-year or 30-year mortgage better?

A 15-year loan has a higher payment but usually a lower rate and far less total interest. A 30-year loan keeps the payment low and flexible; you can still pay extra.

When does PMI go away?

You can ask the lender to drop it at 20% equity, and it ends automatically at 22% based on the original schedule.

When you'll keep the loan past the break-even month, the point where the lower payment has repaid the closing costs. A rate drop of about 1 point or more often clears that bar within a few years.

Debt payoff calculator: avalanche vs. snowball

List your debts and see your debt-free date, the total interest you'll pay, and how much a payoff plan saves over paying only the minimums. Compare the avalanche and snowball methods side by side, month by month.

Avalanche or snowball?

Both put every spare dollar on one debt at a time and roll each paid-off minimum into the next. Avalanche targets the highest interest rate first and costs the least. Snowball clears the smallest balance first, for quicker wins that help many people stick with it.

The rollover is what makes either one work. When a debt is paid off, its minimum payment doesn't go back into your budget; it joins the extra payment on the next debt, so the amount aimed at each debt keeps growing.

A worked example

Jordan owes $49,400 across four debts, the calculator's starting list: a $9,800 credit card at 22.9%, a $1,900 store card at 8.9%, a $16,200 car loan at 7.4% and a $21,500 student loan at 5.5%. The minimum payments add up to $930 a month.

  • Minimums only: 123 months, over ten years, and $18,321 of interest.
  • Avalanche with $300 a month extra: the credit card goes first, and Jordan is debt-free in 47 months, just under four years, paying $8,155 of interest. That's $10,167 less than minimums alone.
  • Snowball with $300 extra: the store card goes first and is gone within months. Debt-free in 48 months with $8,578 of interest: one month longer and $424 more than avalanche.
  • $400 extra instead of $300: avalanche finishes in 43 months with $7,226 of interest.

Here the two methods end up close, because the smallest debt is small. The gap grows when the smallest balance also has the lowest rate, and a large card at a high rate waits its turn. Either way, the extra payment matters far more than the order.
