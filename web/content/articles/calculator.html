About this calculator

Answer six questions and see what your savings could grow to by the age you want to retire, in today's dollars, with a range for better and worse markets. It's the quickest way to answer the first retirement question: am I roughly on track?

How it works

Enter your age, when you'd like to retire, what you've saved and what you add each month, then pick an investment mix. The calculator grows your savings at a return after inflation, raising your contribution with prices each year, so every figure reads in today's money. The yearly income shown uses the 4% rule: about 4% of savings a year is what has historically lasted a 30-year retirement.

A worked example

Sam is 30, has $10,000 saved, puts $500 per month toward retirement, and wants to retire at 65. With a balanced mix of stocks and bonds, the calculator assumes 4.5% per year after inflation. These are its starting numbers.

  • At 65: $537,803 in today's dollars.
  • Income it could support: 4% of that is $21,512 per year, or $1,793 per month, on top of Social Security.
  • Where it comes from: Sam puts in the $10,000 plus $206,672 of contributions, in today's dollars; growth supplies the other $321,131. Over 35 years, the investments do most of the work.

Three changes Sam could make, one at a time:

  • Save $750 per month instead of $500: $783,368 at 65.
  • Invest for growth (mostly stocks, 5.75% after inflation) instead of balanced: $711,143.
  • Retire at 60 instead of 65: $405,105, about 25% less. The last five years add a quarter of the total, because they're when the balance is largest.

How much do you need?

A common starting point is 25 times the yearly spending your savings must cover, after Social Security and any pension. If Sam expects to spend $50,000 per year and Social Security covers $24,000, savings need to cover $26,000: about $650,000. Sam's $537,803 is short of that, and the $750-a-month plan clears it.

How to read your results

  • Today's dollars: everything is shown in what money buys now, so you can compare it with prices you know.
  • The range: the shaded band shows better and worse markets. Plan around the middle, and check that the low end is survivable.
  • Year by year: the table shows what you put in and what growth added each year.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a future balance in future dollars. A million dollars in 35 years buys what about $355,000 buys today, at 3% inflation.
  • Leaving out an employer match. Add it to what you save each month.
  • Assuming stock-market returns for a portfolio that's mostly cash.
  • Forgetting Social Security when judging whether the income is enough.

Questions

How much do I need to retire?

A common starting point is 25 times the yearly spending your savings must cover, after Social Security and any pension. That's the 4% rule turned around: 4% of 25 times your spending is one year of it.

What return should I assume?

After inflation, a mostly-stock portfolio has historically earned around 6% to 7% per year, and a balanced one around 4% to 5%. Planning with the lower end leaves room for bad decades.

Is my information stored anywhere?

Only in your browser, unless you choose to sign in. Every calculation runs in your browser. If you sign in, your household, saved scenarios and Guide answers are saved to your account so they follow you to other devices; the numbers you type into a calculator are not sent anywhere unless you save them. See Privacy.

Am I on track for retirement?

A rough benchmark is to have about one year's salary saved by 30, three times by 40, six times by 50, eight times by 60 and ten times by 67. The readiness guide gives a fuller answer, including Social Security.

How much should I save each month?

For someone starting in their 20s or early 30s, about 15% of pay, including any employer match, usually leads to a comfortable retirement. Starting later calls for more. Change the monthly amount to see its effect.
