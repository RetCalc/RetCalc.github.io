Social Security claiming calculator: 62, 67 or 70?

See what your Social Security check would be at every age from 62 to 70, what each choice adds up to over your life, the age at which waiting pays off, and, for a couple, what the spousal and survivor benefits do to the answer. Then test the usual argument for claiming early, taking the money and investing it, against every market since 1926.

How it works

Enter the monthly benefit at full retirement age from your Social Security statement (ssa.gov/myaccount), or let the calculator estimate one from your income and years worked. Your birth year sets your full retirement age: 66 for anyone born from 1943 to 1954, rising two months a year to 67 for 1960 and later. Then pick an age to claim and an age to plan through.

Claiming early cuts the check for every month before full retirement age: 5/9 of 1% a month for the first 36 months and 5/12 of 1% beyond, so 30% less at 62 with a full retirement age of 67. Waiting past it adds 2/3 of 1% a month, 8% a year, until 70: 24% more. These are permanent, and they are in today's dollars. Social Security's yearly cost-of-living raise keeps whatever you get level in buying power, so the calculator never needs an inflation assumption.

A worked example

Sam was born in 1964, so full retirement age is 67, and Sam's statement shows $2,400 a month at that age.

  • At 62: $1,680 a month. Living to 90, that's $564,480 in all.
  • At 67: $2,400 a month, $662,400 in all to 90.
  • At 70: $2,976 a month, $714,240 in all to 90.

Waiting from 62 to 67 pays off from age 78.7: before that, claiming at 62 has collected more; after it, 67 pulls ahead for good. Waiting from 67 to 70 pays off from 82.5, and 62 against 70 from 80.4. Live to 78 and 62 wins; live to 90 and 70 collects $150,000 more.

To try it, set Year of birth to 1964, Monthly benefit at full retirement age to $2,400, and move Claim at between 62, 67 and 70 with Plan through age at 90.

How to read your results

  • Every claiming age lists each check and the total through the age you planned, with the best marked. The best age depends almost entirely on that one number, so the strip below it matters more.
  • Which age pays the most, by how long you live shows the winning claiming age for every lifespan from 70 to 100. Nobody knows theirs; a 65-year-old today lives to about 84 on average, and in a couple one of the two usually passes 90.
  • Claim early and invest the checks runs the early claim through every retirement since 1926: the checks are invested until the later age, then the pot pays the difference between the two checks. Waiting is a guaranteed, inflation-protected 8% a year, so the market has to beat that after inflation, and in most start years a balanced mix didn't.
  • Taxed as income is the share of the benefit that counts as taxable at the other income you entered: none at low incomes, up to 85%. The Income Tax tool's retirement mode works out the tax itself.

Couples

Each spouse claims their own benefit at their own age. A spouse whose own full benefit is under half the other's gets a top-up to half, once both have claimed, reduced if it starts early. When one spouse dies the survivor keeps the larger of the two checks, including any delayed credits. That is why the higher earner in a couple is usually advised to wait: the larger check is the one that lasts as long as either of them does, while the lower earner's check stops at the first death and can be taken earlier at less cost.

Common mistakes

  • Claiming at 62 because the money is "yours". It is, at every age; the question is which schedule collects the most over the lifespan you actually have.
  • Judging by the break-even age alone. Break-even says when waiting catches up, not how much it pays past that point, or that it insures against outliving your savings.
  • Forgetting the survivor. For a couple, the higher earner's claiming age sets the check the widow or widower lives on.
  • Working while claiming early. Below full retirement age, $1 of benefit is withheld for every $2 earned over the year's limit. It is credited back later, but the early claim then bought nothing.
  • Treating the calculator's own figures as a forecast of the program. The rules shown are today's; a benefit change by Congress would move every age alike.

Questions

Is it better to take Social Security at 62 or 67?

At 62 the check is about 30% smaller for life. It collects more in all if you die before roughly 78 to 80, and less if you live past that. Most people who reach 65 do.

What is the break-even age for Social Security?

Roughly 78 to 80 for 62 against full retirement age, and 82 to 83 for full retirement age against 70, in today's dollars. The calculator works out yours from your own figures.

How much more do I get by waiting until 70?

8% a year past full retirement age: 24% more than the full benefit with a full retirement age of 67, and 77% more than the check at 62.

Should the higher earner in a couple wait?

Usually. The larger check is also the survivor benefit, so it is paid as long as either spouse lives. The lower earner's check ends at the first death, so taking it earlier costs less.

Can I work and collect Social Security?

Yes. Below full retirement age, $1 is withheld for every $2 earned over the year's limit, and the withheld months are credited back at full retirement age. From full retirement age there is no limit.

Is Social Security taxed?

Up to 85% of it can count as taxable income, depending on your other income. The share rises from nothing at low incomes to 85% once other income plus half the benefit passes $34,000 single or $44,000 married, thresholds that aren't indexed to inflation.
