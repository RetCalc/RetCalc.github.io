Social Security calculator: your benefit at 62, 67 or 70

See what your Social Security check will be at every age you could claim, from 62 to 70, worked out from your pay or from your statement. Then see what each choice adds up to over your life, the age at which waiting pays off, and, for a couple, the best pair of claiming ages and the survivor benefit.

How it works

Fill in a sentence about yourself: the year you were born, your pay today, and the ages you started work and will stop. That estimates your whole career, and Edit pay year by year shows every year of it: type what you actually earned in a past year, as your statement lists it (the calculator raises it by how much average wages have grown since, as Social Security does), or a future year's pay in today's dollars for a promotion, and the years after it follow. If you have your Social Security statement (ssa.gov/myaccount), use its monthly benefit at full retirement age instead. Your birth year sets your full retirement age: 66 for anyone born from 1943 to 1954, rising two months per year to 67 for 1960 and later. Then pick an age to claim and how long you expect to live.

Claiming early cuts the check for every month before full retirement age: 5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months and 5/12 of 1% beyond, so 30% less at 62 with a full retirement age of 67. Waiting past it adds 2/3 of 1% per month, 8% per year, until 70: 24% more. These are permanent, and they are in today's dollars. Social Security's yearly cost-of-living raise keeps whatever you get level in buying power, so the calculator never needs an inflation assumption.

A worked example

Sam was born in 1964, so full retirement age is 67, and Sam's statement shows $2,400 per month at that age.

  • At 62: $1,680 per month. Living to 90, that's $564,480 in all.
  • At 67: $2,400 per month, $662,400 in all to 90.
  • At 70: $2,976 per month, $714,240 in all to 90.

Waiting from 62 to 67 pays off from age 78.7. Before that, claiming at 62 has collected more. After it, 67 pulls ahead for good. Waiting from 67 to 70 pays off from 82.5, and 62 against 70 from 80.4. Live to 78 and 62 wins; live to 90 and 70 collects $150,000 more.

To try it, choose I have my Social Security statement, fill in 1964 and $2,400, set the age you expect to live to at 90, and click the steps for 62, 67 and 70.

How to read your results

  • Pick the age you claim shows your check at each age from 62 to 70 and its share of the full benefit, with the age that collects the most marked. How it's figured under it walks from your pay to your check: the average of your best 35 years, the formula that turns it into a full benefit, then the cut or credit for the age you chose.
  • Best age, by how long you live shows the winning claiming age for every lifespan from 70 to 100. Nobody knows theirs. A 65-year-old today lives to about 84 on average, and in a couple one of the two often passes 90.
  • When waiting pays off adds up your own checks from each claiming age and marks the break-even ages, where a later claim has caught up.
  • Taxed as income is the share of the benefit that counts as taxable at the other income you entered: none at low incomes, up to 85%. The Income Tax tool's retirement mode works out the tax itself.

Couples

Each spouse claims their own benefit at their own age. A spouse whose own full benefit is under half the other's gets a top-up to half, once both have claimed, reduced if it starts early. When one spouse dies the survivor keeps the larger of the two checks, including any delayed credits. That is why the higher earner in a couple is usually advised to wait: the larger check is the one that lasts as long as either of them does, while the lower earner's check stops at the first death and can be taken earlier at less cost. Best ages for the two of you tries all 81 pairs of claiming ages and shows which collects the most for the ages you each expect to live to.

Common mistakes

  • Claiming at 62 because the money is "yours". It is, at every age. The question is which schedule collects the most over the lifespan you actually have.
  • Judging by the break-even age alone. Break-even says when waiting catches up, not how much it pays past that point, or that it insures against outliving your savings.
  • Forgetting the survivor. For a couple, the higher earner's claiming age sets the check the widow or widower lives on.
  • Working while claiming early. Below full retirement age, $1 of benefit is withheld for every $2 earned over the year's limit. It is credited back later, but the early claim then bought nothing.
  • Treating the calculator's own figures as a forecast of the program. The rules shown are today's. A benefit change by Congress would move every age alike.

Questions

Is it better to take Social Security at 62 or 67?

At 62 the check is about 30% smaller for life. It collects more in all if you die before roughly 78 to 80, and less if you live past that. Most people who reach 65 do.

What is the break-even age for Social Security?

Roughly 78 to 80 for 62 against full retirement age, and 82 to 83 for full retirement age against 70, in today's dollars. The calculator works out yours from your own figures.

How much more do I get by waiting until 70?

8% per year past full retirement age: 24% more than the full benefit with a full retirement age of 67, and 77% more than the check at 62.

Should the higher earner in a couple wait?

Usually. The larger check is also the survivor benefit, so it is paid as long as either spouse lives. The lower earner's check ends at the first death, so taking it earlier costs less.

Can I work and collect Social Security?

Yes. Below full retirement age, $1 is withheld for every $2 earned over the year's limit, and the withheld months are credited back at full retirement age. From full retirement age there is no limit.

Is Social Security taxed?

Up to 85% of it can count as taxable income, depending on your other income. The share rises from nothing at low incomes to a cap of 85%. Benefits become taxable once other income plus half the benefit passes $25,000 single or $32,000 married, and the 85% tier starts at $34,000 single or $44,000 married. These thresholds aren't indexed to inflation.
