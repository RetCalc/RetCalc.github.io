Portfolio backtest: stock, bond and cash returns since 1926

Pick a mix of stocks, small-cap value, bonds and cash and see what it actually earned, year by year back to 1926: average return, return after inflation, volatility, the worst year, the deepest fall, and the best and worst of every 1, 5, 10, 20 and 30-year stretch.

The data

Stocks are the S&P 500 (and its predecessor index) with dividends reinvested; small-cap value is the Fama-French portfolio of small companies priced low against their book value; bonds are 10-year U.S. Treasuries; cash is one-month Treasury bills; inflation is the Consumer Price Index. The portfolio is rebalanced to your mix once a year, or every few years, when a holding drifts too far, or never, as you choose. Small value and cash start in July 1926, so a mix holding either starts in 1927.

What 100 years of markets show

Here are three mixes over the full record, 1926 through 2025, with no fees. Pick 100, 60 or 0 from the quick mixes under Asset mix to see each.

  • All stocks: 10.4% a year, or 7.3% after inflation. Stocks rose in 74 of the 100 years. The worst single year was −41.8%, and from the 1928 peak to the 1932 bottom the portfolio lost 62% of its value.
  • 60% stocks, 40% bonds: 8.7% a year, 5.6% after inflation. The worst year was −24.7%, and the deepest fall 37%, over the same Depression years. Giving up 1.8 points of return a year cut the worst losses by about 40%.
  • All bonds: 4.8% a year, but only 1.8% after inflation, and its deepest fall came recently: 18% from 2020 to 2022, as interest rates rose.

Time is what makes stocks safer. Over single years they're wild, but the worst 20-year stretch for all stocks still earned 0.8% a year after inflation, and the worst 30-year stretch earned 4.3% a year. For 60/40, the worst 20 years roughly broke even after inflation, and the worst 30 years earned 3.0% a year.

Small-cap value and cash

Open Asset mix to add the other two. Moving a third of a 60/40 mix's stocks into small-cap value (40% US stocks, 20% small value, 40% bonds) earned 9.69% a year from 1927 through 2025, against 8.65% for 60/40 over the same years, but its deepest fall was 44% instead of 37%. Small value has beaten the S&P 500 over the century, with long stretches behind it. Cash alone earned 3.29% a year, only 0.30% after inflation: it barely loses value, and barely grows.

Rebalancing

Left alone, a mix drifts toward whatever has grown fastest, usually stocks. Never rebalancing a 60/40 mix from 1926 earned 9.89% a year instead of 8.67%, but by 2025 it was 100% stocks, and its deepest fall was 42% instead of 37%. Rebalancing every year keeps the risk you chose.

How to read your results

  • Annual return is the compound growth rate, what a dollar actually grew at. It's lower than the simple average of yearly returns.
  • After inflation is the number that matters for spending power. A 3% return with 3% inflation leaves you where you started.
  • Volatility is how much returns swing year to year. Higher volatility means deeper falls on the way.
  • Deepest fall (maximum drawdown) is the largest drop from a peak to a later low. Ask yourself whether you'd have held on through it.
  • Rolling periods show the best, median and worst result for every stretch of a given length, which says more about a plan than one start date.

Common mistakes

  • Planning on the full-history average as if it were guaranteed. Future returns may be lower.
  • Looking only at nominal returns. The 1970s looked fine before inflation and poor after it.
  • Picking a start date that flatters the answer. Compare the rolling worst cases, not one window.
  • Forgetting fees. A 1% fee takes about 1 point off every return shown.

Questions

What has the stock market returned historically?

U.S. stocks have returned about 10% a year since 1926, or about 7% after inflation, with individual years ranging from about +50% to −40%.

What is a 60/40 portfolio?

60% stocks and 40% bonds, a classic balanced mix. Historically it has earned somewhat less than all stocks with much smaller falls. Set Stocks to 60 to see it.

What was the worst year for the stock market?

In this data, 1931, at about −42%. 2008 came close, at about −39%. After the 1929 crash, stocks with dividends reinvested didn't climb back above their 1928 peak until the end of 1936, and fell again the next year.

Are bonds safe?

Safer than stocks, not risk-free. Bonds lose value when interest rates rise, as in 2022, and inflation can erode them for years, as in the 1970s. Their main job in a portfolio is to fall less when stocks fall.

Does past performance predict future returns?

No. History shows the range of things that have happened, which is useful for testing a plan, but the next 30 years may look different. Plan with a margin, like a lower return or a lower withdrawal rate.
