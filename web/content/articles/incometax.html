2026 income tax calculator: federal, state and FICA

Estimate your 2026 take-home pay, or the tax on a year of retirement income. Uses the 2026 federal brackets and standard deduction, Social Security and Medicare tax, and the income tax rules of all 50 states and DC.

Two modes

  • Normal income: a salary, with pre-tax deductions as one figure or split by kind, for net and take-home pay per month and per paycheck.
  • Retirement income: traditional, Roth and brokerage withdrawals, Social Security and pensions, each taxed its own way, with capital-gain stacking, the 3.8% investment income tax and each state's retirement exclusions, which start at each state's own age. Enter your age, and your spouse's on a joint return.

How the 2026 federal brackets work

Income tax is charged in layers. For a single filer in 2026, the first $12,400 of taxable income is taxed at 10%, the next $38,000 (up to $50,400) at 12%, then 22% up to $105,700, and higher rates above that. Taxable income is what's left after the standard deduction of $16,100 single or $32,200 for a married couple. Moving into a higher bracket only raises the rate on the dollars inside it.

A worked example

Taylor is single, lives in Illinois and earns $100,000. These are the calculator's starting numbers.

  • Federal income tax: $100,000 less the $16,100 standard deduction leaves $83,900 taxable. 10% of the first $12,400, 12% of the next $38,000 and 22% of the remaining $33,500 comes to $13,170.
  • Social Security and Medicare (FICA): 7.65% of wages, $7,650.
  • Illinois: a flat 4.95% after a small exemption, $4,805.
  • Take-home: $74,375 per year, after $25,625 in total tax. Taylor is in the 22% bracket, but federal income tax is only 13.2% of pay.

Saving $10,000 in a traditional 401(k) drops Taylor's federal tax to $10,970 and Illinois tax to $4,310. Take-home falls by only $7,305, so the $10,000 saved costs $7,305 of spending money. FICA doesn't change, because it's charged on wages before 401(k) contributions.

In retirement mode: a married couple, both over 65, with $60,000 of traditional IRA withdrawals and $48,000 of Social Security pays $5,804 of federal tax, helped by the extra deductions for age 65 and the senior deduction. Illinois taxes none of it, since it exempts retirement income.

Splitting pre-tax deductions by kind

Pre-tax deductions can go in as one figure, or be split by kind with the Split button: 401(k), 403(b) or 457; traditional IRA; HSA; health premiums through work; and other. On a joint return each spouse gets their own lines, and each line can be entered per year, per month or per paycheck. The split matters because the kinds aren't taxed alike:

  • 401(k), 403(b), 457 and other: off income tax, but not FICA.
  • HSA through payroll and health premiums through work: off income tax and FICA too.
  • Traditional IRA: off income tax only as far as it's deductible. With a 401(k) at work, the deduction phases out between $81,000 and $91,000 of income single, or $129,000 and $149,000 joint.
  • States: a few keep some kinds in their own tax. Pennsylvania taxes 401(k) and IRA money, New Jersey taxes IRA, HSA and premium money, California taxes HSA money and Massachusetts IRA money. The state rules table shows your state's row.

Back to Taylor, with the $10,000 401(k) plus $4,400 into an HSA and $2,400 of health premiums, both through payroll. Entered as one $16,800 figure, FICA stays at $7,650. Split by kind, the $6,800 of HSA and premiums comes off FICA as well, which falls to $7,130, and take-home rises by $520. Federal tax is $9,474 and Illinois tax $3,974 either way.

In New Jersey the same split leaves $6,800 more in the state's taxable income, since New Jersey doesn't let HSA money or premiums off its tax. And if Taylor also put $7,500 into a traditional IRA, only $5,850 of it would be deductible. Taylor has a 401(k) at work, and $83,200 of income after the 401(k), HSA and premiums is inside the phase-out.

How to read your results

  • Marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar. Use it to judge a raise, a 401(k) contribution or a Roth conversion.
  • Effective rate is total tax divided by income, always lower. Use it to compare with other years or other people.
  • The bracket bars show how much of your income falls in each layer.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a raise into a higher bracket lowers take-home pay. It never does. Only the dollars above the line pay the higher rate.
  • Forgetting FICA on top of income tax: 6.2% Social Security on wages up to $184,500, plus 1.45% Medicare on all wages.
  • Assuming Social Security is tax-free, or fully taxed. Between none and 85% of it is taxable, depending on other income.
  • Ignoring state rules. Some states tax retirement income heavily, and others exempt it entirely.

Questions

What is the 2026 standard deduction?

$16,100 single and $32,200 married filing jointly. At 65 and over, an extra $2,050 single or $1,650 per spouse, plus the temporary $6,000 senior deduction for 2025 through 2028, which phases out above $75,000 single or $150,000 joint.

What's the difference between my bracket and my tax rate?

Your bracket is the rate on your last dollars. Your effective rate is total tax divided by income, which is always lower because the first dollars are taxed at 10% or not at all.

How much of Social Security is taxable?

None, up to half or up to 85%, depending on your other income. Most states don't tax it at all.

What are the 2026 federal tax brackets?

For single filers: 10% to $12,400, 12% to $50,400, 22% to $105,700, 24% to $201,775, 32% to $256,225, 35% to $640,600, and 37% above. For married couples filing jointly the thresholds are about double: 10% to $24,800, 12% to $100,800, 22% to $211,400, 24% to $403,550, 32% to $512,450, 35% to $768,700.

How are capital gains taxed?

Long-term gains, on investments held over a year, are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, stacked on top of your other income. In 2026 the 0% rate applies while taxable income is under $49,450 single or $98,900 joint. Short-term gains are taxed like wages.

Should I use the standard deduction or itemize?

Itemize only if your mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and charitable gifts add up to more than the standard deduction. Most households now take the standard deduction. Enter an itemized total to compare.
