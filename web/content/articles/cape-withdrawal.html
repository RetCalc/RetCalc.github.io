CAPE-based withdrawal calculator: spend by market valuation

A CAPE-based rule sets each year's withdrawal from how expensive stocks are. Shiller's CAPE is the S&P 500's price over ten years of its earnings, after inflation; its inverse, the earnings yield, has been a rough guide to future returns. The rule takes a base rate plus a share of that yield: more when stocks are cheap, less when they're dear. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for it.

How it works

Each year: withdrawal rate = 1.75% + 0.5 × (100 ÷ CAPE), applied to that year's balance. At a CAPE of 20 that's 4.25%; at 10, 6.75%. Both numbers are settings in the simulator.

What it says today

Shiller's CAPE is 41.0, among the highest readings on record, so the rule starts at 2.97%: $29,695 a year on $1,000,000. That's the rule's way of saying today's prices leave less room than history's average start.

A worked example

Pat retires with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds and plans for 30 years. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • Year one in history ranged from $35,964 (retiring in 1929, with stocks expensive) to $85,159 (retiring in 1982, at a CAPE of 7.4).
  • It never ran out: as a share of each year's balance, it lasted in all 70 historical retirements and left a typical $1,000,813.
  • The typical retiree spent $51,102 a year in today's dollars.
  • The worst case: retiring in 1966, spending fell to $21,502 a year by year 30.

Questions

Where does the formula come from?

Researchers including Michael Kitces and Karsten Jeske (Early Retirement Now) have proposed rules of this shape, a base plus a share of the earnings yield. The 1.75% and 0.5 here are a common starting point; change them to suit.

Is a high CAPE a reason to retire later?

Not by itself. High valuations have tended to mean lower returns over the following decade, but the link is loose. The Safe spending view shows how much each historical start allowed against its CAPE.

Does it use today's CAPE for year one?

Yes: the first year's figure uses today's reading. In the historical test, each start uses the CAPE of its own time.
