Rent vs. buy calculator

Is it better to rent or buy? This calculator runs a fair race: both households spend the same each month, and whichever side's costs are lower invests the difference. It then compares net worth year by year, after selling costs and taxes on the gains.

What it counts

The down payment and closing costs the renter invests instead, mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance and PMI, rent increases, home appreciation, selling costs, capital-gains tax on investments, and the tax-free exclusion on a home sale ($250,000 single, $500,000 married).

A worked example

Casey and Drew can buy a $450,000 home with 20% down at 6.5% for 30 years, or keep renting a similar place. They expect the home to appreciate 4% a year, rent to rise 3.2% a year, and investments to earn 7%.

  • The cost of owning: $3,213 a month in the first year: $2,275 of principal and interest, plus property tax, insurance and 1% a year of upkeep. On day one, the buyer also spends $103,500 on the down payment and closing costs, which the renter invests instead.
  • If rent is $1,800 a month: renting stays ahead the whole time. After 30 years, the renter's investments are worth $1,969,550 against the buyer's $1,347,907 in home equity and savings, a $621,643 lead.
  • If rent is $2,400 a month: buying pulls ahead in year 5 and leads by $273,524 after 30 years.

The same house, the same loan, and opposite answers. What changed is the price-to-rent ratio: the home price divided by a year's rent. At $1,800 a month it's about 21; at $2,400 it's about 16. As a rough guide, below about 15 buying tends to win, and above about 20 renting tends to win, if the renter really invests the difference.

To try it, enter a Home price of $450,000, Down payment of 20%, Interest rate of 6.5% and a Monthly rent of $1,800, then $2,400.

How to read your results

  • The winner is whoever has more net worth at the end of the time horizon, after selling the home and paying tax on gains.
  • The break-even year is when buying first pulls ahead. If you might move before then, renting is likely cheaper.
  • The chart shows both net worths year by year. Early on, buying and selling costs put the buyer behind.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing rent to the mortgage payment alone, leaving out tax, insurance, maintenance and the cost of the down payment.
  • Assuming the renter invests the difference without checking that they would.
  • Expecting high appreciation. Over long periods, U.S. home prices have risen only a little faster than inflation.
  • Forgetting selling costs, often 3% to 6% of the price with agent commissions, and closing costs when buying.
  • Ignoring how long you'll stay. Buying rarely pays off within three to five years.

Questions

How long do you need to stay for buying to pay off?

Often five to seven years or more, since buying and selling costs together run 7% to 10% of the price. The break-even year for your numbers is shown under the chart.

Is renting throwing money away?

No more than mortgage interest, property tax and upkeep are. Renting wins when rents are low relative to prices and the renter actually invests the difference.

What is the price-to-rent ratio?

A home's price divided by a year's rent for a similar place. A $450,000 home that would rent for $2,000 a month has a ratio of about 19. Lower ratios favor buying; higher ratios favor renting.

Is a house a good investment?

It's forced saving and a hedge against rising rents, and the gain on a main home is largely tax-free. As an investment alone, though, it has usually earned less than a stock portfolio, and it costs money every year to own.

Does the calculator include the mortgage interest deduction?

No. Since 2018 the standard deduction is large enough that most homeowners don't itemize, so mortgage interest saves them no tax. If you itemize, buying looks slightly better than shown.
