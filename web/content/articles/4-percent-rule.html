4% rule calculator: does the safe withdrawal rate still work?

The 4% rule says you can take 4% of your savings in your first year of retirement, raise that amount with inflation every year after, and have the money last 30 years. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for exactly that: a fixed withdrawal, rising with inflation, run through every retirement since 1926.

Where the 4% rule comes from

The financial planner William Bengen published it in 1994. He ran a stock and bond portfolio through every starting year he had data for and looked for the highest first-year withdrawal that never ran out. The hardest start was 1966, the beginning of sixteen years of rising inflation and flat stock prices, and it allowed about 4%. In 1998 the Trinity study found much the same: 4% lasted 30 years in about 95% of historical periods with half or more in stocks.

To see both papers set up the way they were, and what the same tests find on today's longer record, use Reproduce a classic study under the strategy picker.

A worked example

Pat retires with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds and takes $40,000 the first year, 4%, raising it with inflation every year after. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • Over 30 years: the money lasted in 66 of the 70 historical retirements, 94.3%. The four that ran out began in 1965, 1966, 1968 and 1969.
  • The safe maximum: the highest first-year rate that lasted 30 years from every start was 3.79%, set by 1966. The typical start could have taken 6.63%.
  • Most retirees end up with more: in 48 of the 70 starts, Pat finished with more than the $1,000,000 started with, after inflation. The middle outcome was about $1.6 million.
  • Longer retirements: over 40 years, 4% lasted 86.7% of the time, and over 50 years, 80.0%. The safe maximums fall to 3.52% and 3.36%.

Why it failed when it did

Every failure began in the late 1960s. Those retirements met high inflation and a flat stock market in their first ten years, when withdrawals were largest against the balance, and never recovered the ground. Some of them ran out even though their 30 years averaged better returns than retirements that lasted, because the bad years came first. The simulator's When you retire chart plots each start's first decade against how it ended, and picking a year in the table says why that start went the way it did.

Is 4% still safe today?

Two things argue for caution. Stock prices are high against earnings: Shiller's CAPE is 41.0 today, higher than at the start of any retirement with 30 years of history after it, and expensive starts have tended to allow less. And the record is one country's century, which turned out well. The simulator's Monte Carlo mode can draw years in blocks and use your own, lower, return assumptions in place of history's, to see how much margin a lower rate buys.

Questions

Is it 4% of the balance every year?

No. It's 4% of the starting balance in year one. After that, the same dollar amount rises with inflation, whatever the portfolio does. Taking 4% of each year's balance is a different strategy, a fixed percentage. It can't run out, but its income swings with markets.

Does the 4% rule include Social Security?

No. The rate applies to the portfolio alone. Social Security and pensions reduce how much you need to withdraw, so add them in the simulator and look at the rate the portfolio actually has to support.

What withdrawal rate for an early retirement?

Over 50 years, the highest first-year rate that lasted from every start with a 60/40 mix was about 3.4%. Many early retirees plan on 3.25% to 3.5%, or pair a higher start with rules for cutting back.

Is the 4% before or after tax?

Before tax. Withdrawals from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s are taxed as income, so what you can spend is less. The Income Tax tool's retirement mode shows how much.

What if I'm willing to cut spending in a bad decade?

Then you can usually start higher. Guardrails start around 5% and cut when markets fall. VPW spends a share that rises with age. The simulator's Compare strategies view puts them side by side.
