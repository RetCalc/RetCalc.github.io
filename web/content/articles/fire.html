FIRE calculator and Coast FIRE calculator

Find the age your investments could pay for your life, the FIRE number, and when you could stop saving altogether and let growth carry you to a normal retirement: Coast FIRE. See it with a steady return, a range, or every real market since 1926.

How it works

Enter your savings, what you add each month, your expected return and the yearly spending you'd need. At a 4% withdrawal rate your FIRE number is 25 times that spending; at 3.5%, about 29 times. The calculator shows when you'd get there, and how much sooner saving a little more gets you there.

FIRE stands for financial independence, retire early. Independence is the part that matters: the point where work becomes optional because your investments could cover your spending. Many people who reach it keep working, just on their own terms.

A worked example

Alex is 30, has $10,000 invested, and spends $40,000 a year. At a 4% withdrawal rate, Alex's FIRE number is $1,000,000 in today's dollars. The calculator's starting numbers assume an 8.5% return and 3% inflation, about 5.3% a year after inflation.

  • Saving $1,000 a month: Alex reaches $1,000,000 in 36.6 years, at 66.6. That's a normal retirement, not an early one.
  • Saving $2,000 a month: 25.4 years, at 55.4. Doubling the savings cuts eleven years off.
  • Saving $2,500 a month: 22.1 years, at 52.1.

The pattern holds for everyone: early on, what you save matters far more than what the market does. Each extra dollar saved also tends to be a dollar not spent, which lowers the FIRE number itself, so the effect of a higher savings rate is even larger than these figures show.

Coast FIRE: at $2,000 a month, Alex could stop saving entirely at 38.9. What's invested by then would grow to $1,000,000 by 65 on its own, so from 38.9 Alex only needs to earn enough to cover current spending.

To try it, set Current age to 30, Current savings to $10,000, Contribution to $2,000 monthly, and Target to $40,000 of annual withdrawal at 4%. Switch to Coast FIRE for the coast age.

How to read your results

  • FIRE age on a steady return is the middle-of-the-road answer. Historical mode shows the range: the same plan reached the target years earlier in good decades and years later in bad ones.
  • The success slider picks how cautious to be: at 90%, the age by which 9 in 10 historical paths had arrived.
  • Contributions against growth: in the first decade, your own savings do most of the work; later, growth takes over.

Common mistakes

  • Using 4% for a retirement that may last 50 years. Many early retirees plan on 3.25% to 3.5%.
  • Forgetting health insurance before Medicare, which can be one of the largest costs of retiring early.
  • Leaving out taxes. Your FIRE number should cover spending plus the tax on withdrawals.
  • Counting home equity. A paid-off home lowers your spending, but it doesn't pay the bills.
  • Ignoring how you'll reach retirement accounts before 59½.

Questions

What is my FIRE number?

Your yearly spending divided by your withdrawal rate. $50,000 a year at 4% is $1.25 million.

What is Coast FIRE?

The point where what you've already saved would grow to your full retirement target by a normal retirement age without another contribution. After it, you only need to earn enough to cover today's spending.

Is 4% safe for early retirement?

It was built for 30-year retirements. For 40 to 50 years, many plan on 3.25% to 3.5%. The Drawdown Simulator tests your exact plan.

What are Lean FIRE and Fat FIRE?

Lean FIRE means reaching independence on a small budget, often under $40,000 a year. Fat FIRE means a larger one, often $100,000 or more. The math is the same; only the spending figure changes.

What savings rate do I need to retire early?

Starting from nothing, with a 5% return after inflation and a 4% withdrawal rate, saving half of take-home pay reaches independence in about 17 years, and saving a quarter in about 32. What you've already saved shortens that. Enter your own numbers to see yours.

Should I count Social Security?

Many early retirees leave it out of the FIRE number as a safety margin, then treat it as a raise at 62 to 70. If you count it, the portfolio only has to cover spending until benefits start plus the part they don't cover.
