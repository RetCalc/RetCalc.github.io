Vanguard dynamic spending calculator: a ceiling and a floor on each year's change

Vanguard's dynamic spending rule sits between the 4% rule's fixed amount and taking a fixed percentage of the portfolio. Each year you aim at a share of what the portfolio is worth, but spending can't rise more than 5% or fall more than 2.5% from last year's, after inflation. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for the rule and runs it through every retirement since 1926.

How the rule works

  • The target: a share of this year's balance, 5% in the example below.
  • The ceiling: if the target is more than 5% above last year's spending, after inflation, you take last year's plus 5%.
  • The floor: if it's more than 2.5% below, you take last year's less 2.5%.

The ceiling keeps a few good years from setting a spending level a bad decade can't support; the floor keeps a crash from cutting your income in one step. Both limits are settings in the simulator, under Advanced.

A worked example

Pat retires with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds, targets 5% ($50,000 the first year) and plans for 30 years. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • It always lasted: the money lasted 30 years in all 70 historical retirements.
  • Spending moved slowly: no year rose more than 5% or fell more than 2.5%, against swings of 27% up and 28% down for a straight 5% of the portfolio.
  • The typical path: the median spending across starts went from $50,000 in year one to $53,368 in year ten and $55,455 in year thirty, in today's dollars.
  • The worst case: retiring in 1966, spending slid 2.5% almost every year, to $23,994 by year 30. A straight 5% of the portfolio fell further, to $17,674, but much sooner.

The catch

Because a cut can't be bigger than 2.5% a year, spending can lag a falling market for a long time. Over 30 years at a 60/40 mix that didn't matter, but over 35 years it ran out in 4 of 65 starts, something a straight percentage of the portfolio can never do. Questions

Is the 5% ceiling and 2.5% floor after inflation?

Yes. Each year's limit applies to spending in today's dollars, so a 2.5% cut is 2.5% less purchasing power than last year.

What target rate should I use?

The target is the share of the portfolio you'd take with no limits. 4% to 5% is common; a higher target spends more early but leaves the floor more work to do in a bad decade. Compare a few on the Compare strategies view.

How is this different from floor and ceiling?

It's the same rule with Vanguard's limits. The simulator's floor and ceiling strategy lets you set any limits, 10% each way by default.
