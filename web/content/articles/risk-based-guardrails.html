Risk-based guardrails calculator: spending set by the odds of success

Classic guardrails cut or raise spending when the withdrawal rate drifts too far. Risk-based guardrails, an approach many financial planners now use, watch something more direct: the chance, judged from history, that the plan lasts. Spending holds steady while that chance stays in a comfortable band and is reset only when it leaves. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for them.

How they work

  • Start: spend the amount that history gives a 90% chance of lasting the years ahead, counting Social Security and other income still to come.
  • Hold: keep that spending, raised with inflation, while the chance stays between 70% and 99%.
  • Reset: if markets push the chance below 70%, cut spending back to where it's 90% again; if they push it above 99%, raise it to 90%.

The chance is read from every historical start at your stock mix and fees, for however many years are left. The three numbers are settings in the simulator.

A worked example

Pat retires with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds and plans for 30 years. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • Year one: $42,781, a 4.28% rate, the amount with a 90% historical chance of lasting 30 years.
  • Few cuts: the typical start's spending changed 10 times in 30 years, mostly raises, with 2 cuts; 7 of the 70 starts never had a cut.
  • When cuts came, they were big: the largest single cut was 40%. Retiring in 1969, spending fell to $23,777 a year by year 8.
  • Generous over a lifetime: the middle start's typical year was $69,117 in today's dollars, and like VPW it spends the portfolio down by the final year.

Against classic guardrails

Fewer, larger adjustments. Guyton-Klinger guardrails move spending 10% at a time and often; risk-based ones leave it alone through ordinary swings and reset in one step when the odds really change. Whether that suits you depends on whether you'd rather take small cuts often or a bigger one rarely.

Questions

Why does it spend everything by the end?

With a year left, any balance has every chance of lasting it, so the rule raises spending toward the end. Set a Legacy goal or a Maximum spending if you want something left.

Is the probability from Monte Carlo?

Here it's from history: the share of past starts at your mix in which a given withdrawal would have lasted the years left. Planners' software often uses Monte Carlo for the same idea.

What target should I use?

90% with a 70%–99% band is a common setting. A lower target starts higher and resets more often; a narrower band means more frequent, smaller changes.
