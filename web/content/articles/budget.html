Budget calculator

Two tabs. Budget is what you spend, line by line: monthly and yearly bills side by side, grouped, with an emergency fund target sized from your spending. Paycheck starts from your gross pay, takes out taxes and what your employer withholds, covers this budget, and helps you decide where the rest goes.

How to use it

On the Budget tab, work down the categories with a few months of statements, entering annual bills as yearly amounts. Then open the Paycheck tab and enter your gross pay, how often you're paid and your state. Rank where you'd put what's left (the 401(k) match, an HSA, an emergency fund, a Roth IRA and so on) and let Fill in this order do the math, or type your own amounts.

A worked example

Riley's paycheck deposits $6,000 a month after taxes and a 401(k) contribution.

  • The 50/30/20 guide would put $3,000 toward needs (housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments), $1,800 toward wants (dining out, travel, subscriptions), and $1,200 toward saving and extra debt payments.
  • Riley's budget comes to $4,000 a month once yearly bills are spread out: car insurance, holiday gifts and a vacation add more than most people expect.
  • On the Paycheck tab, that leaves $2,000 a month to assign. Pre-tax lines like the 401(k) and HSA cost the deposit less than they put away, because part of each dollar would have gone to tax.
  • The emergency fund: six months of spending is $24,000. It's sized from the $4,000 Riley spends, not the $6,000 Riley takes home, since that's what it would need to cover.

The most useful number is usually the gap between what you think you spend and what the statements say. Irregular bills entered as yearly amounts are where most budgets leak.

How to read your results

  • Your budget, per month is every line, with yearly bills spread over the year. Where it goes splits it by group.
  • Emergency fund is your monthly spending times the months you choose. Goals you set money aside for, like college savings, aren't in it.
  • On the Paycheck tab, Still unassigned is what's left of each check after taxes, the budget and your saving. Below zero means more is planned than the paycheck covers.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting annual and irregular costs: insurance premiums, car repairs, gifts, subscriptions billed yearly.
  • Counting health insurance twice: if it comes out of your paycheck, the Paycheck tab takes it out before tax instead of as a bill.
  • Treating a pre-tax 401(k) dollar as a full dollar of take-home pay. It costs less, by your tax rate.
  • Making it so strict you abandon it. A line for fun money keeps a budget realistic.

Questions

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

About half of take-home pay on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on saving and paying down debt. It's a rough guide, not a rule.

How big should an emergency fund be?

Three to six months of spending is typical; more if your income is irregular or you're the only earner.

How much of my income should I save?

For retirement, 15% of gross pay, including any employer match, is a common target for someone starting in their 20s or 30s. Starting later, or aiming to retire early, calls for more.

How much should I spend on housing?

A common guideline is under 28% of gross income for rent or a mortgage payment with tax and insurance, which is often 30% to 35% of take-home pay.

Where should my emergency fund be kept?

Somewhere safe and quick to reach, like a high-yield savings or money market account. Not in stocks, which can be down just when you need the money.
