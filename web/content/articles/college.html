College savings calculator

Find how much to save each month for college, from a public in-state school to a private or elite one. Costs grow with tuition inflation to the year your child starts, and savings keep earning while earlier years are paid.

How to use it

Pick a school type or enter a yearly cost, set the years until college, and add what you've already saved. The monthly figure is what covers the full cost; many families aim for part of it and cover the rest with income, aid and scholarships.

A worked example

Jamie's parents start saving at birth for four years at a public in-state school that costs $27,000 a year today, including room and board. They expect costs to rise 4% a year and their savings to earn 6%. These are the calculator's starting numbers.

  • The cost when Jamie starts: 18 years of 4% increases make the first year $54,697, about double today's price.
  • What they need to save: $558 a month from birth covers all four years. The money keeps earning during college, so the account only needs to hold $212,674 on the first day, not the full cost of all four years.
  • Starting later: with 10 years to go instead of 18, it's $956 a month for the same school.
  • A private non-profit college at $59,000 a year today, the calculator's second choice, would take $1,220 a month from birth.

Many families don't aim for 100%. Covering half or two-thirds with savings, and the rest from income during the college years, aid and scholarships, is a common and reasonable plan. Saving something early does more than saving more later.

Where to save: the 529 plan

  • Tax-free growth when the money is spent on qualified education: tuition, fees, books, and room and board for students enrolled at least half time.
  • State tax breaks: many states deduct contributions to their own plan from state income tax.
  • Financial aid: a parent-owned 529 counts as a parental asset, reducing aid by at most 5.64% of its value.
  • If there's money left over: change the beneficiary to a sibling or yourself, or roll up to $35,000 over a lifetime into the student's Roth IRA once the account is 15 years old, subject to the yearly Roth limit.

Common mistakes

  • Using today's cost without inflation. College prices have long risen faster than general prices.
  • Saving for college instead of retirement. Students can borrow for college; you can't borrow for retirement.
  • Holding all stocks right up to the first tuition bill. Shift toward safer investments as college gets close.
  • Forgetting that sticker price isn't what most families pay after grants and scholarships.

Questions

What is a 529 plan?

A college savings account whose investments grow tax-free when spent on qualified education. Many states add a tax deduction for contributions.

How fast does college cost rise?

Tuition has long outpaced general inflation; 4% to 5% a year is a common planning assumption.

How much should I save for college each month?

For a public in-state school at today's average price of about $27,000 a year, saving about $560 a month from birth covers four years, at 6% returns and 4% cost growth. Starting at 8 years old, it's closer to $960.

Should I save for college or retirement first?

Retirement, at least up to any employer match. There are loans, grants and scholarships for college but none for retirement, and retirement savings generally don't count against financial aid.

What if my child doesn't go to college?

You can change the 529's beneficiary to another family member, use it for trade school or apprenticeship programs that qualify, or roll part of it into the child's Roth IRA. Otherwise, earnings withdrawn for other uses are taxed plus a 10% penalty; your contributions come back tax-free.
