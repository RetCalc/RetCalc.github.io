VPW calculator: variable percentage withdrawal, tested since 1926

Variable percentage withdrawal (VPW) is a way of spending a portfolio that can't run out early and is meant to use it all by the end. Each year you take a percentage of what's left, and the percentage rises as the years left shrink. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for VPW and runs it through every retirement since 1926.

How VPW works

VPW comes from the Bogleheads investing community. Each year's payment is what an annuity would pay on the current balance over the years remaining, at the return the portfolio's mix is expected to earn after inflation: 5% for stocks and 1.9% for bonds in the Bogleheads' table, about 3.8% for a 60/40 mix. Because it's always a share of what's there, the money can't run out before the last year; because the share rises, it is spent down by then.

A worked example

Pat retires at 65 with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds and plans to spend it by 100, a 35-year horizon. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • Year one: 5.02%, or $50,223. The rate rises with age: 6.04% at 75 and 8.54% at 85.
  • It never ran out: in all 65 historical retirements, spending lasted to 100 and the portfolio was spent by then, as designed.
  • The typical retiree spent more: the middle start's typical year was $64,648 in today's dollars.
  • The worst case: retiring in 1966, spending fell to $22,209 a year at 81, 44% of year one, before recovering. In 28 of the 65 starts it fell more than a quarter below year one at some point.

VPW against the 4% rule

The 4% rule holds spending steady and accepts a small chance of running out, and usually leaves a large balance unspent. VPW never runs out and leaves nothing, but its income moves with markets. A Minimum spending in the simulator shows what happens if you hold a floor under it, and Social Security added as income usually covers much of the swing.

Questions

How is the VPW percentage calculated?

It's the payment on an annuity due: the balance times r ÷ ((1 − (1 + r)−n) × (1 + r)), where n is the years left and r the mix's expected return after inflation. With r = 3.8% and 35 years left, that's 5.02%.

Can VPW run out of money?

Not before its final year: each payment is a share of what's left. What can happen is a long stretch of low payments after a bad start, which is the number to look at.

Why plan to 100?

The Bogleheads' table runs to 100 so the payments stay sustainable for anyone who lives that long. Planning to a younger age raises the early payments but spends the money sooner.

What if I want something left at the end?

Set a final value under VPW's settings. The payment is worked out to leave that much, in today's dollars, after the last year.
