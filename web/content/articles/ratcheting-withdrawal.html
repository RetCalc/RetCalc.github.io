Ratcheting withdrawal calculator: the 4% rule with raises

The 4% rule sizes spending for the worst decade in history, so most retirees following it end up with far more than they started with. Michael Kitces proposed a fix in 2015: start at 4%, and give yourself a permanent 10% raise whenever the portfolio grows to 50% above where it began, at most once every three years. Spending never goes down. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for the ratchet.

How it works

  • Start: 4% of the portfolio, raised with inflation each year, exactly like the 4% rule.
  • Ratchet: when the portfolio, after inflation, is 50% above its starting value, raise spending 10% for good.
  • Wait: at least three years between raises.

A worked example

Pat retires with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds, takes $40,000 the first year and plans for 30 years. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • No extra risk: it lasted in 66 of 70 starts, 94.3%, the same as the plain 4% rule. The four failures began in 1965, 1966, 1968 and 1969, and none of them ever got a raise.
  • Raises for most: 44 of the 70 starts got at least one; the first typically came in year 9.
  • The typical retiree was spending $64,420 a year in today's dollars by year 30. Retiring in 1982, raises came every three years from year 5, reaching $94,318.
  • Still a cushion: the middle start ended with $1,428,402, against $1,616,418 without the ratchet.

Why it doesn't add risk

The raises come only after the portfolio has grown well beyond what the 4% rule needs, and the starts that test the rule, like 1966, never grow that much early. You can set the threshold, the size of each raise and the wait between them under Advanced.

Questions

Does spending ever go down?

No. Like the 4% rule, it never cuts; it only adds raises. That's why its failures are the same as the 4% rule's.

Can I ratchet more aggressively?

Yes: a lower threshold or bigger raises spend more, at some risk. The Safe spending view shows how the highest safe starting rate changes.

How does it compare with guardrails?

Guardrails start higher and cut when markets fall; the ratchet starts at 4% and only ever raises. See guardrails and the 4% rule.
