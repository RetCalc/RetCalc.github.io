Debt payoff calculator: avalanche vs. snowball

List your debts and see your debt-free date, the total interest you'll pay, and how much a payoff plan saves over paying only the minimums. Compare the avalanche and snowball methods side by side, month by month.

Avalanche or snowball?

Both put every spare dollar on one debt at a time and roll each paid-off minimum into the next. Avalanche targets the highest interest rate first and costs the least. Snowball clears the smallest balance first, for quicker wins that help many people stick with it.

The rollover is what makes either one work. When a debt is paid off, its minimum payment doesn't go back into your budget; it joins the extra payment on the next debt, so the amount aimed at each debt keeps growing.

A worked example

Jordan owes $49,400 across four debts, the calculator's starting list: a $9,800 credit card at 22.9%, a $1,900 store card at 8.9%, a $16,200 car loan at 7.4% and a $21,500 student loan at 5.5%. The minimum payments add up to $930 a month.

  • Minimums only: 123 months, over ten years, and $18,321 of interest.
  • Avalanche with $300 a month extra: the credit card goes first, and Jordan is debt-free in 47 months, just under four years, paying $8,155 of interest. That's $10,167 less than minimums alone.
  • Snowball with $300 extra: the store card goes first and is gone within months. Debt-free in 48 months with $8,578 of interest: one month longer and $424 more than avalanche.
  • $400 extra instead of $300: avalanche finishes in 43 months with $7,226 of interest.

Here the two methods end up close, because the smallest debt is small. The gap grows when the smallest balance also has the lowest rate, and a large card at a high rate waits its turn. Either way, the extra payment matters far more than the order.

How to read your results

  • Debt-free date and total interest for each method, next to minimums only.
  • The payoff order shows which debt each method targets, and the month each one is cleared.
  • The chart shows the total balance falling; each bend is a debt cleared and its minimum rolled over.
  • The next $100: the calculator also shows how much sooner an extra $100 a month would get you there.

Common mistakes

  • Spending a paid-off debt's minimum instead of rolling it into the next one.
  • Adding new charges to a card you're paying off.
  • Emptying savings to pay debt. Keep a small emergency fund so a surprise doesn't go back on a card.
  • Stopping 401(k) contributions and losing an employer match, which is worth more than paying off most debts.
  • Ignoring a 0% promotional rate's end date, when the full rate applies.

Questions

How much faster does an extra payment get me out of debt?

Often by years. Enter an amount in Extra payment and the tool shows the new date and the interest it saves.

Should I pay off debt or invest?

Debt above about 7% to 8% interest, like most credit cards, is usually worth paying off first; it's a guaranteed return at that rate. Always keep getting any employer 401(k) match.

Is the debt avalanche or snowball better?

Avalanche always costs the same or less in interest. Snowball can be worth the small extra cost if early wins keep you going. The best method is the one you'll stick with.

Should I consolidate my debt?

A consolidation loan or 0% balance transfer helps if it cuts your rate and you stop adding new debt. Count the fees, usually 3% to 5% for a balance transfer, and check what rate applies when a promotion ends.

Does paying off debt improve my credit score?

Usually. Lower credit card balances relative to your limits help most. Closing a paid-off card can lower your score slightly by reducing your available credit.
