Retirement savings calculator with taxes, fees and market history

Project a retirement portfolio the way a planner would: contributions that rise with your pay, a return before inflation, fees, a glide path toward bonds, and the tax you'll owe when you withdraw. Then see the same plan through every market since 1926, or 5,000 random ones.

What you can model

  • Account types: split savings into traditional 401(k)/IRA, Roth and taxable brokerage, with your employer match, and see the 2026 federal and state tax on what you'd withdraw.
  • Fees: an expense ratio of 1% can cost a fifth of a portfolio over a career. The tool shows the dollar cost.
  • Work backwards: set a target income or balance and it solves for the contribution, or the number of years, that reaches it.
  • Coast FIRE: finds the point where you could stop contributing and still reach the target.
  • A glide path: shift from stocks toward bonds over the last years before retirement, as target-date funds do.

A worked example: what a 1% fee costs

Jesse starts with $10,000, saves $500 a month, raises that 3% a year, and invests for 35 years at 8.5% a year before fees, with 3% inflation.

  • With no fees: $648,142 in today's dollars.
  • With a 1% annual fee, typical of an advisor or an actively managed fund: $522,585.
  • The difference: $125,557, or 19% of what Jesse would otherwise have. The fee is charged on the whole balance every year, so its cost compounds just like the returns do.

A broad index fund charging 0.05% would cost a small fraction of that. To try it, set Starting value to $10,000, Contribution each period to $500 monthly, Contribution growth to 3%, Time period to 35 years and Rate of return to 8.5%, then change Fees from 0 to 1.

Steady returns, history, or random markets

  • Steady grows your savings at one fixed return: the clearest way to compare plans.
  • Historical runs your plan through every real stretch of markets since 1926, month by month, and shows the range of outcomes, with each starting year as a faint line.
  • Monte Carlo draws thousands of random return sequences with the volatility you set, including ones history never produced.

How to read your results

  • Nominal and today's dollars: the nominal figure is the number on your future statement; today's dollars is what it will buy.
  • After-tax income estimates what you'd keep from withdrawals, given how your savings are split between account types.
  • Percentiles in the historical and random modes show a bad case (10th), a typical case (50th) and a good case (90th).

Common mistakes

  • Entering a return after inflation in the nominal field, which counts inflation twice.
  • Ignoring fees because they look small as a percentage.
  • Treating a traditional 401(k) balance as all yours. Part of it is future income tax.
  • Planning on the median outcome with no margin for a bad decade.

Questions

Why show "inflation adjusted" figures?

A million dollars in 30 years buys what about $410,000 buys today at 3% inflation. Today's-dollar figures let you compare the result with prices you know.

What's the difference between historical and Monte Carlo?

Historical runs your plan through every real stretch of markets since 1926, month by month. Monte Carlo draws thousands of random return sequences with the volatility you set, including ones history never produced.

What rate of return should I use?

The calculator starts at 8.5% before inflation, a little under the 8.7% a year that 60% stocks and 40% bonds earned from 1926 through 2025. All stocks earned 10.4%. Use less for a more conservative mix, and remember that history is a guide, not a promise.

Should I use a traditional or Roth 401(k)?

Traditional saves tax now and is taxed later; Roth is taxed now and tax-free later. Traditional usually wins if your tax rate will be lower in retirement, Roth if it will be the same or higher. Many people split contributions between the two.

What is a glide path?

A gradual shift from stocks toward bonds as retirement approaches, so a crash just before you retire does less damage. Target-date funds follow one automatically.
