RMD withdrawal strategy: spending by the IRS life expectancy table

Required minimum distributions force you to take a share of a traditional IRA each year: the balance divided by a life-expectancy divisor from the IRS. Some retirees use the same math as their spending rule for the whole portfolio. It adjusts to markets, can't run out, and spends a little more each year as the horizon shortens. This page opens the Drawdown Simulator set up for it.

How it works

Each year, divide the portfolio by the divisor for your age from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table: 27.4 at 72, 20.2 at 80, 12.2 at 90. That's 3.65%, 4.95% and 8.20% of the balance. The table starts at 72, so before then the simulator extends it by 0.9 per year, which gives 2.97% at 65.

For the required distributions themselves, and what converting to Roth first would save, see the RMD calculator.

A worked example

Pat retires at 65 with $1,000,000 in a 60/40 mix of US stocks and bonds and plans for 30 years. These are the numbers this page opens with.

  • Year one: $29,674, less than the 4% rule's $40,000.
  • Spending rises with age: the median spending across starts was $49,306 in year ten, $78,848 in year twenty and $104,354 in year thirty, in today's dollars.
  • It never ran out in any of the 70 historical retirements, and left a typical $935,660.
  • The worst case: retiring in 1973, spending fell to $18,881 in year 3 before climbing.

Who it suits

Retirees who want spending to follow markets, are comfortable starting low, and expect to spend more later, or who'd rather leave something behind. It spends less early than most strategies, which is when many retirees most want to spend.

Questions

Is this the same as my required distributions?

The math is the same, but this applies it to the whole portfolio as a spending rule. Your actual RMDs apply only to traditional accounts and start at 73 (75 for people born in 1960 or later).

Can the RMD method run out?

No. It always takes a share of what's left, so the balance never reaches zero, though spending falls when markets do.

Why does spending rise so much late in retirement?

The divisor shrinks every year, so the share taken grows: about 3% at 65, 5% at 80, 8% at 90. If the portfolio holds its value, spending climbs with it.
