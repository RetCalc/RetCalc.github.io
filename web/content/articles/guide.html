Retirement readiness check: are you on track?

A guided check-up, one question at a time, that teaches as it goes and turns your answers into your number, a retirement readiness score out of 100 and a plan you can adjust. It covers cash flow, emergency savings, debt, big goals like a home or college, saving that changes over time, and retirement itself.

What you get

  • Your number: what you're on course for at retirement against what a plan like yours needs, from the sixth card on.
  • A short lesson on every card, in your own figures, with the glossary term and the tool that goes deeper.
  • A score built from your retirement outlook, savings rate, emergency fund, debt and monthly cash flow.
  • Your plan tested against every retirement since 1926, account by account, with Social Security estimated for you and each year's income tax and health insurance worked out.
  • Specific changes that would put you on track, such as saving more, retiring later or spending less, sized for you.
  • Short trips into the other tools, with your numbers filled in.

Choose a pace. The Quick check is twelve short cards in about ten minutes, with defaults standing in for the details, each marked. The Full walkthrough adds the deeper cards and trips into the tools that find the numbers you don't know: an hour or more, best with your statements open. You can switch at any time without losing an answer; it saves as you go, and nothing leaves your browser.

How the score works

The score weighs five areas. Each earns full marks at a clear, common-sense target, and partial marks on the way there:

  • Retirement outlook, 40 points: how often your plan's money lasted through retirement in the historical record, with Social Security included. Full marks when it lasted in 95% of retirements.
  • Savings rate, 20 points: full marks at 15% of gross income, counting any employer match. If your plan already has enough, you get full marks at a lower rate, so choosing to coast isn't penalized. Missing part of an employer match costs a quarter of this area.
  • Emergency fund, 15 points: full marks at six months of spending in cash.
  • Debt, 15 points: full marks with nothing owed besides a mortgage. Debt at 8% or more counts far more heavily than low-rate loans.
  • Monthly cash flow, 10 points: full marks when you spend no more than 80% of take-home pay.

Scores of 85 and up read as On track, 70 to 84 Nearly there, 50 to 69 Getting there, 30 to 49 Needs work, and below 30 Needs attention. You get a score as soon as two areas are answered, and it sharpens as you go.

Each area shows the points it still has to give and the change that earns them: for the emergency fund, "3 months earns 11; 6 months earns 15". A line under the score says how many of the answers behind it are your own, and how many are estimates or defaults standing in until you replace them.

An answer the score can't use, like spending of $0 or a retirement age before your age today, keeps its area in the score at zero until it's fixed, so a slip never raises the score. And an area is listed as going well only when none of your next moves is working on it.

Common mistakes

  • Guessing spending instead of checking statements. It's the number the whole plan rests on.
  • Leaving out an employer match, which counts toward your savings rate.
  • Treating a low score as a verdict. It's a map of which area to work on first.

Questions

What is a good retirement readiness score?

85 or above means you're on track. Most people starting out land somewhere in the middle, and the guide shows which change would raise the score most.

Does it estimate Social Security?

Yes, from your income and age using the 2026 benefit formula, or you can enter the figure from your Social Security statement for a more exact answer.

Does it include taxes?

Yes. The spending you enter is what you live on after tax, and every year of the plan pays the 2026 federal and state income tax on where its money came from, plus any Medicare surcharge and, before 65, a marketplace health plan less its subsidy.

Can I stop and come back later?

Yes. Your answers are saved in your browser as you go, and the guide picks up where you left off.

Is it financial advice?

No. It's an educational tool that shows where you stand by common rules of thumb and historical markets. For decisions with tax or legal consequences, talk to a professional.
