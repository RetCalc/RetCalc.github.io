An emergency fund is cash set aside for the bills you can't see coming: a lost job, a transmission, a trip to the emergency room. It earns little, and that's fine, because its job is to protect everything else. Without one, an ordinary bad month lands on a credit card or comes out of a retirement account, and either can set a plan back by years.

What it protects

Emergencies are certain over a working life even if each one is a surprise. Cars break, roofs leak, employers lay people off, and health insurance has deductibles. The question is only what pays for them.

Without cash on hand, the usual answers are expensive. A credit card balance at 24% interest doubles in about three years if it isn't paid down. Taking money from a 401(k) or IRA before 59½ usually means income tax plus a 10% additional tax, and the money stops compounding for good. A 401(k) loan avoids the tax, but if you leave the job, the balance can become due quickly, and an unpaid loan is treated as a withdrawal. Each of these turns a one-time cost into a lasting one.

Many households have no cushion at all. In the Federal Reserve's survey of household finances for 2025, 63% of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense entirely with cash or its equivalent. That share was unchanged from the previous three years and down from 68% in 2021. The other 37% would borrow, sell something, or not be able to pay.

How many months, and for whom

An emergency fund is measured in months of spending, not months of income. In a job loss, what you need to cover is your bills, and the taxes and retirement contributions that came out of your paycheck stop along with it. Someone who takes home $6,000 per month but spends $4,000 needs $4,000 per month of cushion, not $6,000.

Three to six months of spending is the usual range. Where you belong in it depends mostly on how likely you are to lose your income and how long it might take to replace.

How long job searches last supports the range. In 2025 the typical unemployed person in the US had been out of work about ten weeks, so three months covers a normal spell. Recessions are different: in June 2010, after the financial crisis, the median spell had stretched to about 25 weeks, and nearly half of unemployed people had been looking for more than six months. Regular state unemployment benefits help, but they usually last at most 26 weeks, fewer in some states, and replace only part of your pay.

Two refinements make the figure more accurate. First, count what you'd actually spend in a lean stretch. Subscriptions, travel and eating out can be cut quickly; rent, insurance and loan payments can't. Second, add costs that only appear in a job loss. The biggest is usually health insurance: continuing your employer's plan through COBRA means paying the whole premium yourself, and a marketplace plan may be cheaper depending on your income for the year.

Where it fits in the order

An emergency fund competes with other good uses of money: the employer match, paying off debt, retirement saving. A common order that balances them:

  • A starter fund of about one month of spending, or $1,000 to $2,000 if that's out of reach, so a small surprise doesn't go on a card.
  • Enough in your workplace plan to get the full employer match, since that's an immediate return you can't make up later.
  • Paying off high-interest debt, such as credit cards.
  • The rest of the emergency fund, up to three to six months.
  • More retirement saving, and other goals.

Where each dollar should go covers the full order and when to break it. One case for building the fund early even with debt: someone whose income is unstable, since without cash they'll likely borrow again at the next surprise.

Building it up

Six months of spending is a large sum, and it rarely appears at once. At $4,000 per month of spending, the full $24,000 takes two years at $1,000 per month, or four years at $500. A few habits shorten that. Set up an automatic transfer on payday, so the money moves before it can be spent. Send windfalls straight to the fund: a tax refund, a bonus, the proceeds from selling something. And when a regular bill ends, such as a car loan, keep paying the same amount into savings for a while.

It helps to keep the fund at a different bank from your checking account. A day's delay is no problem in a real emergency, and it makes the money a little harder to dip into for things that aren't.

Where to keep it

The money needs to be safe, quick to reach, and earning something. Safe means it won't be down when you need it, which rules out stocks: US stocks fell by about half more than once in the last 25 years, and those falls tend to come with recessions and layoffs, exactly when an emergency fund gets used. Quick means days, not weeks.

Most people keep the first month or two in a high-yield savings account linked to their checking, where a transfer takes a day, and the rest wherever earns a bit more: a money market fund, a ladder of Treasury bills that mature every few weeks, or I bonds once their first 12 months have passed. FDIC insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, so very large balances may need more than one bank.

Expect the fund to roughly hold its value after inflation, not grow. One-month Treasury bills earned 3.29% per year from 1927 through 2025, about 0.30% after inflation, and less than inflation in long stretches such as 2009 to 2021. That's the price of having money that's always there.

Backstops, not substitutes

A few other sources can help in a pinch, but each has a catch that makes it a poor main fund.

  • Roth IRA contributions (not the earnings on them) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty. The catch is that you can't put them back beyond the yearly limit, so the money loses its tax-free growth for good.
  • Since 2024, retirement plans and IRAs can allow one emergency withdrawal of up to $1,000 per year without the 10% additional tax. It's still taxed as income, and you can't take another for three years unless you repay it or put in at least as much again.
  • A home equity line of credit can cover a large repair, but lenders can freeze or cut a line in a downturn, the moment you might need it.
  • A credit card works for a week or two if you can pay it off in full. Beyond that, the interest outruns almost anything your savings earn.

When to use it, and refilling

The fund is for costs that are necessary, unexpected and urgent: losing income, a medical bill, a car or home repair you can't put off. Predictable irregular costs are a different thing. Car insurance billed every six months, holiday gifts, a vacation or the next car are known in advance, and they belong in your budget as monthly amounts set aside for them. Paying them from the emergency fund leaves it empty when the real emergency comes.

Once you've used it, refilling it comes before raising other saving. Keep contributing enough for the employer match, and point the rest of what you'd save at the fund until it's back to its target.

In retirement: the cash buffer

Retirees don't have a paycheck to lose, but they have a different emergency: a market fall in the years they're selling investments to live on. Selling stocks after a large drop locks in the loss and leaves less to recover. That's the risk covered in Sequence-of-returns risk.

A common answer is a cash buffer: one to two years of the spending your savings must cover, held in cash or short-term bonds. In a bad year you spend from the buffer and leave stocks alone; in good years you refill it. If Social Security covers $40,000 of $70,000 in spending, savings must supply $30,000, so a one-to-two-year buffer is $30,000 to $60,000. The cost is that the buffer earns less than stocks over time, so more than a few years of it tends to cost more than it protects.

Retirees still have ordinary emergencies too, and some get larger: a roof, a car, dental work Medicare doesn't cover, help for a family member. Many keep a few months of spending in cash on top of the buffer for those.

Seeing it in your own plan

The Budget tool totals what you spend, with yearly bills spread across the months, and sizes an emergency fund from it: your monthly spending times the number of months you choose. Goals you set money aside for, like college savings, aren't included, since the fund only has to cover what you'd keep spending. Its worked example is Riley, who takes home $6,000 per month and spends $4,000, so six months is $24,000.

In the Retirement Readiness Guide, the emergency fund is one of the five areas of your readiness score, worth 15 points. It gets full marks at six months of spending in cash, and partial marks on the way: three months earns 11.

Common questions

Should I invest my emergency fund to earn more?

Not in stocks. The extra return isn't worth the chance that the fund is down 30% or more in the same recession that costs you your job. Treasury bills, a money market fund or I bonds can earn more than a savings account while staying safe.

Isn't a credit card my emergency fund?

A card can carry you for a few weeks if you pay it off in full. As a plan for a job loss it fails: the interest compounds against you, and issuers can lower credit limits when the economy turns.

Should I build the fund or pay off my credit card first?

Usually a small starter fund first, then the card, then the rest of the fund. Without a starter fund, the next surprise goes straight back onto the card you're paying off.

Does my emergency fund count toward my savings rate?

Not for retirement purposes. The savings rate measures what's invested for the long term. The fund is a cushion you hope not to touch, and while you're building it, it competes with retirement saving for the same dollars.

In short

  • An emergency fund keeps an ordinary bad month off credit cards and out of retirement accounts.
  • Size it in months of spending: about three with two steady incomes, six with one, more with irregular pay.
  • Keep it safe and reachable: high-yield savings, money market funds, Treasury bills, and I bonds after their first year. Not stocks.
  • Use it for the unexpected, keep predictable costs in the budget, and refill it after.
  • In retirement it becomes a buffer of one to two years of withdrawals, so you don't have to sell after a market fall.
