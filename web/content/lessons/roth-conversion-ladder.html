A Roth conversion ladder turns pre-tax retirement money into money you can spend before 59½ without the 10% penalty. Each year you convert part of a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA and pay the income tax; five years later, that conversion can come out penalty-free. Done in the low-income years of an early retirement, the tax can be small. The catch is the five-year wait, and the income each conversion adds.

How a ladder works

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA (or from a 401(k), usually after you've left the job) into a Roth IRA. The amount converted counts as ordinary income that year, taxed at your normal rates, but the conversion itself carries no 10% penalty at any age. From then on, the money grows tax-free.

The rule that makes a ladder possible is in IRS Publication 590-B: each conversion has its own five-year period. If you withdraw a converted amount before its five years are up, and you're under 59½, the 10% penalty applies to the part of the conversion that was taxed. Once the five years have passed, you can withdraw that conversion with no tax and no penalty, at any age.

Each conversion's five years start on January 1 of the year you convert, whatever the date of the conversion itself. So the clock runs like this:

A conversion late in the year waits a little over four years in practice; one in January waits the full five. Converting each year, after the first five you have a conversion maturing every January, the rungs of the ladder.

The order money leaves a Roth IRA

You don't pick which dollars a Roth withdrawal comes from. The IRS ordering rules decide it, counting all your Roth IRAs together:

  • First, your regular contributions, at any age, with no tax and no penalty.
  • Next, conversions, oldest first, the taxed part of each before any untaxed part.
  • Last, earnings.

Because the oldest conversions come out first, and those are the ones whose five years have passed, spending the rungs in order works naturally. Earnings come out last, and before 59½ they're taxed and penalized, so a ladder leaves them in place. If $60,000 converted in 2027 has grown to $75,000 by 2032, the $60,000 is available and the $15,000 of growth waits until 59½.

Earnings have their own clock too. To come out tax-free they need you to be 59½ and five years past your first Roth contribution or conversion of any kind. For someone who starts a ladder at 50, that second condition is long met by 59½.

The five-year runway

The first conversion can't be spent for five years, so a ladder needs five years of spending from somewhere else: cash, a brokerage account, Roth contributions, or 72(t) payments from part of an IRA. That's the main thing that decides whether a ladder is open to you.

Here's a hypothetical couple. Ines and Paul retire at the start of 2027, the year they turn 50. They spend $60,000 per year, with $320,000 in a brokerage account and cash, about $40,000 of Roth contributions, and $1,000,000 in traditional IRAs. They live on the brokerage account and cash from 2027 to 2031. Each of those years they convert $60,000, and the conversion from 2027 is ready on January 1, 2032, the year they turn 55. From then until 59½, each year's spending comes from the conversion made five years earlier.

The rungs pay for spending, not for the conversions' own tax. The tax on each conversion is due in the year of the conversion, so it has to come from the runway money too. That's one reason the runway needs a little more than five times spending.

Rungs are fixed in dollars

Only the amount you converted can come out early; its growth waits until 59½. So a rung is worth the same number of dollars in five years as the day you converted it, while prices keep rising. At 3% inflation, a $60,000 conversion buys about $51,800 of today's spending when it matures. To cover the same spending five years on, a rung needs to be about 16% larger than today's budget, or the gap has to come from Roth contributions or the brokerage account.

The ordering rules matter here too. If you still have Roth contributions in the account, a withdrawal takes those first and the matured conversions after. That costs nothing, since both come out tax- and penalty-free, but it means your records need to track contributions and each year's conversion separately. Conversions are reported on IRS Form 8606 in the year you make them, and Roth withdrawals on the same form in the year you take them; keeping every year's copy is the simplest way to prove later which dollars came out.

Sizing each conversion: tax

In early retirement, with no paycheck, your income can be very low. A conversion uses that room: income that would otherwise go untaxed, or taxed at 10% or 12%, instead of withdrawals taxed at a higher rate later, when Social Security and required minimum distributions stack on top.

Converting $60,000 per year, Ines and Paul owe about $2,840 of federal tax on each conversion, an average of under 5%. If the same money came out later at 22%, the difference adds up to tens of thousands of dollars over a ladder.

Gains from the brokerage account stack on top. Long-term gains are taxed at 0% while taxable income, gains included, stays under $98,900 for a couple in 2026 ($49,450 single). The conversion fills the lower brackets first and the gains sit above it. With a $60,000 conversion and $30,000 of gains, their taxable income is $57,800, well under the line, so the gains are still taxed at 0%.

If you've ever made nondeductible, after-tax contributions to a traditional IRA, each conversion is partly taxable and partly not, in proportion across all your traditional IRAs, the pro-rata rule covered in the IRAs lesson. And since 2018 a conversion can't be undone, so convert the amount you mean to.

Sizing each conversion: health premiums

Before Medicare, the income that sets your marketplace premium tax credit, modified adjusted gross income or MAGI, includes conversions. In an early retirement, health premiums often cost more at the margin than the tax does.

Between about $35,000 and $65,000 of income, each extra $1,000 raises the premium by about $130 to $190, because the share of income you're expected to pay rises as income rises. Add the 10% or 12% income tax and a converted dollar in that range costs roughly 23 to 31 cents. Above about 300% of the poverty line the premium rises about $100 per $1,000.

Then there's the cliff. For 2026, the credit stops entirely above 400% of the poverty line, $84,600 for a household of two, and this couple's premium jumps from about $8,400 to about $27,100 per year. A conversion that crosses it by a dollar costs them almost $19,000. Ines and Paul's $60,000 conversion plus $30,000 of gains, $90,000 in all, would put them over it.

At the other end, very low income has its own problem. Once the rungs are maturing, you live on money that isn't income at all. Income under 138% of the poverty line, about $29,900 for two in 2026, moves adults to Medicaid in states that expanded it. In the ten states that haven't, income under the poverty line gets no premium credit at all. Ladder builders often convert enough each year to keep income inside the range they want, rather than as little as possible.

The health cover before 65 lesson goes into the credit itself. For a ladder, the practical rule is to set each year's conversion with both the tax brackets and the premium curve in view.

When a ladder fits

A ladder suits someone retiring years before 59½ with most of their savings in pre-tax accounts and enough elsewhere to cover the first five years. It's more flexible than 72(t) payments: you choose each year's amount, you can skip a year, and nothing is clawed back if plans change. It also shrinks the traditional balance before required minimum distributions begin in your seventies, which can lower taxes for decades.

It fits less well if:

  • you're close to 59½ already, since a conversion made within five years of it won't mature before you can reach the money anyway;
  • you have little outside your traditional accounts, in which case 72(t) payments from part of an IRA can pay the first five years while the ladder builds;
  • you're leaving a job at 55 or later, when the rule of 55 may open the 401(k) directly. Rolling that 401(k) to an IRA to convert gives the rule up.

A ladder also depends on the rules staying much the same for a decade. They do change: until 2018, a conversion could be undone, and now it can't.

Common questions

Does the five-year rule apply after 59½?

Not for the 10% penalty: after 59½, converted amounts can come out penalty-free whenever you like. Earnings still need five years since your first Roth contribution or conversion to come out tax-free.

Can I pay the conversion's tax out of the conversion?

You can have tax withheld, but before 59½ the withheld part counts as an early withdrawal and carries the 10% penalty. Paying the tax from other money keeps the whole amount converting and avoids it.

Do I need earned income to convert?

No. Contributions to a Roth IRA need earned income; conversions don't, and there's no income limit on them.

What if I need a rung early?

You can take it. Under 59½, the 10% applies to the taxable part of that conversion, but you don't pay income tax on it again.

In short

  • Each conversion has its own five-year clock, starting January 1 of the year you convert; after it, the converted amount can come out with no penalty.
  • A ladder needs five years of spending, plus the conversions' tax, from other money first.
  • In 2026 a couple with no other income can convert $32,200 tax-free and $57,000 with at most 10% tax.
  • Conversions count as income for marketplace premium credits, so size each year with both tax and premiums in view.
  • Earnings on converted money stay put until 59½; the ladder spends only what was converted.
