For many retirees the home is the largest thing they own and the largest bill they pay. It can stay a cost, be turned partly into cash by moving somewhere smaller, or be borrowed against without moving at all. Each choice changes what your savings have to do.

The home as part of the plan

A retirement plan usually counts the home in two ways. Its costs are part of yearly spending, and its value is held back as a reserve rather than spent. That's a sensible default: you have to live somewhere, and a home is hard to spend a piece at a time. But it means a lot of wealth sits outside the plan, and it's worth deciding on purpose what role it plays.

Owning outright doesn't make housing free. Property tax, insurance and upkeep continue for as long as you own, and they tend to rise. On a $450,000 home, property tax at 1.1% of value and upkeep at 1% come to about $9,450 per year before insurance. Those costs rise with the home's value, and homeowners insurance has climbed sharply in some states as storm and wildfire losses have grown.

A mortgage adds to that. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies found that the share of homeowners aged 65 to 79 with a mortgage rose from 24% in 1989 to 41% in 2022. The mortgage payoff lesson covers whether to clear one before retiring, including the tax cost of paying it off from an IRA.

Downsizing: what it really frees up

Selling a larger home and buying a smaller one is the most common way to turn home equity into retirement money. It almost always frees up less than the gap between the two prices, and the yearly savings can be smaller than expected.

A $200,000 difference in price becomes $148,000 of cash. Invested and drawn at 4% per year, that's about $5,900 of income per year, useful but not life-changing. The ongoing savings nearly vanish here, because condo dues replace most of what's saved on tax and upkeep. Downsizing tends to pay off most when the move also cuts property taxes sharply, when it's to a cheaper area, or when the smaller home really does cost less to run.

There are good reasons to move that have nothing to do with money: a single floor, less to maintain, being closer to family or care. Moving at 70 by choice is usually easier than moving at 82 because you have to. Some retirees sell and rent instead, which frees all the equity and ends upkeep and property tax, at the price of rising rents. The rent or buy lesson works through that trade.

Taxes on the sale

Most home sales owe no federal tax. You can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned the home and lived in it as your main home for at least two of the five years before the sale. Gain is the sale price, less selling costs, less what you paid and what you spent on improvements over the years. Keep those records.

Long-time owners in expensive areas can exceed the limit. If the house above was bought decades ago for $250,000, the gain is $320,000: $600,000 less $30,000 of selling costs, less $250,000. A married couple owes nothing. A single owner has $70,000 over the $250,000 limit, about $10,500 at a 15% capital gains rate.

Widows and widowers get two breaks. A surviving spouse who hasn't remarried can still use the $500,000 limit on a sale within two years of the spouse's death, if the usual requirements are met. And the late spouse's share of the home gets a new tax basis at its value on the date of death, the whole home in community property states, which can wipe out much of the gain. Both can be worth planning a sale around.

Staying put: aging in place

Most people say they want to stay in their homes as they age, and many do. Whether the home will still work at 85 is another matter. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies found that fewer than 4% of US homes have all three basic accessibility features: single-floor living, a no-step entry, and wide hallways and doorways.

Changes like grab bars, a walk-in shower, better lighting or a ramp can be modest; a first-floor bedroom suite or stair lift costs much more. It's cheaper and easier to make them on your own schedule, ideally while you're still doing other renovations, than in a rush after a fall. Staying put also means planning for help at home if you need it, which is often the largest cost of aging in place. The long-term care lesson covers those costs.

Property tax relief

For retirees on fixed incomes, rising property tax can be what makes staying hard. Most states and many localities offer some relief, though the rules vary widely and you usually have to apply:

  • Homestead exemptions take part of a main home's value off the tax rolls. Many states add a larger exemption for older homeowners.
  • Assessment freezes or caps hold the taxable value, or its growth, steady for qualifying seniors.
  • Circuit breakers give a credit or refund when property tax exceeds a set share of income, so the relief is largest for those with the lowest incomes.
  • Deferral programs let qualifying older owners postpone property tax, usually with interest, as a lien repaid when the home is sold or the owner dies.

Your county assessor's office can tell you what's available and how to apply. These programs often have income or age limits, and some are little used because people don't know about them.

Reverse mortgages

A reverse mortgage lets you borrow against your home without monthly payments. Instead of you paying the lender, the loan balance grows as you draw money and as interest and fees are added. It's repaid when you sell, move out for good or die. Most are Home Equity Conversion Mortgages, or HECMs, insured by the Federal Housing Administration.

Who can get one

  • You must be 62 or older and live in the home as your main residence.
  • You must own it outright or owe little enough that the reverse mortgage can pay off what's left at closing.
  • You can't be behind on federal debt, such as federal taxes or student loans, though the loan can be used to pay it off.
  • You must show you can keep paying property tax, insurance and upkeep, or agree to have some of the loan set aside for them.
  • You must first meet with a counselor from a HUD-approved agency, who goes through the costs and the alternatives.

How much and how it's paid

How much you can borrow depends on the age of the youngest borrower, current interest rates and the home's value, up to a national limit. For 2026, HUD's maximum claim amount is $1,249,125, up from $1,209,750 in 2025, so value above that doesn't raise the loan. You get only part of the value, a share that rises with age and falls as interest rates rise. You can take it as a lump sum, as monthly payments for a set number of years or for as long as you live in the home, as a line of credit, or as a mix. Money from the loan isn't taxable income, since it's borrowed.

With an adjustable-rate HECM, the unused part of a line of credit grows each year at the same rate the loan does. A line opened at 62 and left alone can be much larger at 75. That growth is the reason some planners suggest opening one early as a standby reserve.

What it costs

HECMs are expensive to set up. Since 2017 the FHA has charged an upfront mortgage insurance premium of 2% of the home's value, up to the limit, plus 0.5% per year on the balance. On a $400,000 home that's $8,000 at closing. Lenders add an origination fee, which HUD caps, and the usual closing costs. All of it can be paid from the loan, which is why the balance can grow faster than people expect.

The rules that protect you, and the ones that can cost you the home

  • The loan is non-recourse: you or your heirs never owe more than the home is worth. If the balance exceeds the value, heirs can sell the home for at least 95% of its appraised value, or keep it by paying the lesser of the balance or 95% of the appraisal, and FHA insurance covers the rest.
  • A spouse not on the loan who qualifies as an eligible non-borrowing spouse can generally stay in the home after the borrower dies. A spouse too young to be on the loan should make sure this protection applies before closing.
  • You must keep paying property tax and insurance and keep the home in repair. Falling behind is the main way reverse mortgage borrowers lose their homes.
  • Moving out ends it. The loan generally comes due once the home stops being your main residence, including after more than 12 months in a row in a care facility, unless a co-borrower or an eligible spouse still lives there.

Where it can fit

A reverse mortgage used to be thought of only as a last resort. In a 2012 paper in the Journal of Financial Planning, Barry Sacks and Stephen Sacks tested drawing on a reverse mortgage line of credit after years when investments fell, and drawing from savings in other years. They found that the coordinated approach made it more likely that savings lasted than using home equity only once savings ran out. It works the same way as holding cash for down markets, explained in the sequence-risk lesson: you avoid selling investments at low prices.

It costs a lot to set up, though, and it uses equity that might otherwise go to heirs or pay for care later. A reverse mortgage tends to fit people who plan to stay in their home for many years, have most of their wealth in it, and want either more income or a reserve. It fits poorly for anyone likely to move within a few years, or who couldn't keep up with tax and insurance.

Home equity as a reserve

Even if you never sell or borrow, your home's value is doing work in your plan. It's a backstop for long-term care, which can cost far more than ordinary spending late in life. It covers the risk of living much longer than planned. And it's an inheritance that gets a tax break: heirs get a stepped-up basis to the value at death, so the gain built up over your lifetime is never taxed.

For these reasons, a common approach is to leave home equity out of the withdrawal-rate math and treat it as the last reserve. A plan that lasts on savings and income alone, with the home behind it, is sturdier than one that needs the house sold at a set age. If you do plan to downsize, say at 75, put the cash it would free, after costs, into the plan at that age, and lower your spending by any real ongoing savings, rather than counting the whole price difference.

A home equity line of credit opened while you're still working, when it's easier to qualify on income, is another way to keep the reserve within reach. Lenders can freeze or reduce these lines, though, so a HELOC is less dependable than a HECM line of credit, which can't be cut while you meet the loan's terms.

Seeing it in your own numbers

Mortgage shows what's left on your loan, when it ends and what extra payments or a lump sum would do, so you can see whether it will be gone before you retire. For a move, work out the cash it would free from your own price, selling costs and the new home's cost, as in the table above, and the change in yearly costs. Then add those to your plan at the age you'd move and see how much they change the result. Often the answer is less than expected, which is useful to know either way.

Common questions

Should I count my home's value in my retirement savings?

Usually not in the amount you plan to draw from. You still need somewhere to live, so only the part you'd free by moving, after costs, can fund spending. Many people keep the home as a reserve for care or a long life instead.

Can heirs lose the house if I have a reverse mortgage?

They can keep it by paying off the loan, or 95% of the appraised value if that's less, often with a new mortgage of their own. If they don't want it, they can sell it and keep anything above the balance. They never owe more than the home is worth.

Do I owe tax when I sell my home?

Usually not. Up to $250,000 of gain is excluded, $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned and lived in the home for two of the last five years. Only gain above that is taxed, at capital gains rates.

Is it better to downsize or take a reverse mortgage?

They suit different situations. Downsizing frees more cash and usually lowers costs, but means moving. A reverse mortgage lets you stay but costs more and shrinks what's left for heirs. A HUD-approved counselor will go through both with you as part of the reverse mortgage process.

In short

  • A paid-off home still costs property tax, insurance and upkeep every year, and those rise.
  • Downsizing frees less than the price difference after selling, buying and moving costs, and condo dues can eat the yearly savings.
  • Gains on a main home are tax-free up to $250,000, or $500,000 for a married couple, and heirs get a stepped-up basis.
  • Reverse mortgages (HECMs) start at 62, require counseling, are capped at $1,249,125 of value for 2026, and cost a lot to set up.
