Married people can collect Social Security on their own record, on their spouse's, or as a widow or widower, and the rules connect the three. For a couple, the most important figure is usually the survivor benefit: what the one left behind will live on. It's set mostly by when the higher earner claims, which is why couples often should not make the decision one person at a time.

The spousal benefit

A husband or wife can receive a benefit based on their spouse's work record. At the receiving spouse's own full retirement age, it's worth up to 50% of the worker's primary insurance amount (PIA), the worker's benefit at full retirement age (see how your benefit is figured). It doesn't come out of the worker's check. Both are paid in full.

Three conditions apply. The worker has to have filed for their own benefit first. The spouse has to be at least 62, or caring for the worker's child who is under 16 or disabled. And the couple must usually have been married for at least a year.

It tops up your own benefit

Most spouses today have a work record of their own. Social Security always pays your own benefit first, then adds a spousal top-up only if half the other spouse's PIA is larger than your own PIA. You end up with the larger of the two amounts, never both added together.

Take Sam and Jordan, both born in 1964, so both have a full retirement age of 67. Sam's PIA is $2,800 per month. Jordan worked fewer years and has a PIA of $1,000. Half of Sam's PIA is $1,400, so Jordan's top-up is $400. At 67, with Sam already collecting, Jordan gets $1,000 on Jordan's own record plus $400 on Sam's: $1,400 in all.

Had Jordan's own PIA been $1,500, more than half of Sam's, there'd be no top-up at all. That's the common case now that most couples both work, and it's why a two-earner couple's decisions turn mostly on the survivor benefit, covered below.

Claiming it early

A spousal benefit is cut more steeply for an early claim than a worker's own: 25/36 of 1% for each of the first 36 months before full retirement age, and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond. At 62 with a full retirement age of 67, it's 65% of the full spousal amount, or 32.5% of the worker's PIA instead of 50%.

And unlike your own benefit, a spousal benefit earns no delayed credits. It reaches its maximum at your full retirement age, so a spouse who's collecting only a spousal amount gains nothing by waiting past it.

You can't pick one and save the other

Until 2016, some couples used "file and suspend" or a "restricted application" to collect a spousal benefit while letting their own grow. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2015 closed both. For anyone born on or after January 2, 1954, applying for either your own or a spousal benefit counts as applying for both, which Social Security calls deemed filing. You get whatever combination you're due at that age, with the reductions for that age. One consequence: the spousal top-up can't start until the worker files, so if Jordan claims at 62 while Sam waits until 70, Jordan gets $700 until Sam files and the top-up only then.

The survivor benefit

When a worker dies, a widow or widower can receive the worker's benefit in place of their own. In effect the larger of the couple's two checks outlives the first death, and for most couples that's worth far more over a lifetime than any spousal benefit.

At the survivor's full retirement age, the survivor benefit is 100% of what the deceased was receiving, or was due. That includes any delayed credits they earned by waiting. If the deceased died before claiming, it's based on their PIA, plus any credits for months they had waited past full retirement age.

The survivor gets the larger of the two checks, never both, so the household loses the smaller one when the first spouse dies.

The age rules for survivors

A survivor can claim as early as 60, or 50 if disabled, or at any age while caring for the deceased's child under 16. Claimed at 60, the benefit is 71.5% of the full survivor amount, and the reduction shrinks evenly to zero at the survivor's full retirement age. That age runs on its own schedule, two years behind the retirement one: 66 for survivors born from 1945 to 1956, rising by two months per birth year to 67 for those born in 1962 or later.

Survivor benefits aren't covered by deemed filing, which gives a widow or widower a real choice. You can take a reduced survivor benefit early and switch to your own benefit, grown with delayed credits, at 70. Or take your own reduced benefit early and switch to an unreduced survivor benefit at your survivor full retirement age. Which works better depends on which of the two would be larger in the end.

If the deceased claimed early

A survivor's benefit is cut when the deceased claimed early, but there's a floor, sometimes called the widow's limit. The survivor gets the larger of the reduced amount the deceased was receiving and 82.5% of the deceased's PIA. So the cost of an early claim to the survivor is smaller than the cost to the worker: it can't take the survivor below 82.5%.

Other rules worth knowing

  • The marriage generally must have lasted at least nine months, with exceptions such as an accidental death.
  • Remarrying before 60 ends a survivor's eligibility while the new marriage lasts. Remarrying at 60 or later doesn't.
  • Social Security also pays a one-time death payment of $255 to a surviving spouse or child, an amount capped there since 1954.

Why the higher earner's claim matters most

The lower earner's own check stops at the first death, whichever spouse dies first. The higher earner's check carries on until the second death. So the higher earner's claiming age sets the income of whichever spouse lives longer, possibly for decades.

How couples usually split it

The common pattern for a couple with unequal records is for the higher earner to wait, often to 70, and the lower earner to claim earlier. The lower earner's own check stops at the first death however long they waited, so taking it earlier costs less and pays for some of the years while the higher earner waits. If the lower earner's own PIA is well under half the higher earner's, there's even less reason for them to wait past full retirement age, since the spousal part stops growing there.

The pattern bends with the details. A large age gap, with a much younger spouse, strengthens the case for the higher earner to wait. Two similar records make the choice closer, because the survivor benefit adds little over the survivor's own. And if the higher earner is in poor health, waiting still protects the spouse, since the survivor gets the larger check however short the higher earner's own life turns out to be.

Divorced spouses

A marriage that lasted at least 10 years keeps counting after a divorce. A divorced spouse can collect on an ex-spouse's record if they are 62 or older and unmarried, and the ex is 62 or older. If the divorce was at least two years ago, the ex doesn't have to have filed yet. The benefits are figured the same way as for a current spouse: up to 50% of the ex's PIA at full retirement age, as a top-up to your own.

Collecting on an ex's record doesn't reduce the ex's benefit or the benefit of the ex's current spouse. Several former spouses can each collect on the same record if each marriage lasted 10 years.

Survivor benefits follow the same 10-year rule. A surviving divorced spouse can get the full survivor benefit on the same terms as a widow or widower, and remarrying at 60 or later doesn't end it.

Government pensions and the Social Security Fairness Act

Until recently, two rules cut Social Security for people with a pension from work that didn't pay Social Security tax, such as many teachers, police officers and firefighters in some states, and federal workers hired before 1984. The Windfall Elimination Provision reduced their own benefit. The Government Pension Offset reduced spousal and survivor benefits by two-thirds of the government pension, which often wiped them out entirely: a retired teacher with a $3,000 monthly pension lost $2,000 of any spousal or survivor benefit.

The Social Security Fairness Act, signed on January 5, 2025, repealed both. They no longer apply to benefits payable for January 2024 and later, and Social Security paid back benefits for the months from January 2024. By July 2025 it had made more than 3.1 million of those payments, totaling $17 billion.

If you or your spouse have a non-covered pension, two things follow. Spousal and survivor benefits that used to be zero may now be worth a lot, so the couple's claiming decision may look quite different from what it did a few years ago. And anyone who never applied for a spousal or survivor benefit because the offset would have erased it has to apply now, since a benefit only starts once someone files for it.

Common questions

Can I collect a spousal benefit while my own benefit grows?

Not if you were born on or after January 2, 1954. Deemed filing means applying for one is applying for both. The exception is survivor benefits: a widow or widower can take one and switch to the other later.

My spouse died before claiming. What do I get?

The survivor benefit is based on what your spouse was due. If they died before full retirement age, that's their full PIA. If they died after it without claiming, it includes the delayed credits they had earned up to the month they died.

Does my spousal benefit go up if I wait until 70?

No. The spousal part reaches its maximum at your full retirement age. Only your own benefit earns delayed credits after that.

I'm divorced and remarried. Can I collect on my first spouse's record?

Not while you're remarried, for a spousal benefit. For a survivor benefit, remarrying at 60 or later doesn't stop it; remarrying before 60 does, unless that later marriage ends.

We both earned about the same. Does any of this matter?

Less. With similar records there's no spousal top-up, and the survivor benefit is only a little larger than the survivor's own. Each of you can treat the decision much as a single person would, though the longer of your two lives is still the one that counts for the larger check.

In short

  • A spousal benefit tops up your own to as much as 50% of your spouse's PIA at your full retirement age, and it earns no delayed credits.
  • A survivor keeps the larger of the two checks, including any delayed credits the deceased earned, and loses the smaller.
  • So the higher earner's claiming age sets the survivor's income, which is why the higher earner often waits.
  • Marriages of 10 years or more count after a divorce, for both spousal and survivor benefits.
  • The Social Security Fairness Act ended the WEP and GPO from January 2024, so people with government pensions may now be due spousal or survivor benefits they were denied before.
