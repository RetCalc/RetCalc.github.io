A pension or an annuity turns a pile of money into a paycheck that lasts as long as you do. That protects against two of the biggest risks in retirement, a bad run of markets and a long life, in a way no withdrawal strategy can. The price is giving up control of the money, and often protection from inflation.

How lifetime income works

An annuity, at its simplest, is a contract: you pay an insurance company a lump sum, and it pays you a set amount every month for the rest of your life. A traditional pension works the same way, except your employer funded it while you worked.

The reason an insurer can pay more than you could safely pay yourself is pooling. If you're spending your own savings, you have to plan for the chance you'll live to 100, even though you probably won't. An insurer selling to thousands of 65-year-olds only has to plan for how long the group lives on average. The money left behind by those who die early pays for those who live long. Economists call the extra income this produces mortality credits.

Two things set what an annuity pays. Your age at purchase matters because the older you are, the fewer years the insurer expects to pay. Interest rates matter because the insurer invests your premium, mostly in bonds, until it's paid out; when rates are higher, each dollar buys more income. That's why payout rates quoted a few years ago, or even a few months ago, may not apply today. Always get a current quote.

The main kinds

For retirement income, the plain products are usually the clearest value: a pension, an immediate annuity or a deferred income annuity. You can compare them directly, because each comes down to one number, the income per dollar. Variable and indexed annuities with income riders can also guarantee lifetime withdrawals, but their costs are spread across several fees and their guarantees are harder to compare, so read the contract closely or have someone without a commission at stake read it for you.

Pensions: monthly income or a lump sum

If you have a pension, you'll likely face two decisions when you leave: which payment form to take, and, if the plan offers one, whether to take a lump sum instead.

The survivor choice

A single-life pension pays the most but stops when you die. A joint-and-survivor pension pays less while you're alive and continues to your spouse afterwards, at 50%, 75% or 100% of the amount. For a married participant, federal law makes a joint-and-survivor pension of at least 50% the default. Choosing anything else needs your spouse's written consent, witnessed by a notary or a plan representative. For most couples who depend on the pension, the survivor option is the safer choice, since the household loses one Social Security check at the first death as well.

Lump sum or monthly

Many plans offer to pay the pension's value as a lump sum, which you can roll into an IRA. The lump sum is worked out from interest rates the IRS publishes each month, based on corporate bond yields, and a mortality table. Higher rates mean a smaller lump sum for the same pension.

A useful check: divide the yearly pension by the lump sum. If the plan offers $300,000 or $24,000 per year for life, the pension pays 8% of the lump sum's value. Then ask an insurer what the same $300,000 would buy in an immediate annuity, with the same survivor terms. If the pension pays more, taking the monthly income gets you lifetime income more cheaply than you could buy it. A lump sum can still make sense if you're in poor health, if you already have enough guaranteed income, or if you're worried about the employer, though private pensions are insured.

That insurance comes from the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC), which covers most private-sector pensions up to a limit if a plan fails. For a plan that ends in 2026, the limit for a 65-year-old taking a single-life pension is $7,789.77 per month, and it's lower for earlier ages and joint pensions. Government pensions aren't covered by the PBGC; they're backed by the government that sponsors them.

Inflation: the cost most people miss

Most annuities, and most private-sector pensions, pay the same number of dollars every year. Many government pensions rise with inflation at least in part, and Social Security rises with it fully. A level payment loses buying power every year, and over a long retirement the loss is large.

At 2.5% inflation, a $24,000 pension buys what $14,647 buys today by 85 and $11,442 by 95. At 3.5% it falls by almost two-thirds over thirty years. A level annuity is still a promise you can count on. It just covers less of your spending each year, so the rest of your plan has to grow to fill the gap.

You can buy protection. Some insurers offer annuities whose payments rise by a fixed amount each year, such as 2% or 3%, and a few offer increases tied to the consumer price index. Either one starts with a noticeably smaller payment than a level annuity for the same price, and the inflation-linked kind is hard to find.

What an annuity really costs

An immediate annuity has no visible fee. The cost is built into the payout, and it comes from a few places:

  • The insurer's expenses and profit, including any commission.
  • Who buys annuities. People who expect to live a long time are the ones who buy, so insurers price for longer lives than the population average. If your health is average or worse, you're paying for other buyers' longevity.
  • Giving up the money. Once you buy, you generally can't get the premium back. If you need a large sum later for care, a house or a family emergency, the annuity can't provide it.
  • Dying early. A life-only annuity stops at death with nothing to heirs. A refund feature or a guaranteed period, such as ten years, protects against that, and lowers the payment.
  • The insurer's strength. Annuities are backed by the insurance company, not the government. Every state has a guaranty association that protects annuity owners if an insurer fails, commonly up to $250,000 per person per insurer, though limits vary by state. Many buyers split large purchases between insurers to stay under the limit.

Taxes depend on where the money comes from. An annuity bought inside an IRA or 401(k), including a QLAC, is taxed like any other withdrawal: all of each payment is income. One bought with money you'd already paid tax on is taxed only partly: until you've received your premium back, part of each payment is treated as a return of your own money and isn't taxed.

What research says about annuities

Economists have long found annuities more attractive in theory than people find them in practice. In 1965 Menahem Yaari showed that someone with no wish to leave money behind should, under idealized conditions, turn all of their savings into lifetime income. In 2005 Thomas Davidoff, Jeffrey Brown and Peter Diamond showed the conclusion holds under much looser assumptions, and that even when full annuitization isn't best, some usually is. Yet few retirees buy annuities with their own savings. The gap between the theory and what people do is known as the annuity puzzle, and the usual explanations are the ones in the list above: wanting money for emergencies and heirs, the cost of insurers pricing for long-lived buyers, already having Social Security, and the discomfort of handing over a large sum for good.

When lifetime income makes sense

Annuities and pensions tend to fit people who:

  • Have essential spending that Social Security and any pension don't cover, and would be badly hurt if it went unpaid.
  • Are in good health, or come from a long-lived family, so pooling works in their favor.
  • Don't have a strong wish to leave a particular amount to heirs, or have other money set aside for that.
  • Worry about markets, or about managing money in their eighties and nineties, and would spend more comfortably with a paycheck.

They fit less well for people with small savings who need access to all of it, people in poor health, and people whose guaranteed income already covers their essential spending.

Social Security first

Before buying an annuity, look at the one you already have. Social Security is lifetime income that rises fully with inflation and is backed by the federal government, which no insurer offers. Each year you wait past full retirement age, up to 70, raises the benefit by 8%. Many planners see delaying Social Security, and spending savings in the meantime, as the cheapest way to buy more inflation-protected lifetime income. When to claim covers the trade-offs.

Timing and laddering

Because payouts depend on age and interest rates, some retirees spread purchases over several years instead of buying all at once, and buy later rather than at 65: income per dollar rises with each year of age, and the decision can wait until you know more about your health and spending. A deferred income annuity or QLAC that starts at 80 or 85 is another way to buy late-life income cheaply, since the insurer expects to pay fewer people for fewer years. It pairs well with spending more freely in your sixties and seventies; Spending changes with age explains why that's often how spending runs anyway.

Common questions

Is an annuity a good investment?

It's better thought of as insurance than as an investment. Its return depends mainly on how long you live: short if you die early, high if you live into your late nineties. You're buying certainty of income.

What happens to an annuity if I die soon after buying it?

With a life-only annuity, payments stop and nothing goes to heirs. A cash refund or installment refund feature pays heirs whatever you haven't yet received of your premium, and a period-certain annuity keeps paying for a set number of years. Each lowers the payment.

Should I take my pension at the earliest age?

Plans reduce the pension for each year you start early, and some raise it for starting later. Compare the extra years of payments with the larger checks, the same way you'd think about when to claim Social Security, and check whether the plan's early-retirement reduction is generous; some are.

Can I buy an annuity with my IRA?

Yes. Buying inside the IRA keeps the tax deferral, and every payment is taxed as income. Its payments generally take care of the RMDs on the money used to buy it, and a QLAC lets you shelter up to $210,000 (2026) from RMDs until it starts paying.

In short

  • Pensions and annuities pay for life by pooling: those who die early fund those who live long.
  • Payouts rise with age at purchase and with interest rates, so only a current quote tells you the price.
  • Most pay a level amount, and inflation can cut its buying power by half or more over a long retirement.
  • The costs are the insurer's margin, pricing for long-lived buyers, and giving up access to the money for good.
  • Delaying Social Security is usually the cheapest inflation-protected lifetime income to buy first.
