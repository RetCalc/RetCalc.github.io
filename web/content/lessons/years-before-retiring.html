Most of the decisions that shape a retirement are made in a short stretch: the last five years of work and the first year after it. Some have deadlines set by law, some can't be undone, and several depend on each other. This lesson puts them in order, says what each one involves, and points to the lesson that covers it in full.

The ages that matter

The rules for retirement accounts, Social Security and Medicare each run on their own age. None of them is the age you stop working, and the plan for the last few years is mostly about the gaps between them.

Three gaps come out of that table. Retire before 59½ and you need money you can reach without a penalty. Retire before 65 and you need health cover. And between the day you stop work and the start of Social Security and required distributions, your taxable income is often the lowest it will be for the rest of your life, which is worth planning around.

Five years out: find the number and close the gap

A spending figure from records

Everything else rests on what you'll spend, and five years out is the time to stop estimating it. Twelve months of bank and card statements, added up, will usually differ from the figure in your head, and the difference is usually upward. Take out what ends with work (commuting, payroll taxes, the saving itself) and add what begins: health premiums before 65, travel, a car that will need replacing. From spending to a target turns the result into a savings target, roughly 25 times what savings must pay each year after Social Security and any pension, and The 4% rule explains where the 25 comes from and when to use a larger multiple.

Starting five years ahead leaves time to act on the answer. If the target is out of reach you can save more, plan to spend less, or move the date, and each of those is easier with five years' notice than with one.

Catch-up contributions

The contribution limits rise in your fifties and again in your early sixties. For 2026 the ordinary limit on a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b) or the federal Thrift Savings Plan is $24,500. From the year you turn 50 you can add $8,000, and in the years you turn 60 through 63 the catch-up is $11,250 instead.

Two details catch people. A plan doesn't have to offer the larger age 60–63 catch-up, so check yours. And from 2026, if your wages from that employer were over $150,000 the year before, catch-up contributions have to go in as Roth. This year's limits has both rules in full, with the IRA and HSA figures.

The red zone and your stock share

The years just before and just after the retirement date are when a market fall costs the most, because the balance is at its largest and withdrawals are about to begin. Some planners call this stretch the retirement red zone, and Sequence-of-returns risk explains the mechanics. A fall of a third at 35 is an inconvenience. The same fall at 61 can move the date.

That raises a question: whether to hold less in stocks as the date approaches. Target-date funds do it automatically, and Vanguard's reach about half stocks at 65. In the US record, though, retirees holding anywhere from half to four-fifths in stocks could start at nearly the same safe rate, so cutting much below half gave up growth without making the worst cases better. How much in stocks has those figures.

Wade Pfau and Michael Kitces studied a different shape in a paper first circulated in 2013 and published in the Journal of Financial Planning in January 2014. They tested holding the least in stocks at the retirement date and then raising the share through retirement, a rising equity glide path. In their simulations a portfolio that started at 30% stocks and finished at 60% did better than one that held 60% throughout, and the best results at a 4% withdrawal rate came from paths starting at 20% to 40% in stocks. On the site's own history since 1926 the gain is small: at 4%, a path rising from 40% to 80% lasted in 68 of 70 retirements and one rising from 30% to 70% in 65, against 66 for a fixed 60%. It's a reasonable approach for someone most worried about the first few years, and it means the lowering has to happen before the date, which is a five-years-out decision.

A reserve outside stocks

A simpler version of the same idea is to build a reserve: two to five years of what savings will have to pay, held in bonds or cash, so that a fall in the first years doesn't force you to sell stocks at low prices. It's easier to build from new contributions over several years than by selling a large block of stocks in the last month. The cost is lower expected growth on that money. If you'll retire before 59½, the reserve also has to be somewhere you can reach it.

Three years out: the decisions with long lead times

Health cover, if you'll stop before 65

Medicare starts at 65 whatever age you retire. Before that the choices are a spouse's plan, COBRA, retiree cover if your employer has it, or a marketplace plan. COBRA usually runs 18 months, so it can reach Medicare only if you leave at 63½ or later. A marketplace plan's price depends on your income in the year of coverage: for 2026 the premium tax credit stops entirely above 400% of the poverty line, $62,600 for one person and $84,600 for a couple. The first year can be a problem, because a final paycheck, unused leave paid out and a bonus all count. Health cover before 65 covers what counts as income and how retirees keep under the line.

The pension choice

If you have a pension, ask the plan for an estimate at two or three retirement dates and for every payment form it offers. You'll choose between a single-life payment and a smaller one that continues to a surviving spouse, and possibly between monthly income and a lump sum. The survivor choice can't be changed once payments begin. Annuities and pensions shows how to compare a lump sum with the income it replaces.

Social Security: check the record, sketch the plan

Open a my Social Security account at ssa.gov and read your earnings record line by line. Your benefit is built from your 35 highest-paid years, and a missing or wrong year lowers it; errors are much easier to fix while you still have the W-2. The statement estimates your benefit at every age from 62 to 70.

Then sketch when each of you would claim. You don't have to claim when you stop work, and the two decisions are better made separately. For a couple, the higher earner's claiming age also sets what the survivor lives on. When to claim Social Security goes through the trade, and the Social Security Calculator compares the ages on your own record. Waiting means paying the early years from savings, so the claiming plan and the reserve above are one decision.

The mortgage

A mortgage payment in retirement has to come out of savings, and from a traditional account each dollar of payment needs more than a dollar of withdrawal. Paying the loan off with one large withdrawal in the year you retire can push income into higher brackets and over a Medicare surcharge line. Three years out there's room to pay extra from salary, or to decide to keep a low-rate loan and plan for the payment. Pay off the mortgage early? works through a case of each.

The income Medicare will look at

Medicare's premiums for higher incomes are set by your tax return from two years earlier. Your premium at 65 comes from the year you turn 63. A last year of full salary, a large Roth conversion or a big capital gain at 63 or 64 can add a surcharge at 65 and 66. For 2026 the first line is $109,000 of income for a single person and $218,000 for a couple. Stopping work counts as a life-changing event, so if the high income was your salary, you can ask Social Security to use your lower current income with Form SSA-44. A conversion or a capital gain doesn't qualify. IRMAA: Medicare's income surcharge has the tiers.

The last year: how you'll draw, and the date itself

A withdrawal strategy and an account order

Before the last paycheck, decide two things on paper. One is the rule for how much you'll take each year: a fixed amount that rises with inflation, a share of the balance, or guardrails that adjust after good and bad markets. Choosing a strategy compares fifteen of them at the same risk. The other is which accounts the money comes from, since traditional, Roth and taxable accounts are taxed differently and the order changes the lifetime tax bill. Which account to draw from first covers it.

If you'll leave between 55 and 59½, the order has one extra constraint. The age-55 rule covers only the plan at the employer you're leaving. Roll that 401(k) into an IRA and the money is back under the 59½ rule.

One more year

Nearly everyone close to the date asks whether to work another year. A year of work changes the plan in three ways at once: you save for one more year, the whole balance has one more year to grow, and there's one fewer year of retirement to pay for.

Take Sam and Jordan, both 62, with $1,200,000 saved in a mix of 60% stocks and 40% bonds. They save $30,000 per year and plan for the money to last to 95. Assume the balance grows 4.5% per year after inflation, the Calculator's Balanced setting. To measure what the savings can pay, use the highest starting withdrawal, raised with inflation each year, that lasted through every retirement of that length in the US record since 1926.

You can check the balance by hand: $1,200,000 grows by 4.5%, which is $54,000, and $30,000 of saving brings it to $1,284,000. One year of work raised the spending their savings support by $3,476 per year, 7.9%, for the rest of their lives.

The chart shows two things. In this example growth is the biggest part, because $1,200,000 earning 4.5% adds more in a year than the couple can save. For someone with a smaller balance and a high savings rate, the saving would lead. And the shorter retirement is the smallest part by this measure, since the worst stretches in the record did most of their damage in the first decade, so a 32-year plan is only a little easier to fund than a 33-year one.

The example leaves out some of what a year of work does. It's a year of employer health cover instead of a marketplace premium, and a year closer to Medicare. It's a year you aren't drawing on savings while waiting to claim Social Security, and if it replaces a low-paid year among your best 35, it raises the benefit a little. A 2018 study by Gila Bronshtein, Jason Scott, John Shoven and Sita Slavov, which assumed people claim Social Security when they stop work, found that working three to six months longer raised a household's sustainable retirement income as much as saving one more percentage point of pay for 30 years.

The growth isn't guaranteed. If markets fall during the extra year, the balance can end lower than it began, and the year's value is then that you didn't make withdrawals during the fall. What you give up is a year of retirement in good health, which the arithmetic leaves out. Past the point where the plan works with a margin you're comfortable with, each additional year buys less that you need.

The first year: enrollment, taxes and the first withdrawal

Medicare at 65

Your initial enrollment period is the seven months around the month you turn 65. Miss it without cover from current work and the Part B penalty lasts for life. If you work past 65 with employer cover, you have eight months after the job or the cover ends to sign up, and COBRA doesn't extend that. Anyone still paying into an HSA should stop six months before applying for Medicare, because Part A is backdated by up to six months. Medicare has the windows, the penalties and the choice between Medigap and Medicare Advantage, which is hard to reverse later.

The low-income years

Between the last paycheck and the start of Social Security and required distributions, many retirees have a few years of unusually low taxable income. These years can be used to move money from traditional accounts to Roth at low rates, or to sell appreciated investments while long-term gains are taxed at 0%. Both raise income, so each has to be weighed against the health-insurance credit before 65 and the Medicare surcharge lines from 63 on. Roth conversions explains filling a bracket, and Required minimum distributions shows what the conversions are trying to shrink. The window closes a little each year, so it helps to have the first year's amount worked out before the year starts.

The first withdrawal

Nobody withholds tax from a retiree's income unless the retiree arranges it. IRA withdrawals have 10% withheld by default, which you can raise or lower, and a 401(k) payment made to you usually has 20% withheld. If withholding won't cover the bill, the IRS expects estimated payments through the year. You generally avoid a penalty by paying at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's, with a higher prior-year share for higher incomes. How retirement income is taxed covers how each kind of income is treated.

The rest is mechanics. Many retirees set up a monthly transfer from the reserve to a checking account, so spending still arrives like a paycheck, and refill the reserve once or twice a year according to the strategy they chose. If you're claiming Social Security, you can apply up to four months before you want it to start. And after twelve months, compare what you spent with what you planned. The first year is usually the first time the spending figure has been tested, and it's early enough to adjust.

Common questions

What if the market falls just before my date?

You have three levers: work longer, spend less in the first years, or draw on the reserve while stocks recover. A plan that already includes a reserve and some spending you could cut has answered the question in advance. When markets fall covers what tends to help and what doesn't.

Should I move everything to cash before I retire?

History argues against it. At a 4% starting withdrawal, retirements held entirely in bonds ran out within 30 years more often than not, while those with half or more in stocks nearly always lasted, because the money has to keep up with decades of inflation. A few years of spending outside stocks handles the near-term risk without giving up the growth on the rest.

I only have a year left. What comes first?

The items with deadlines or that can't be undone: health cover for the day your employer's plan ends, the pension election, Medicare if you're near 65, and where the first year's cash will come from. The spending figure comes next, since the withdrawal plan depends on it. Catch-up saving and shifting the mix matter less with a year to go.

Does this apply if I'm retiring in my forties or fifties?

The order of decisions is the same, but the gaps are longer: more years before 59½, before Medicare and before Social Security. The Retiring early lessons, starting with FIRE, cover reaching the money and paying for the years in between.

Do I have to stop all at once?

No. Part-time or seasonal work in the first few years cuts the withdrawals that matter most, and it can keep taxable income in a useful range. Before full retirement age, earnings above $24,480 in 2026 hold back Social Security you've already claimed, so the work and the claiming date need planning together.

In short

  • Three years out: line up health cover before 65, the pension election, a claiming plan for Social Security and the mortgage, and watch income from the year you turn 63.
  • The last year: choose a withdrawal strategy and an account order, and price one more year of work before deciding on it.
