Most people weigh their rent against a mortgage payment. That leaves out most of what owning costs, and the money a renter keeps free to invest. Which one comes out ahead depends on a handful of numbers you can look up and one you can't: how long you'll stay.

What owning costs beyond the payment

Take a $450,000 home bought with 20% down, $90,000, and a 30-year loan of $360,000 at 7.25%. These are the numbers Rent vs. Buy opens with. The loan payment, principal and interest, is $2,456 per month. That's the figure a lender quotes and the one people hold up against their rent, but it's only part of the bill.

Property tax and insurance usually get collected with the payment through an escrow account, so most owners feel them. Upkeep is the one people leave out. Roofs, water heaters, furnaces, appliances, paint and the plumbing call that arrives on a holiday all come from the owner, and 1% of the home's value per year is a common rule of thumb for the long-run average. In practice it comes in lumps: little for years, then a new roof.

With less than 20% down, lenders add private mortgage insurance, PMI, until the loan falls to 80% of the price, when you can ask to drop it (it ends on its own at 78%). Rent vs. Buy assumes 0.6% of the loan per year when it applies. And there's the cash at closing: $103,500 that a renter would still have, invested.

The costs you don't get back

Comparing rent with the full cost of owning isn't quite fair either, because part of each mortgage payment is principal. That part isn't spent. It moves from your bank account into your home equity, and you get it back when you sell. The fair comparison sets rent, which is all spent, against the parts of owning that are also gone for good.

Before price growth, owning this home throws away about $3,700 per month in its first year, double the $1,800 rent. After 4% growth on the home's value, it's about $2,200 per month. So at $1,800 rent, renting is cheaper even if the house appreciates as hoped; at $2,400, owning is cheaper from year one, and it gets more so over time, because rent rises every year while most of the mortgage payment stays fixed and slowly turns from interest into principal.

Two one-time costs sit on top. Closing costs when you buy, $13,500 here at 3% of the price, are gone the day you sign. Selling costs, mostly agent commissions, often run 3% to 6% of the sale price. Spread over thirty years they hardly matter; spread over three, they can wipe out everything else. So how long you stay often decides the answer.

Price-to-rent and the break-even year

The quickest test is the price-to-rent ratio: a home's price divided by one full year's rent on a similar place. The $450,000 home renting at $1,800 per month has a ratio of about 21 ($450,000 ÷ $21,600). At $2,400 per month it's about 16. A low ratio means rent is expensive compared with buying; a high one means renting is the bargain.

Rent vs. Buy turns that into a race. Both households spend the same each month. The buyer pays the costs of owning; the renter pays rent and invests the down payment, the closing costs and, each month, whatever owning would have cost beyond the rent. Each year it values both as if they cashed out: the home sold, selling costs paid, gains taxed. The break-even year is the first year the buyer's net worth comes out ahead.

A rough guide you'll see often is that buying tends to win below a ratio of about 15 and renting tends to win above about 20. The table shows why it's only rough. At a 7.25% mortgage rate, even a ratio of 17 never pays off over thirty years, because higher rates make owning dearer. With cheaper money the line moves the other way.

The lead is small, though: after thirty years, buying is ahead by about $83,000 on net worth of $1.3 million, around 6%. Cases this close are common, and in them a small change in one assumption can flip the answer.

The assumptions that swing it

Every rent-or-buy answer rests on guesses about the next few decades: how fast home prices rise, how fast rent rises, what the renter's investments earn and what upkeep really costs. The table changes one guess at a time in the $2,400 case.

One point on home-price growth moves the result by hundreds of thousands of dollars, in either direction. So does one point on the renter's investment return, which is the cost of the money tied up in the house. Property tax rates vary a lot from place to place, from well under 1% of value in some states to around 2% in others, and they can matter as much as the price. Selling costs change the break-even year more than the thirty-year result, because they're paid once.

Rent growth gets less attention than it should. A fixed-rate mortgage locks in most of the owner's housing cost for thirty years. Only tax, insurance and upkeep keep rising. A renter's whole housing cost rises with the local market. In places where rents climb quickly, that steady payment is worth a lot.

Taxes on both sides

Mortgage interest and property tax

Mortgage interest and property tax are deductible, but only if you itemize, and only the part of your itemized total above the standard deduction actually saves anything. For 2026 the standard deduction is $32,200 for a married couple and $16,100 for a single filer. Interest is deductible on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt, and state and local taxes, property tax included, are capped at $40,400 for 2026, lower for incomes above $505,000.

In the example, the first year's interest of about $26,000 plus $4,950 of property tax comes to about $31,000, still under the $32,200 standard deduction for a couple. Unless they pay a lot of state income tax or give a lot to charity, the deduction saves them nothing. A single buyer would get more from it. Rent vs. Buy leaves the deduction out; if you itemize, buying looks a little better than it shows.

When you sell

Gains on a main home are largely tax-free. You can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned the home and lived in it as your main home for at least two of the five years before the sale. You can generally use the exclusion once every two years. The renter's investments get no such break; their gains are taxed when sold, which Rent vs. Buy counts at 15%.

What history says about home prices

In dollars, home prices have mostly gone up over long stretches. After inflation they've risen much more slowly. From January 1987 to July 2026, the S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller national index rose about 4.3% per year while consumer prices rose about 2.8%. That's around 1.4% per year faster than inflation. Rent vs. Buy's default of 4% per year, against inflation of about 3%, assumes a little less than that.

The drops can be large and long. From its peak in July 2006 to its low in February 2012, the same index fell 27%, and 35% after inflation. Owners who had to sell in those years, for a job or a divorce, often sold at a loss, and many with small down payments owed more than the home was worth.

Leverage works in both directions. With 20% down, a 10% rise in the home's price is a 50% gain on your down payment, and a 10% fall is a 50% loss. Most households hold no other investment with that much borrowed money behind it, in one asset, in one town.

What the numbers leave out

The race above treats a home as a pile of money. It's also where you live, and much of what makes people buy or rent doesn't fit in a spreadsheet.

  • The renter has to invest the difference. The comparison only works if the down payment and the monthly savings go into investments and stay there. A mortgage forces saving; renting only allows it. If the money would get spent, buying usually wins.
  • A renter can move for a job within weeks. An owner who has to sell in a slow market, or within a few years of buying, pays the transaction costs and takes whatever price is on offer.
  • Owners pick the paint and keep the dog; they can't be asked to leave when a lease ends. For families with children in school, that can count for a lot.
  • Owning means managing repairs and carrying the risk of a large, sudden bill. A renter calls the landlord.
  • In retirement, a paid-off home lowers what you need to live on for the rest of your life, and home equity is a reserve you can tap later. The lessons on paying off the mortgage and your home in retirement go into both.

These have no right answers. Running the numbers tells you what the reasons that aren't about money are costing you, or saving you, so you can weigh both.

Running it on your own numbers

Rent vs. Buy takes the price of a home you'd actually buy, your quoted rate, your local property tax rate and the rent on a similar place. Start there and look at two things: who's ahead at the end, and the break-even year.

Then compare the break-even year with how long you expect to stay. If you'd likely move within three to five years, buying rarely pays off. If the break-even year is far out, or "never", look at which assumption would have to change to bring it in. Try home prices at 3% and 5%, and investments at 6% and 8%, as in the table above. If buying wins in every version you'd call reasonable, it's a sturdy answer. If it flips on one guess, you're closer to a coin toss than it might seem, and the reasons in the last section can fairly decide it.

Common questions

Isn't renting just throwing money away?

No more than mortgage interest, property tax, insurance and upkeep are. In the example, those come to about $44,500 in the first year before counting any rise in the home's value, more than the rent on the same house at $1,800 per month. Both sides spend money on housing every month; the question is which spends less after the equity and the investments are counted.

How long do I need to stay for buying to pay off?

Often five to seven years or more, because buying and selling costs together can run 6% to 9% of the price. In the examples above it ranges from three years when rent is high to never when it's low. Rent vs. Buy shows the break-even year for your numbers.

Should I wait for mortgage rates to fall?

Nobody can tell you when or whether they will. A lower rate does help a lot: in the $2,400 case, 6.25% instead of 7.25% moves the break-even year from 9 to 5. If rates fall after you buy, you can often refinance, though it costs money to do it. If home prices rise while you wait, that can cancel out the lower rate.

Is a house a good investment?

It's a place to live that also builds equity and protects you from rising rents. As an investment alone, US home prices have grown only a little faster than inflation over long periods, and owning costs money every year.

What about putting less than 20% down?

You can buy sooner, but you'll pay private mortgage insurance until the loan falls to about 80% of the price, and you'll have more borrowed against the house. With less down, the renter in the comparison also has less cash to invest. Rent vs. Buy adds PMI when the down payment is under 20%.

In short

  • Owning costs much more than the mortgage payment: property tax, insurance, upkeep, the earnings on the down payment, and closing and selling costs.
  • The fair comparison sets rent against the costs of owning you don't get back. Principal paid becomes equity.
  • The price-to-rent ratio is the quickest test, and how long you stay is often the deciding number.
  • Small changes in home-price growth, investment returns, rent growth and the mortgage rate can flip a close answer.
  • Renting comes out ahead only if the renter invests the difference.
