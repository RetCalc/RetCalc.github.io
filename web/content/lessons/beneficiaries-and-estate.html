Most of what a typical household owns doesn't pass by will at all. Retirement accounts and life insurance go to whoever is named on a form, often filled out years ago, and the house and bank accounts usually follow how they're titled. Getting those forms and titles right, and having a few basic documents in place, matters more for most families than any tax strategy. This lesson explains how it works in general; it isn't legal advice, and state law changes many of the details.

How things pass when you die

Everything you own reaches someone by one of four routes, and the will only controls one of them.

  • A beneficiary designation. 401(k)s, IRAs, annuities, HSAs and life insurance pay whoever is named on the account's beneficiary form. The will has no say over them.
  • How it's titled. A home or account owned jointly "with right of survivorship" passes to the surviving owner automatically. A bank account marked payable on death (POD), or a brokerage account or, in many states, a home with a transfer on death (TOD) registration, goes straight to the named person.
  • A trust. Anything held in a living trust is handed out by the trustee under the trust's terms.
  • The will. Whatever's left, owned in your name alone with no beneficiary, goes through probate, the court process that confirms the will, pays the debts and distributes what remains. Without a will, state law decides who inherits.

The first three skip probate entirely, which is faster, private and usually cheaper. It also means a beneficiary form beats a will. If your will leaves everything to your children but your IRA's form still names an ex-spouse, the ex-spouse gets the IRA.

Beneficiary designations

Each form lets you name a primary beneficiary, who inherits first, and a contingent beneficiary, who inherits if no primary beneficiary is alive. You can name several in each tier and split by percentage. Always name a contingent. Without one, the account goes wherever the plan's or custodian's default sends it, which is often your estate.

Why not your estate

Naming your estate, or letting an account fall to it, sends money that could have skipped probate through the court, where it waits and can be reached by creditors. For an IRA it's worse: an estate isn't an individual, so it gets the least flexible payout rules. If the owner died before required distributions began, the whole account generally has to be emptied within five years.

Per stirpes or per capita

If you name your three children equally and one dies before you, what happens to that child's third? Under per stirpes, Latin for "by branch", it goes to that child's own children. Under per capita, it's usually split between the two surviving children. Many forms let you choose; if yours doesn't say, the plan's default or state law decides. It matters most in families with grandchildren.

Minors and trusts

Children under 18 can't legally manage money on their own. If a minor inherits directly, a court usually appoints a guardian or conservator to manage the money, with court reports and costs along the way, and the child gets full control at 18 in most states. There are better routes:

  • Name a custodian under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. The custodian manages the money until the child reaches the state's age, usually 18 or 21 and later in some states.
  • Name a trust as beneficiary, either a living trust or one your will creates. The trust can hold the money until whatever ages you choose, pay for school and living costs along the way, and keep it out of a court's hands.
  • For a beneficiary with a disability who receives, or may need, means-tested benefits such as SSI or Medicaid, a direct inheritance can disqualify them. A special needs trust is designed to avoid that, and it's a job for an attorney.

A trust named on a retirement account has its own payout rules, which depend on how the trust is written. That's one more reason to have an attorney draft it with your accounts in mind.

A spouse's rights in a 401(k)

Federal law gives your spouse a claim on most workplace retirement plans. Under ERISA, the law covering private employers' plans, a 401(k) must generally pay the whole account to your surviving spouse unless your spouse has agreed in writing to someone else. The consent has to acknowledge what it gives up and be witnessed by a notary or a plan representative. A prenuptial agreement doesn't count, because it's signed before the person is a spouse; the waiver has to be signed after the wedding. Pensions carry a similar right, a survivor pension, covered in Annuities and pensions.

IRAs aren't covered by ERISA. You can generally name anyone on an IRA without your spouse's signature, though in the nine community property states a spouse may have rights to part of it anyway. That's worth knowing when you roll a 401(k) into an IRA: the rollover leaves the spouse's federal protection behind, and the IRA needs a beneficiary form of its own. The 401(k)'s designation doesn't carry over. Government and church plans follow their own rules.

Divorce: the form still wins

Many states have laws that cancel an ex-spouse's beneficiary designation automatically at divorce. Those laws can't reach plans covered by ERISA. In Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (2001), the Supreme Court held that ERISA overrides a Washington state law that would have cut out an ex-wife, so she was entitled to the life insurance and pension, because her ex-husband had never changed the forms. In Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont (2009), the Court ruled that a DuPont savings plan was right to pay an ex-wife named on the form even though she'd given up her claim in the divorce decree. For assets outside ERISA the state laws can apply: in Sveen v. Melin (2018), the Court upheld Minnesota's law revoking an ex-spouse's designation on a private life insurance policy.

The safest rule follows from all three cases: after a divorce, change every form yourself. Don't rely on the decree or on state law to do it for you.

What heirs receive, and the tax

Retirement accounts

An inherited 401(k) or IRA keeps its tax character. A surviving spouse can generally treat it as their own. A few others, such as a minor child of the owner (until 21), a beneficiary who is disabled or chronically ill, or one no more than ten years younger than the owner, can stretch withdrawals over their own life expectancy. Almost everyone else, including most adult children, must empty it by the end of the tenth year after the owner's death, with yearly withdrawals along the way if the owner had already started required distributions. Required minimum distributions covers those rules, from the 2024 final IRS regulations.

Every dollar of an inherited traditional account is taxable income to the heir as it comes out. Ten years of large withdrawals can land in an adult child's peak earning years, at a higher rate than you paid. An inherited Roth IRA must also be emptied within ten years, but with no yearly minimum and, generally, no tax. That difference is one argument for Roth conversions if leaving money to children is part of the plan.

Everything else

Stocks, funds and a home you own when you die get a step-up in basis: their cost for tax purposes resets to their value on the date of death, so the gain during your lifetime is never taxed. When a married couple owns something jointly, half of it steps up at the first death; in community property states, both halves usually do. Retirement accounts get no step-up, since their tax was only ever deferred. The taxable account explains how this shapes which accounts to spend first.

Life insurance paid to a beneficiary isn't taxable income to them. It does count toward your estate for estate tax if you own the policy, which matters only for the largest estates.

Payable on death and transfer on death

These registrations are among the simplest estate tools. You fill in a beneficiary on an account you own; you keep full control while you're alive and can change it at any time; and when you die, the beneficiary claims it with a death certificate, without probate.

  • Bank accounts and CDs can be made payable on death. FDIC insurance treats these as trust accounts: $250,000 per owner for each beneficiary named, up to five beneficiaries, so $1,250,000 per owner at one bank.
  • Brokerage accounts can carry a TOD registration. Most states have adopted the uniform law that allows it, and most brokerages offer it.
  • Real estate can pass by a transfer on death deed in more than 30 states and the District of Columbia. Elsewhere, joint ownership or a living trust does the same job.
  • Vehicles can carry a TOD title in some states.

The common shortcut, adding a grown child to your account or deed as a joint owner, is riskier than it looks. The child legally owns part of it now, so their creditors or divorce can reach it, they can withdraw it, it may count as a gift, and it passes to that child alone even if your will splits everything between several. A TOD or POD registration avoids all of that.

The documents that matter

A basic estate plan is a handful of documents. Four of them work while you're still alive, if illness or injury leaves you unable to act for yourself.

For parents of young children, the guardian nomination is often the most important line in the will, and only a will can make it.

A revocable living trust is useful when you own property in more than one state, want privacy, or want money held for children past 18. It doesn't save income or estate tax, and it only covers what you actually retitle into it. Many families get the same result more simply with beneficiary forms, TOD and POD registrations and a will.

Estate and gift tax

The federal estate tax reaches very few families. For 2026, each person can leave $15 million free of federal estate tax, under the 2025 tax law known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The amount rises with inflation from 2027, and the law has no end date. Without it, the exemption would have fallen by about half in 2026. Above the exemption, the tax runs up to 40%.

Married couples do even better. Anything left to a spouse who is a US citizen passes free of estate tax, however large. And under portability, a surviving spouse can add whatever exemption the first spouse didn't use to their own, so a couple can leave up to $30 million between them. Portability isn't automatic: the executor has to file an estate tax return, Form 706, for the first spouse to die, even if no tax is owed. The IRS allows a late election for up to five years after the death for estates that weren't otherwise required to file.

Gifts

The same $15 million covers gifts made while you're alive. On top of it, you can give up to $19,000 per person per year in 2026, to as many people as you like, without using any of it or filing anything. Spouses can each give that much, so a couple can give $38,000 to each person. Gifts above that need a gift tax return, Form 709, but no tax is due until lifetime gifts pass the exemption. Paying a hospital or a school directly for someone's medical care or tuition doesn't count toward any of these limits. A 529 plan has a special rule that lets you count one gift as spread over five years, so up to $95,000 per child at once (see Saving for college).

Gifts of shares you've held for a long time carry your original cost with them, while the same shares inherited would get a step-up. For appreciated investments, waiting can save your heirs tax.

State estate and inheritance taxes

States are another matter. Twelve states and the District of Columbia have their own estate tax, some starting much lower than the federal one; Oregon's starts at $1 million. Five states have an inheritance tax, paid by the heir, usually with exemptions or lower rates for spouses and children. If you live in one of them or own property there, a modest estate can owe state tax even though it's far below the federal line. State taxes covers how states differ more broadly.

Keeping it current

Forms go out of date as life changes. Review every form and title after:

  • Marriage, divorce or the death of anyone named.
  • A birth or adoption. A child born after the form was filled in isn't on it, unless you named your children as a class and the plan allows it.
  • A new job, a rollover or a new account, each of which starts with a blank form.
  • A move to another state, which can change how wills, trusts and community property work.
  • A beneficiary who develops a disability, has creditor trouble or isn't ready to handle a large sum.

Most retirement plans and insurers let you check and change designations online. Ask for a confirmation and keep it, since the custodian's record is the one that counts. A short letter for your executor listing every account, every insurer and where the documents are kept has no legal force, but it saves the people you leave behind a great deal of searching.

When to see an estate attorney

Forms and registrations handle a lot on their own. A will, powers of attorney and health care documents are worth having drawn up properly for nearly everyone, and an estate attorney earns their fee when:

  • You have minor children and need a guardian named and money held in trust.
  • You're in a blended family, with children from an earlier marriage you want to protect.
  • Someone you'd leave money to has a disability, an addiction, money troubles or a shaky marriage.
  • You own a business, rental property or land in more than one state.
  • Your estate could owe state estate tax, or is anywhere near the federal exemption.
  • Your spouse isn't a US citizen, which changes how the marital exemption works.
  • You expect a family dispute.

Laws on wills, trusts, guardianship and probate are set by each state, and the details matter. Arriving with your accounts listed, your forms checked and your questions written down makes the meeting far more useful.

Seeing it in your own plan

The RMD calculator projects your traditional accounts year by year to age 100. The balance it shows at any age is roughly what your heirs would inherit and have to withdraw within ten years, all of it taxable to them. If that number is large, look at what Roth conversions in your low-income years would do to it, and to the tax your heirs would pay.

Then make a list of every account, who owns it and who's named on it, and fix any form that's out of date.

Common questions

Can I leave my 401(k) to my children instead of my spouse?

Only with your spouse's written consent, signed after the wedding and witnessed by a notary or plan representative. Without it, the plan has to pay your spouse. An IRA doesn't need that consent, except possibly in a community property state.

Do I need a living trust?

Many families don't. Beneficiary forms, TOD and POD registrations and joint ownership already keep most of what they own out of probate. A trust earns its cost when you have property in several states, want money held for children past 18, or want privacy or control a will can't give.

Will my heirs pay tax on what they inherit?

Usually not on the inheritance itself, unless your state has an inheritance tax. They will pay income tax on withdrawals from inherited traditional retirement accounts, but inherited investments and homes get a step-up in basis, and life insurance is tax-free to them.

What happens if I die without a will?

State law decides who inherits what passes through probate, usually a spouse and children in fixed shares, and a court chooses a guardian for minor children. Accounts with beneficiaries and jointly owned property still pass as named or titled.

In short

  • Beneficiary forms and account titles, not the will, decide where most money goes. Keep them current and always name a contingent.
  • Your spouse has a federal right to your 401(k) unless they consent in writing; after a divorce, change the forms yourself.
  • Don't leave money directly to minors. Use a trust or a custodian, and name a guardian in a will.
  • For 2026 the federal estate tax applies only above $15 million per person, but some states tax much smaller estates.
  • A will, financial and health care powers of attorney, and up-to-date forms cover most families; see an estate attorney when things are complicated.
