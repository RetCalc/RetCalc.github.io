Long-term care is help with everyday living, such as bathing, dressing or eating, for months or years, because of age, illness or dementia. Most people who reach 65 will need some of it, a smaller group will need a lot, and Medicare pays for very little of it. It's one of the largest costs a retirement plan can face, and one of the hardest to plan for, because nobody knows in advance which group they'll be in.

What long-term care is

Long-term care means ongoing help, rather than medical treatment, with the activities of daily living: bathing, dressing, eating, using the toilet, continence and moving from a bed to a chair. It also includes supervision for someone with dementia, who may be physically able to do these things but can't safely be left alone. Insurance policies and some public programs use the same list, and usually require that you need help with two of the six, or have a severe cognitive impairment, before they pay.

Most of it happens at home, and most of it is unpaid, given by a spouse, a daughter or son, or other family. When it's paid for, it can come in several forms:

  • home care, from a few hours a week of help with chores to round-the-clock aides,
  • adult day programs, which provide care and company during working hours,
  • assisted living, a private apartment with meals, help with daily activities and staff on hand,
  • a nursing home, with 24-hour nursing care, for people who need the most help.

How likely you are to need it

The federal Administration for Community Living, part of the Department of Health and Human Services, puts the odds this way for someone turning 65 today.

The averages hide a very uneven spread. About a third of people never need long-term care. Many need it for a few months, often after a hospital stay. And about 20% need it for more than five years. Women need care for longer on average than men, 3.7 years against 2.2, partly because they tend to live longer and are more often widowed by the time they need it.

A 2021 HHS study by Richard Johnson of the Urban Institute and colleagues came to a similar conclusion from another angle: more than half of adults turning 65 will develop a disability serious enough to need long-term care and will use some paid care.

That uneven spread is what makes this a planning problem. The average cost is manageable for many retirees. The worst cases, years in a nursing home, can use up a large estate.

What it costs

A widely used source for prices is the Cost of Care Survey by CareScout, a Genworth company, which collects rates from providers across the country each year. The 2025 survey, released in March 2026, found these national medians.

What those figures mean depends on how long care lasts:

  • A year and a half of part-time help at home, 20 hours per week at $35 per hour, costs about $55,000.
  • Two years in assisted living cost about $149,000.
  • Three years in a private nursing home room cost about $389,000.

These are today's prices. Care is mostly labor, so its price follows wages, which over long periods have risen faster than prices in general. In a plan that runs in today's dollars, it's sensible to assume care costs grow at least with inflation. Rates also vary enormously by place: the same survey finds assisted living and nursing homes cost far more in some states than in others.

Who pays for it

Many people assume Medicare covers long-term care. It mostly doesn't.

How Medicaid pays for care

Medicaid is the safety net for long-term care, and for many people it's what pays in the end. It's means-tested: you qualify only when your income and countable assets are low. The rules differ by state, but the federal framework is the same everywhere.

Assets and income

For a single person, the asset limit is often around $2,000, plus some exempt items. Your home is usually exempt while you or a spouse live in it, as long as your equity is under the state's limit, which for 2026 is at least $752,000 and in some states up to $1,130,000. A car, personal belongings and a small burial fund are usually exempt too. Most of your income, including Social Security and pensions, goes toward the cost of care, apart from a small personal allowance set by the state.

Protecting a spouse at home

When one spouse needs nursing home care and the other stays at home, federal rules stop the one at home from being impoverished. The community spouse can keep a share of the couple's assets, between $32,532 and $162,660 in 2026 depending on the state and the couple's assets, plus the home. They can also keep enough of the couple's income to bring their own up to a minimum, as much as $4,066.50 per month in 2026.

The five-year look-back

Medicaid looks back 60 months from the date you apply. Money or property given away in that time, or sold for less than it's worth, triggers a period during which Medicaid won't pay for nursing home care. Giving assets to children a year before needing care doesn't work. Planning more than five years ahead, for example with certain irrevocable trusts, can, and that's work for an elder-law attorney in your state.

Estate recovery

After a Medicaid recipient dies, the state must try to recover what it spent on long-term care for anyone 55 or older from their estate, which often means the house. Recovery is postponed while a spouse or a young or disabled child is still alive, and states have hardship rules.

Medicaid also limits choice. Not every nursing home takes it, and home and community care is often limited or has a waiting list. That's one reason people with savings try to pay privately for at least the first part of their care.

Long-term care insurance

A traditional long-term care policy pays a set amount, say $200 per day or $6,000 per month, once you need help with two of the six daily activities or have a severe cognitive impairment. Each policy has a few dials:

  • the benefit amount, per day or per month,
  • the benefit period, such as two, three or five years, or a pool of money,
  • the waiting period, often 90 days, during which you pay yourself,
  • inflation protection, which raises the benefit each year, for a higher premium.

Policies are medically underwritten, so you have to buy while you're healthy. The share of applicants turned down rises steeply with age, which is why most buyers are in their fifties or early sixties.

The biggest problem has been premiums. Insurers priced early policies assuming more people would drop them and fewer would claim, and when that proved wrong, they raised premiums on existing policyholders, some by large amounts several times over. A policy's premium isn't guaranteed, and someone who can no longer afford it may have to cut the benefit or drop the coverage after years of paying. Ask any insurer for its history of increases.

Tax treatment

Premiums for a tax-qualified policy count as medical expenses up to a limit that rises with age: for 2026, $4,960 per person at 61 to 70 and $6,200 at 71 and older. You can pay them from an HSA tax free up to the same limits. Benefits from a tax-qualified policy are generally tax free. Most states also run Partnership programs: buy a qualifying policy, and each dollar it pays out is a dollar of assets you can keep and still qualify for Medicaid later.

Hybrid policies

A hybrid policy combines life insurance or an annuity with a long-term care benefit. You pay a lump sum or a fixed number of premiums, often guaranteed not to rise. If you need care, it pays a multiple of what you put in; if you don't, your heirs get a death benefit. The trade-offs are a larger payment up front and, for the same premium, less care coverage than a traditional policy. You can move money from an existing life insurance policy or annuity into one without tax, through a 1035 exchange.

State programs

Washington State is the first to run its own long-term care insurance. Workers there pay 0.58% of wages into the WA Cares Fund, and from July 2026 people who qualify can draw a lifetime benefit of up to $36,500, adjusted for inflation. Other states have studied similar programs.

Paying for it yourself

For many people with substantial savings, the plan is to pay for care from their own money if it's needed. That works best as a deliberate plan:

  • Decide on a reserve. One common approach is to set aside the cost of two to three years of care, in today's dollars, and not counting it as spendable. With the 2025 medians, that's roughly $150,000 to $390,000 per person.
  • Count the house. Home equity is often the largest source for care in later life, through a sale when moving into assisted living, or a reverse mortgage to pay for care at home. The lesson on your home in retirement covers the options.
  • Think about the healthy spouse. In a couple, the risk is that one spouse's care uses up money the other will need to live on for years afterwards.

Spending on care also tends to come late, often in the last years of life, while retirees' other spending tends to fall in their late seventies and eighties, so the two partly offset. The lesson on how spending changes with age looks at that pattern.

Seeing it in your own plan

The simplest way to test the risk is to add it. In the Drawdown Simulator, add a future expense, such as $75,000 per year for three years starting at age 85, and see whether your savings still last through every market since 1926. Then try it starting at 80 and lasting five years, the bad case. If the plan survives both, you can probably self-fund. If it doesn't, you know the size of the gap that insurance, a home sale or Medicaid would need to cover.

For a couple, run it for each spouse in turn: care for the first can leave the second with a smaller portfolio for a long time.

Common questions

Does Medicare pay for a nursing home?

Only for short stays after a hospitalization of at least three days, for skilled nursing or rehab, up to 100 days, with a $217 daily copay from day 21 in 2026. It doesn't pay for custodial care, help with daily living, which is what most nursing home residents receive.

Can I give my house to my children to qualify for Medicaid?

Not within five years of applying, without triggering a penalty period. There are some exceptions, for example transfers to a spouse or to a child who lived in the home and provided care for two years. Rules vary by state, so talk to an elder-law attorney first.

When is the best time to buy long-term care insurance?

Most people who buy do so in their fifties or early sixties, while they're healthy enough to qualify and premiums are lower. Waiting raises both the premium and the chance of being turned down.

Is long-term care insurance worth it?

It depends on what you're protecting. People with little savings will likely end up on Medicaid, and the very wealthy can usually pay for care. Insurance does the most for people in between, who could pay for a few years but would be badly hurt by a long stay, especially if a spouse depends on the same savings.
