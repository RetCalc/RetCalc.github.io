Children are the largest cost most households take on by choice, and the most expensive years tend to land just when retirement saving would do the most good. Knowing roughly what each stage costs, and which tax breaks help, makes it possible to plan for both instead of putting retirement on hold.

What a child costs

The most detailed US estimate comes from the Department of Agriculture. Its last study, Expenditures on Children by Families, 2015, found that a middle-income married couple with two children would spend $233,610 raising a child from birth through age 17, in 2015 dollars, or between $12,350 and $13,900 per year depending on the child's age. Brought forward to 2026 prices with the consumer price index, that's about $332,000.

Housing was the biggest share, 29% of the total, since children mean more bedrooms and often a move to a pricier area. Food came next at 18%, then childcare and education at 16% for families who paid for it. The study also found that families with one child spent 27% more on that child than each child cost in a two-child family, and families with three or more children spent 24% less per child. Children share bedrooms, clothes and cars.

These are averages for middle-income families, and your own figures can easily differ by half in either direction. Costs scale with income and with where you live; families in high-cost cities and families who pay for private school spend well beyond them. Use them as a starting point, then replace them with your own numbers once you have them.

Everyday costs creep up as children grow: teenagers eat more, drive and cost more in activities. Paid childcare is the big jump. For the first five or six years, a family paying for care in a suburb spends about $2,300 per month on one child, more than in any later stretch. Once school starts, the childcare bill largely ends, and that freed-up money is one of the best chances to restart or raise retirement saving.

With more than one child the stretches overlap. The chart follows a family with two children three years apart, both in paid childcare in a suburb, with the second counted at 20% less. Costs peak at $4,140 per month in the three years both are in care, fall to $3,240 when the older one starts school and to about $2,500 when both are in school. Over the 22 years until the younger turns 19, they add up to about $704,000 in today's dollars, before college.

Tax breaks that help

Several federal tax rules cut the cost of children, and the 2025 law known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act changed most of them.

The child tax credit

The child tax credit is $2,200 per child under 17 for 2026. A credit comes straight off the tax you owe, so two children cut a family's federal income tax by $4,400. Families who owe less tax than the credit can get up to $1,700 per child of it paid to them as a refund; that refundable part builds up at 15 cents for each dollar earned above $2,500. The credit shrinks by $50 for every $1,000 of income above $200,000 for a single filer and $400,000 on a joint return. The child needs a Social Security number, and so does the parent claiming the credit (one parent, on a joint return). The amounts now rise with inflation: for 2027 the IRS has set the credit at $2,300, with up to $1,800 refundable.

Two breaks for childcare, and how they interact

A dependent care FSA through your employer lets you pay for childcare with pre-tax money. The limit rose from $5,000 to $7,500 per household from 2026, or $3,750 if married filing separately, though each employer decides whether its plan allows the higher amount. The money escapes federal income tax and payroll tax, so at a 22% tax bracket it saves more than $2,000 per year. You choose the amount once per year at enrollment, and money you don't spend on care is generally lost.

The child and dependent care credit covers part of up to $3,000 of care costs for one child or $6,000 for two or more. The care has to be for a child under 13 so that you can work, or both of you on a joint return. From 2026 the credit is 50% of those costs at the lowest incomes. It falls to 35% once income passes $43,000 and stays there for most families, then falls again above $75,000 for a single filer and $150,000 on a joint return, reaching 20% above $103,000 and $206,000.

The same dollar of care can't get both breaks. Every dollar that goes through the FSA comes off the $3,000 or $6,000 the credit can count, so a family that puts $7,500 through an FSA has nothing left for the credit. Which one is worth more depends on your tax bracket:

Roughly, the credit wins in the 12% bracket and the FSA wins from the 22% bracket up. A family with one child has only $3,000 of costs the credit can count, which tips things further toward the FSA. One side effect is worth knowing: pay that goes into the FSA isn't counted as Social Security earnings, which trims a future benefit very slightly.

Trump accounts

The same law created a new account for children, the Trump account. It's a kind of IRA opened for a child under 18 who has a Social Security number. For children who are US citizens born from 2025 through 2028, the Treasury puts in $1,000 if a parent or guardian asks for it on IRS Form 4547. Since July 4, 2026, parents, relatives and others can add up to $5,000 per year between them. An employer can put in up to $2,500 per year for each employee (that's per employee, however many children they have), and it counts toward the $5,000.

The money has to be invested in low-cost funds that track an index of US stocks, and it can't be taken out before the year the child turns 18. After that the account follows the rules of a traditional IRA. Family contributions aren't deductible, so they come back out tax-free, but the growth, the Treasury's $1,000 and any employer money are taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn, with the usual 10% penalty before 59½ unless an exception applies.

The free $1,000 is worth claiming for any child who qualifies. Whether to add your own money is a closer call. For college, a 529 plan's growth comes out tax-free, which a Trump account's doesn't. And for a teenager with a job, a Roth IRA's growth is tax-free in retirement. The saving for college lesson compares the college accounts.

What children do to a retirement plan

Their timing makes child costs harder still. They peak when parents are in their late twenties to early forties, which is when retirement saving has the longest to grow. A dollar saved at 32 has 33 years to compound before 65; a dollar saved at 52 has 13.

Take a hypothetical couple, Priya and Marcus, both 32. They save $1,500 per month for retirement and plan to stop at 65. At 4.5% per year after inflation, that grows to about $1.34 million in today's dollars. Then their first child arrives, and they weigh what to do with their saving through the childcare years.

Six years without saving at the start cost about $405,000 at 65, nearly a third of the total, even though those years supplied only 18% of the deposits. To make it up, they'd need to save about $650 per month more, on top of the $1,500, every month from 38 to 65. Keeping even $500 per month going cuts the damage by a third, and if that $500 is what it takes to get an employer match, the real saving is larger still.

Parents shouldn't skimp on childcare to protect a retirement account. But early saving is unusually valuable, so it's worth looking hard for ways to keep some of it going, and worth deciding now when it will go back up.

Ways to keep saving through it

Protect the match first

If your employer matches contributions, saving at least enough to get the full match is the highest-return dollar you have. Dropping below it gives away part of your pay. The employer match lesson explains how matches work.

Decide which saving gives way first

If saving has to drop, it matters which saving. Not every dollar you put away is worth the same. Money going into an ordinary brokerage account gets no tax break on the way in, so it's the cheapest to pause. 401(k) contributions above the match, IRA contributions and HSA contributions each give up a tax break when they stop. The part of your 401(k) contribution that earns the match is the last to touch. Working down that order, and stopping as soon as the child's costs are covered, keeps the most valuable saving going for longest.

The other common approach is to trim a share from every account. It keeps the habit alive everywhere and can feel simpler, but it usually gives up some tax break or match that cutting in order would have kept. Whichever rule you pick, it's easier to choose it once than to renegotiate every time a daycare bill rises.

Count what a child replaces

Not every dollar of a child's cost is new money. Some replaces spending that stops: fewer restaurant meals, less travel, a commute that changes during parental leave. Building a new budget from your actual spending after the child arrives, rather than adding the full average on top of the old one, usually shows a smaller gap. Budget is built for this.

Plan the step back up

Childcare ends, or nearly ends, when school starts. Decide now that most of that money goes back into saving, and set the date. Automatic increases in your 401(k), such as one point of pay each year, do this without anyone having to remember. Raises work the same way: sending half of each raise to saving keeps take-home pay rising while saving catches up.

Use the tax-favored accounts

A dependent care FSA and the care credit cut the cost of childcare itself. If one parent stops working or goes part-time, a spousal IRA lets the working spouse contribute for both, up to $7,500 each for 2026, so the parent at home keeps building savings. It's worth knowing, too, that Social Security bases your benefit on your 35 highest-earning years, so a few years at home often lowers it less than people expect. The Social Security lesson shows how.

Protect the plan while children depend on you

Children make income protection matter more. Term life insurance covering the years until the youngest is grown, and disability insurance, often through work, keep the plan intact if a parent's income stops.

Later costs: college and grown children

The costs above run through age 18 and leave college out, because families handle it so differently. Some pay all of it, some part, some none. The saving for college lesson covers how much to save and why retirement usually comes first: students can borrow for college, and nobody lends you money for retirement.

Support often doesn't end at 18 or 22. Many parents help grown children with rent, phone plans, a car or a wedding. If you expect to, it's worth putting a figure in the plan, even a rough one, so it comes out of a budget instead of out of retirement savings later.

Seeing it in your own plan

The Guide lets you add the children you have, by age, and any you're planning, with when the first might arrive and how far apart. You choose whether you'll pay for childcare and what kind of place you live in, and it uses the costs above, lowering saving in the years they apply. You can say how that cut is shared between your accounts: ordinary investment saving first, a share from each, or an order of your own. Then it shows how your saving runs year by year and what that does to the balance at retirement.

Try it two ways: once with saving paused during the childcare years, and once with a smaller amount kept going. Then look at the year childcare ends and see what raising saving at that point does to the result.

Common questions

Is the USDA figure still right if it's from 2015?

It's the latest such study the USDA has published, and the method holds up: it measures what families actually spend. Prices have risen since, which is why the figures here are brought forward with inflation. Childcare prices have often risen faster than prices overall, so check local rates.

Should we stop retirement saving while paying for daycare?

If it's that or fall behind on bills, yes, for a while. But early years are the most valuable ones to save in, so keeping at least the employer match going, and planning when to raise saving again, makes a large difference later. In the example, keeping $500 per month going saved about $135,000 at 65 compared with stopping.

Should we save for our children's college or our retirement first?

Retirement, at least up to the full employer match. Loans, grants and scholarships exist for college; nothing like them exists for retirement. Once retirement saving is on track, a 529 plan is an efficient next step.

Do children change how much we'll need in retirement?

Usually not much once they're grown. Most of their costs end before you retire. Help to grown children, or to grandchildren, can continue, though, and it's worth a line in the plan if you expect it.
