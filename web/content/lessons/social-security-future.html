You've probably heard that Social Security is running out of money. It isn't, quite: what's running out is a reserve fund, and when it does, the program can still pay most of what it owes from the taxes coming in. The size of that gap, the years it arrives and what Congress did the last time are all public, and they're enough to allow for a cut in your own plan without assuming the worst.

How the money flows

Social Security is mostly pay-as-you-go. Today's workers and employers pay 12.4% of wages, 6.2% each, up to the wage base ($184,500 in 2026), and the self-employed pay both halves. That money goes almost straight out as today's benefits. A smaller amount comes from income tax that higher-income retirees pay on their benefits.

When taxes coming in exceed benefits going out, the surplus is lent to the federal government in exchange for special Treasury bonds, held in two trust funds: the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) fund, which pays retirement and survivor benefits, and the Disability Insurance (DI) fund. The bonds earn interest and are backed by the government like any other Treasury debt. From the 1980s into the 2010s the program ran large surpluses, mostly because of the baby boom generation's working years, and the funds built up to about $2.9 trillion.

Now the boomers are retired, and the flow has reversed. Benefits exceed what comes in, and the difference is made up by cashing in those bonds. At the end of 2025 the combined reserves were $2.56 trillion, down $160 billion in the year.

What the 2026 Trustees Report says

Every year the program's trustees, a board that includes the Secretaries of the Treasury, Labor, and Health and Human Services and the Commissioner of Social Security, publish a 75-year projection. The 2026 report came out on June 9, 2026.

The headline date you'll often see is 2034, for the two funds combined. That's unchanged from the 2025 report. The retirement fund on its own is in worse shape, running out in the last quarter of 2032, a little earlier than the 2033 date in last year's report.

Over the full 75 years, the trustees put the shortfall at 4.42% of taxable payroll, up from 3.82% in the 2025 report. According to an analysis by the Center for Retirement Research, most of that change came from lower assumed birth rates (the trustees cut their long-run fertility assumption from 1.90 to 1.75 children per woman), lower immigration and the 2025 tax law, whose new deduction for people over 65 reduces the income tax collected on benefits, part of which goes to the trust funds.

What "running out" would mean

The trust funds running dry doesn't stop the checks. Payroll taxes keep coming in, and they'd cover most of the scheduled benefits. What the law doesn't allow is paying more than the funds hold, so when the reserves are gone, Social Security could only pay out what arrives. The law doesn't spell out how, but the trustees' projections, and most analysts, assume every benefit would be cut by the same percentage at once, whatever your age or when you claimed: about 17% for everyone in 2034 if the funds are combined, or about 22% for retirees and survivors in late 2032 if they aren't.

The payable share then keeps slipping, because the number of people collecting grows faster than the number paying in.

The reason is demographic. In 1960 there were about five workers paying in for each person collecting. Today there are fewer than three, and the 2026 report projects about 2.3 by 2035. People live longer, families are smaller, and the baby boom generation is moving through retirement. A larger share of all pay also now sits above the wage base, where it isn't taxed.

This has happened before

In the early 1980s, the retirement fund was within months of being unable to pay benefits on time. President Reagan and Congress set up a bipartisan National Commission on Social Security Reform, chaired by Alan Greenspan, and its January 1983 report became the Social Security Amendments of 1983, signed on April 20, 1983. They:

  • raised full retirement age from 65 to 67, phased in over two decades for people born from 1938 onward,
  • began taxing up to half of benefits for higher-income retirees, with the tax going to the trust funds,
  • delayed that year's cost-of-living raise by six months, from July to January,
  • brought newly hired federal employees into the system, and
  • moved scheduled payroll tax increases earlier.

The fix spread the pain. Workers paid more and would retire later; current retirees lost half a year of raises and some paid tax on their benefits. Most of it phased in slowly, so few people's plans changed overnight. The 1977 amendments had done something similar on a smaller scale, raising payroll taxes and correcting a flaw in how benefits were indexed.

Since then, the projected depletion date has moved around but not much. Every Trustees Report since 2012 has put the combined date somewhere from 2033 to 2035. What's changed is that it's no longer far off.

What a fix would take

The gap is large but well understood, and every fix is some mix of more money in and less money out. The Congressional Research Service put the size of the 2026 shortfall in two simple ways.

Proposals that have been put forward over the years include:

  • raising the wage base, or taxing pay above it, so higher earners pay on more of their income,
  • raising the payroll tax rate a little at a time,
  • raising full retirement age further, or tying it to life expectancy,
  • slowing benefit growth for higher earners by changing the bend-point formula,
  • using a different inflation measure for cost-of-living raises, which would make each raise slightly smaller,
  • investing part of the reserves in stocks, or adding money from general tax revenue.

Each has supporters and opponents. What history suggests is that fixes tend to come late, often close to the deadline, and tend to protect people already retired or close to it from the largest changes, though nothing guarantees it will happen that way again.

Planning for a cut

Nobody knows what Congress will do. You can still make a plan that holds up whether it does a lot or nothing.

How much to allow for

The current-law outcome is a cut of about 17% to 22% starting in the early 2030s. One cautious approach is to plan on 75% to 80% of your statement's figure and treat anything more as a bonus. That's roughly what incoming taxes alone could pay in the 2030s, so it rests on current law rather than a guess about politics. For someone young, the later figures, 62% to 65% by 2100, are the cautious ones.

How much it matters depends on when you'll collect. Someone already retired might see a cut for the later part of retirement; someone in their thirties would collect entirely in the years after depletion, though also with the most time for a fix to phase in.

What a cut would and wouldn't change

A cut of that kind would hit every check by the same percentage, so it wouldn't change which claiming age gives the most over your life. Claiming early to "get yours before the cut" doesn't work: an early claim is permanently smaller, and it would be cut by the same share as a later one. The reasoning in when to claim holds either way.

What a cut would change is how much of your spending your savings have to cover. Every $1,000 per year less from Social Security means $1,000 more drawn from savings, every year, with inflation. At a 4% withdrawal rate, replacing $5,000 per year takes about $125,000 more in savings. That's the figure to check: whether your plan still works if the guaranteed part shrinks.

Two things soften a cut. Taxes: less Social Security usually means slightly less income tax, and a smaller taxable share. And Social Security still rises with inflation, so even a reduced benefit is a kind of income you can't easily buy anywhere else.

Seeing it in your own plan

Run your plan twice: once with your full estimated benefit, once with 75% to 80% of it. In the Drawdown Simulator, choose I know my benefit under Social Security and enter the reduced figure, then compare how often the money lasts through every market since 1926. In the Social Security Calculator, you can enter a lower figure in place of your statement's benefit and see how your claiming ages and break-even points move. If the reduced version still works, a cut is something you'd notice but not something that breaks the plan. If it doesn't, you know how much extra saving or spending flexibility it would take.

Common questions

Will Social Security go bankrupt?

No, not in the sense of disappearing. Payroll taxes keep coming in as long as people work. Even with no change in law, the 2026 Trustees Report projects the program could pay 83% of scheduled benefits in 2034 and about 65% by 2100.

Didn't the government spend the trust fund?

The surpluses were lent to the Treasury, which spent them like any other borrowing, and the funds hold Treasury bonds in return. Those bonds are legal obligations of the government, and the program is redeeming them now. The question is what happens once they're used up, not whether they'll be honored.

Should I claim early in case benefits are cut?

The cut the trustees project would be the same percentage for every check, whenever it started, so claiming early just locks in a smaller check that would then be cut too.

Would people already retired be cut?

If Congress did nothing, yes, most likely by the same share as everyone else. In practice, past reforms have mostly protected current retirees from the largest changes, though the 1983 law did delay a raise and start taxing some benefits.

What about Medicare?

Medicare's hospital insurance fund, which pays for Part A, is projected to run short in 2033, after which it could pay 89% of costs. Parts B and D are paid by premiums and general tax revenue each year, so they can't run out the same way, but their cost to the budget keeps rising. See Medicare.

  • After that, incoming taxes would still pay 78% to 83% of scheduled benefits, falling to about 62% to 65% by 2100, if Congress did nothing.
  • Congress faced the same problem in 1983 and fixed it with a mix of higher taxes, a later retirement age and taxing benefits.
