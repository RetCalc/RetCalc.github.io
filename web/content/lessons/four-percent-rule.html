The 4% rule says you can take 4% of your savings in your first year of retirement, raise that dollar amount with inflation every year after, and expect the money to last 30 years. It gets quoted everywhere, often loosely. Knowing what it was built on tells you when it fits your plan and when it doesn't.

What the rule says

The rule sets one number, your first year's withdrawal: 4% of what you have on the day you retire. On $1,000,000, that's $40,000. After that, the percentage is never used again. Each year you take last year's dollar amount plus that year's inflation, whatever the markets have done. If prices rise 3%, next year's withdrawal is $41,200. If they rise 4% the year after, it's $42,848.

That one has its own lesson, Spending a percentage. When people say "the 4% rule", they almost always mean the version in the first columns: a fixed amount, rising with prices.

Your withdrawal rate is the first year's withdrawal divided by your savings. A plan that takes $48,000 from $1.2 million starts at 4%; one that takes $60,000 from the same $1.2 million starts at 5%. It's the single number most of the research on retirement spending is written in.

Where 4% came from

In October 1994 the financial planner William Bengen published "Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data" in the Journal of Financial Planning. Planners at the time often took the long-run average return, subtracted inflation, and treated what was left as safe to spend. Bengen showed why that fails: a retiree who meets a bad market in the first few years can run out long before the averages say they should.

Instead, he took a portfolio of half large US stocks and half intermediate-term Treasury notes, rebalanced every year, started a retirement on January 1 of each year from 1926 onward, withdrew a fixed amount rising with inflation, and counted how many years each one lasted. He assumed the money sat in tax-deferred accounts, so taxes didn't enter into it.

A 4% first-year withdrawal never ran out in fewer than 33 years, and most starts lasted 50 years or more. At 4.25%, the worst start ran out in 28 years. The hardest retirements began in the late 1960s and early 1970s, when high inflation pushed withdrawals up while stocks went nowhere. Bengen also found that between 50% and 75% in stocks worked best: less than half in stocks shortened the worst cases and left less behind, and much more than three quarters made the worst starts worse.

Four years later, in 1998, three professors at Trinity University in Texas, Philip Cooley, Carl Hubbard and Daniel Walz, published what became known as the Trinity study. They tested many withdrawal rates, mixes and lengths against the record from 1926 to 1995 and reported the share of starts in which the money lasted, a measure now called the success rate. A 4% withdrawal rising with inflation lasted 30 years in 95% of starts with all stocks, 98% with 75% stocks, 95% with half stocks, 71% with a quarter and 20% with all bonds. Between them, the two papers made 4% the standard starting point. You can read more about what a success rate does and doesn't promise in What "chance of success" means.

What the full record shows

The site's own test runs the same rule through every 30-year retirement that began in a January from 1927 to 1996, 70 in all, with 60% in the S&P 500 and 40% in 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced yearly, no fees, all in today's dollars. Take $40,000 from $1,000,000 and raise it with inflation:

  • The money lasted 30 years in 66 of the 70 starts, 94.3%.
  • The four that ran out began in 1965, 1966, 1968 and 1969. Retiring in 1966, the money was gone in year 27.
  • The highest first-year rate that lasted from every start was 3.79%, set by 1966.
  • The typical start could have taken far more: the middle start's highest safe rate was 6.63%.
  • In 48 of the 70 starts, the retiree finished with more than the $1,000,000 they began with, after inflation. The middle outcome was about $1.6 million.

The chart shows the highest rate each start year could have taken and still lasted 30 years.

The 4% rule is a worst-case number. It's sized for the handful of starts around 1966, and nearly everyone else could have spent much more. The bad starts also cluster: they're the retirements that began just before a long stretch of high inflation and flat stock prices. Retiring in 1966, the portfolio lost about 1.7% per year after inflation over its first decade. By year 10 it was down to about $460,000 in today's dollars, and the same $40,000 was 8.7% of it. Over all 30 years the portfolio still averaged about 4.3% per year after inflation, which would have been plenty in a different order. Sequence-of-returns risk covers why.

The site's worst case, 3.79%, is a little under Bengen's. His bonds were intermediate-term Treasuries; the record here uses 10-year Treasuries, which lose more when interest rates rise, as they did through the 1970s. Testing a retirement starting every month instead of every January, 835 starts in all, gives 94.0% for 4%, close to the yearly figure.

What the rule assumes

Every part of the rule's setup moves the answer. Before you lean on 4%, check how many of these match your plan.

Thirty years

Bengen took 30 years as the least a portfolio should last, enough to carry someone retiring around 60 into their 90s. Retire at 50 and the money may need to last 45 years or more, and the safe rate falls. On the same 60/40 mix, the highest rate that lasted from every start was 4.10% over 25 years, 3.79% over 30, 3.52% over 40 and 3.36% over 50. Retire at 75 with 20 years to fund, and it rises to 4.62%.

A mix with plenty of stocks

The rule needs growth to keep up with rising withdrawals. Over 30 years, the safe rate was 3.79% with 60% in stocks and 3.83% with 75%, but 3.69% with 40%, 3.43% with 20% and only 2.42% with no stocks at all. Bonds feel safer, but inflation eats a portfolio that can't grow, and the 1940s and 1970s were brutal for bonds after inflation. Very high stock shares don't help much either: at 100% stocks the worst case was 3.69%. How much in stocks goes further.

Spending that never adjusts

The rule assumes you raise spending with inflation every single year, through every crash, and never cut. Few retirees behave that way, and that's the main reason it's conservative for most people. If you'd trim spending after a bad year, you can usually start higher. Fixed or flexible spending shows how much that's worth.

No fees

The classic tests ran on index returns with no investment costs. Fees come straight off the return every year, and over a long retirement they add up. A 0.25% fund cost lowers the safe rate to 3.68%; 0.5% takes it to 3.57%; a 1% advisory fee takes it to 3.36%, and the 4% rule's success rate falls from 94.3% to 88.6%. Fees and index funds has more on what costs do.

Before taxes, and from the portfolio only

The 4% is what comes out of the account, not what you get to spend. Withdrawals from a traditional 401(k) or IRA are taxed as income, so $40,000 out might be $35,000 or less after tax, depending on your bracket. And the rule says nothing about Social Security or a pension. Those cover part of your spending, so the withdrawal that matters is only the gap they leave.

One country's history

The test uses the US record, and the US had one of the best-performing stock markets of the 20th century. In 2010 the researcher Wade Pfau ran the same test on 17 developed countries over 109 years and found that 4% would have held up in only 4 of them, even with generous assumptions. US history may well repeat, but it's one good draw among many.

Is 4% too high or too low?

The case for less

Stocks are expensive against their earnings. The CAPE ratio, the S&P 500's price over its last ten years of earnings after inflation, stands at 41.0, higher than at the start of any retirement with 30 years of history after it. Historically, expensive starts have tended to allow lower withdrawals, though the link is loose; Starting valuations and CAPE covers it. Morningstar's retirement researchers, using their own forecasts of future returns rather than history, put the safe starting rate at 3.9% for people retiring in 2026, for a 90% chance of lasting 30 years. Their figure for the year before was 3.7%. A long retirement, high fees or a bond-heavy mix all push the number lower still.

The case for more

The 4% rule is set by the worst start in a century. In the typical start, a retiree could have taken 6.63% and still lasted 30 years, and following the rule most people die with more than they retired with. Bengen himself has moved up: in his 2025 book A Richer Retirement he puts the figure at 4.7%, largely because he now spreads the portfolio across more asset classes, including smaller-company and international stocks. Flexible spending raises it further. In the site's tests, guardrails that cut spending 10% after bad stretches started at 5% and lasted in every historical retirement, at the cost of deep cuts in the worst ones. And if you retire later, a shorter horizon allows more.

The 4% rule answers a narrow question: the most a never-adjusting retiree could have spent over 30 years in the worst US start. Your answer depends on how far your plan is from that setup.

Using it in a plan

The rule is most useful in two places: setting a savings target before you retire, and checking a spending plan once you're close.

Turned around, 4% gives the familiar shortcut of 25 times spending. If you need $40,000 per year from savings, 25 × $40,000 is $1,000,000. Note "from savings": if Social Security will pay $28,000 of a $68,000 budget, the portfolio only has to cover $40,000. From spending to a target works through it.

Close to retiring, work out your own withdrawal rate the same way: your first year's spending from savings, after Social Security and any pension, divided by your savings. Somewhere around 4% or under, for a 30-year retirement, has held up through all of US history with a balanced mix. Well above it, the plan depends on markets being kinder than they were in the 1960s, or on your willingness to cut.

A few things people get wrong once they're retired:

  • Resetting to 4% of a lower balance after a crash. That's switching strategies at the worst moment, and it cuts your income more than the rule ever asked.
  • Never adjusting after a long boom. A retiree who started in 1982 had more than five times their starting balance by year 30, after inflation. One way to share in that is the ratchet the planner Michael Kitces proposed in 2015: raise spending 10% whenever the portfolio is 50% above where it started, at most once every three years. In the site's test it gave 44 of the 70 starts at least one raise and didn't add a single failure.
  • Forgetting big one-off costs. A new roof or a car every ten years isn't in "spending raised with inflation" unless you put it there.

Common questions

Is it 4% of my balance every year?

No. It's 4% of the starting balance in year one only. After that the dollar amount rises with inflation, whatever the portfolio does. Taking 4% of each year's balance is a different strategy, which can't run out but whose income swings with markets.

Does the 4% include Social Security?

No. It's for the portfolio alone. Social Security and pensions cover part of your spending, so subtract them first and apply the rate to what's left.

What rate for an early retirement?

Over 50 years with a 60/40 mix, the highest rate that lasted from every start since 1926 was 3.36%, and 3.5% lasted in 94% of them. Many early retirees plan on 3.25% to 3.5%, or start higher with rules for cutting back. The FIRE lesson goes into it.

If markets fall the year I retire, should I spend less?

The rule as written says no: keep raising with inflation. But the retirements that failed were the ones that met a bad first decade and kept spending as planned. If you can trim a little after a large fall, you lower the risk that matters most. That's the idea behind guardrails.

Is the 4% rule dead?

Over 30 years with a 60/40 mix, it lasted through every US start since 1926 except four in the late 1960s, and those ran out between years 27 and 30. What's changed is the starting point: high stock valuations and a longer record of other countries suggest less margin than the US past implies. Treat 4% as a reasonable first estimate, then test your own plan.

In short

  • The 4% rule sets year one's withdrawal at 4% of savings, then raises that dollar amount with inflation every year.
  • It comes from Bengen's 1994 study and the 1998 Trinity study: the most a retiree could have spent, without ever adjusting, through the worst 30-year US start.
  • In the site's test with a 60/40 mix, it lasted in 66 of 70 starts since 1926; most retirees following it ended with more than they started with.
  • Longer retirements, fees, a bond-heavy mix and high valuations argue for less; flexibility, a shorter horizon and a broader mix argue for more.
  • Apply it to the gap Social Security leaves, before taxes, then test your own plan.
