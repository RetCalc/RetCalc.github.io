A retirement plan needs an end date, and nobody knows theirs. Planning to your life expectancy sounds sensible, but it means planning for a retirement that about half of people outlive. This lesson looks at what the actuarial tables say, how the odds change for couples, and why most plans, RetCalc's included, run to 95.

Life expectancy is an average

The Social Security Administration's actuaries publish a life table each year: for every age, the chance of dying within the year and the average number of years left. The latest, for 2023, used in the 2026 Trustees Report, gives a 65-year-old man an average of 18.1 more years and a 65-year-old woman 20.7. So the average man who reaches 65 lives to about 83, and the average woman to about 86.

An average describes a spread. Some 65-year-olds die within a few years; others live past 100. Early deaths pull the average down, so the middle of the spread, the age by which half have died, is a little later than the average: about 84 for men and about 87 for women. If you plan to your life expectancy, you're planning for a retirement that slightly more than half of people outlive.

Period and cohort tables

There's a second reason the average understates. The table above is a period table: it assumes today's death rates at every age stay as they are for the rest of your life. In practice they have kept falling, so the SSA also publishes cohort figures that build in projected improvements. For people turning 65 around now, the Trustees' cohort figures run roughly one year longer than the period table's. The figures below use the period table, which gives the shorter answer, so treat them as a floor.

The finish line moves as you age

Life expectancy is worked out for people who have already reached a given age, so it rises as you get older. Surviving each year removes one year of risk, and the expected age at death moves later.

Someone who plans at 55 to an average age of 81 and is still healthy at 75 finds the average has moved to 86. A plan revisited every few years has to keep pushing its end date out, so it makes sense to set one generously at the start.

The odds of reaching each age

The more useful question for a plan is how likely you are to reach a given age. Here's what the SSA's table implies for someone who has already reached 65.

Two things in the table matter for planning. A 65-year-old woman has better than a one-in-three chance of reaching 90. And for a couple, the chance that at least one of them reaches 90 is about even, and about one in five that one reaches 95.

Couples plan for the longer life

A couple's savings have to last until the second death, not the first, so the plan has to reach the later of two lifetimes. That's why the couple column runs so much further to the right than either person alone. The chance that both of a 65-year-old couple are alive at 90 is 8%, but the chance that one of them is, and still needs an income, is 51%.

The survivor's years usually cost less than the couple's did, but not half as much: housing, utilities and a car cost much the same for one person as for two. Income falls further than costs. When one spouse dies, the household keeps only the larger of the two Social Security benefits, and the smaller one stops; a pension may drop too, depending on the option chosen at retirement. That's one reason the higher earner's claiming age matters so much for couples, since it sets the survivor's benefit. Spouses and survivors covers how it works.

Age gaps stretch the horizon further. If one spouse is five years younger, the plan has to last until the younger one's late 80s or 90s, which may be 30 or 35 years after the older one retires.

Your own odds are not the average

The SSA's table describes the whole population, and individuals differ a great deal from it. Health and habits matter most: smoking, weight, chronic illness and family history all move the odds. So do income and education. In a 2016 study of tax and Social Security records published in JAMA, Raj Chetty and colleagues found that at age 40, the richest 1% of Americans could expect to live 14.6 years longer than the poorest 1% for men, and 10.1 years longer for women.

People who reach retirement with substantial savings tend to sit toward the longer-lived end of the population, so for them the averages above are, if anything, too short. The Actuaries Longevity Illustrator, built by the American Academy of Actuaries and the Society of Actuaries, asks about your age, sex, health and smoking and shows the odds of reaching each age, for you and a spouse.

How the numbers have changed

Retirements have lengthened a great deal since Social Security began paying monthly benefits in 1940. Life expectancy at 65 has risen by about six years for men and more than seven for women, with a dip during the COVID-19 pandemic that has since reversed.

The Trustees expect gains to continue slowly: about 1.5 more years at 65 between 2026 and 2050. For a 40-year-old today, that's a reason to treat the current tables as a lower bound on how long retirement might last.

What a longer horizon costs

Longevity risk is the risk of living longer than your money. The obvious defense is to plan for a long life, and the natural worry is that doing so means saving far more. The historical record suggests the cost is smaller than it sounds.

Here's how a fixed withdrawal, raised each year with inflation, did across every US retirement start since 1926 for a 60% stock, 40% bond portfolio, for retirements of different lengths. The safe maximum is the highest first-year rate that lasted the full length from every start.

Going from 30 years to 40 lowers the safe rate from 3.79% to 3.52%, which raises the savings needed by about 8%. Each extra five years costs less than the last, because money set aside for the far end of retirement has decades to grow before it's spent. Planning too short is the more expensive mistake: running out at 88 can't be fixed by having had a little extra at 70.

That asymmetry is why most planners use a planning age well past life expectancy, typically 95 and sometimes 100. RetCalc's Retirement Readiness Guide tests whether the money lasts to 95, or to the younger spouse's 95, and the Dashboard's chance of success also runs to 95. By the SSA's period table, a 65-year-old man has about an 8% chance of outliving a plan that runs to 95, a woman about 14% and a couple about 21%, before counting the extra years that better health and income usually add.

Other ways to protect against a long life

Planning to a late age isn't the only defense, and the others can let you plan with less.

  • Delaying Social Security. Each year you wait past full retirement age, up to 70, raises the benefit by 8%, for life and adjusted for inflation. It pays off most in exactly the case you're worried about, a long life. When to claim works through the trade-off.
  • An income annuity. A single-premium immediate annuity turns a lump sum into payments for life, so the insurer carries the risk of you living long. A deferred version bought at 65 and starting at 80 or 85 covers only the far end, for much less. Annuities and pensions covers the costs and catches.
  • Flexible spending. Strategies that adjust withdrawals to the portfolio, like guardrails or spending a percentage of the balance, can't run out on a fixed schedule; they spend less in bad stretches instead. Fixed or flexible spending compares them.
  • Keeping the house as a reserve. Home equity isn't spending money, but it can pay for care late in life through a sale or a reverse mortgage.

Common questions

Isn't planning to 95 too pessimistic?

Planning to 95 is a decision about how much margin the money needs, and it says nothing about how long you expect to live. The cost of planning long is modest: going from 30 years to 40 raises the savings needed by about 8%. The cost of planning short is running out at an age when you can't go back to work. If you reach 85 with plenty left, you can spend more then.

Why do the SSA figures differ from other life expectancy numbers I've seen?

Different tables answer different questions. Figures from birth are much lower than figures at 65, because they include deaths at younger ages. Period tables use one year's death rates; cohort tables project improvements and run longer. And insurers' annuity tables run longer still, because people who buy annuities tend to be healthier.

My parents died in their 70s. Should I plan shorter?

Family history is one factor among many, and it doesn't guarantee a short life. If you plan shorter, you're betting the plan on it. A safer use of the information is to plan to a normal horizon and spend more freely early on, when you can be sure of enjoying it.

What if I'm retiring at 55?

Then 95 means 40 years, and the safe withdrawal rate for 40-year historical retirements was 3.52%, not 3.79%. Early retirees also face more years before Social Security and Medicare, which raises what savings must cover at first. FIRE covers planning for long retirements.

In short

  • At 65, the SSA's tables give men about 18 more years and women about 21, but those are averages and about half of people live longer.
  • A 65-year-old couple has about a 51% chance that one of them reaches 90, and 21% that one reaches 95.
  • Healthier, wealthier and better-educated people tend to live longer than the population tables show.
  • Planning to 95 instead of 85 costs less than it sounds, because the far years have time to grow; running out early can't be undone.
  • Delaying Social Security, a lifetime annuity and flexible spending all hedge a long life.
