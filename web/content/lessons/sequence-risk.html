Two retirees can earn the same average return over thirty years and end up in very different places. One runs out of money in their eighties; the other dies with more than they started with. The difference is the order the returns arrive in, and it matters most in the first years of retirement.

Why withdrawals make the order matter

If you invest a lump sum and never add to it or take from it, the order of returns makes no difference at all. Each year's return multiplies the balance, and multiplying gives the same answer in any order. Three years of −20%, +10% and +20% turn $100,000 into $105,600 whichever way round they come.

Take money out each year and that stops being true. The table below starts with the same $100,000 and the same three returns, but $5,000 comes out at the start of each year, the way a retiree's spending would.

With the bad year first, the second and third withdrawals come out of a balance that has already shrunk. Each $5,000 is a bigger share of what's left, and the dollars sold after the fall are no longer invested when prices recover. With the good year first, the withdrawals come out of a growing balance and the fall arrives once there's more cushion to absorb it. After three years the gap is $4,200; over a thirty-year retirement, the same effect can decide whether the money lasts.

Thirty years of withdrawals

The chart follows one retirement three ways. Each starts with $1.48 million and takes out $62,000 per year, raised with inflation, so it spends the same in today's dollars every year. All three earn exactly the same average return over the thirty years, 3.7% per year after inflation; only the order changes.

With the five weak years first, the money runs out in year 21. With the same five years at the end, there's more left after thirty years than there was at the start. The dotted line, which earns the average every single year, ends in between. It's what a simple calculator with one fixed growth rate shows you, and it describes neither of the real outcomes.

So a plan tested against one average return can look safer than it is, because it never sees a bad stretch arrive early.

When the risk is highest

Sequence risk isn't the same at every age. Early in a career, a crash hits a small balance, and every contribution for years afterwards buys shares at lower prices; a bad decade in your thirties can even help. Late in retirement, a crash hits a balance that only needs to last a few more years. The danger is greatest in between.

The most exposed years are the last few before you stop working and the first ten or so after. Your balance is the largest it will ever be, so a given percentage fall costs the most dollars, and you're about to start, or have just started, selling to pay for living. Some planners call this stretch the retirement red zone.

Bad years just before retirement count too. A large fall the year before you leave work means retiring on a smaller balance or working longer. Several of the defenses below therefore begin a few years before the retirement date.

What history shows

When William Bengen tested withdrawal rates against every retirement start year in the US record for his 1994 paper, the source of the 4% rule, the worst cases came from the years with the worst starts rather than the lowest thirty-year returns.

In 2008, the planner and researcher Michael Kitces looked at every thirty-year retirement since the 1870s and found that the real return over the first fifteen years explained most of the difference in how much each retiree could safely spend. What happened in the second half mattered far less, because by then the balance was smaller and fewer years were left to pay for.

For your own plan, that means the early years of retirement are where flexibility does the most good.

Ways to manage it

You can't choose the order your returns arrive in, but you can make a plan less sensitive to it. None of the approaches below needs you to predict the market, and most can be combined.

Be ready to spend less after a fall

Take out less in the years after a large fall. Every dollar you don't sell at a low price is still invested for the recovery. The cut doesn't need to be large or long. Guardrail rules, first published by Jonathan Guyton and William Klinger in 2006, trim spending by 10% when withdrawals have grown well above their starting share of the balance, and raise it again after strong years.

This only works if you can actually cut. If your spending is mostly fixed costs, a plan that depends on cutting 10% in a bad year is a plan that depends on something you can't do. It helps to know which parts of your budget could flex: travel, gifts, a new car pushed back a year.

Hold a few years of spending outside stocks

Money in bonds or cash can pay for living after a fall, so you aren't forced to sell stocks at their lows. Some retirees keep two to five years of spending this way and refill it after good years. The cost is lower expected growth in the good years, and too much cash slowly loses ground to inflation.

Hold less in stocks around the retirement date

Since the risk peaks around the retirement date, one approach is to hold the least in stocks right then and raise the share over the following years. Research by Michael Kitces and Wade Pfau, published in 2014, found that starting retirement with less in stocks and adding a little each year could cut both the chance of running out and how far short the money fell when it did. In their simulations, a mix that started at 30% stocks and rose to 60% did better than one held at 60% the whole way. It feels backwards, holding more stocks at 80 than at 65, but it puts the most stocks in place after the dangerous years have passed.

Cover part of your spending with income markets can't touch

Every dollar of spending covered by Social Security, a pension or an annuity is a dollar you don't have to withdraw. Smaller withdrawals mean less to sell in a bad year. Waiting to claim Social Security raises the benefit by 8% for each year past full retirement age, up to 70, and it rises with inflation for life.

Delaying Social Security has a cost here. It usually means drawing more from savings in the first years to bridge the gap, and those are exactly the riskiest years. In return you get a larger, inflation-adjusted income for the rest of your life, which protects against a bad sequence later and against living a long time. Whether it's worth it depends on your health, your other income and how much you've saved.

Keep some flexibility in the date

Working one more year does three things at once: another year of saving, another year of growth, and one less year of withdrawals. Part-time work in the first years of retirement has a similar effect, since even modest earnings shrink the withdrawals that matter most. If markets fall sharply just before your planned date, it helps a great deal to be able to wait.

What doesn't help

  • Selling after a fall to wait it out turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss and usually means missing the first, largest part of the recovery.
  • Assuming a lower average return makes the plan more cautious overall, but it still assumes the same return every year, so it doesn't show the risk this lesson is about.
  • Picking a retirement date by guessing the market doesn't work, because nobody knows the next decade's returns. Starting valuations give some hint, which is the subject of the next lesson, but not a forecast you can time.

Seeing it in your own plan

The Drawdown Simulator runs your plan through every stretch of market history since 1926, starting in each year in turn. Each start year is a different sequence. Your chance of success is the share of those starts in which the money lasted, and the gap between the best and worst starts is sequence risk, measured on your own numbers.

To see how much a defense helps, change one thing and run it again. Switch your withdrawals to guardrails, lower your stock share in the first years, or try retiring a year later, and compare the worst starts before and after. Watch the worst starts, since that's where these changes show most.

Common questions

Isn't this just volatility?

Related, but not the same. Volatility is how much returns swing from year to year. Sequence risk is what those swings do once money is coming out. A volatile portfolio that nobody withdraws from ends in the same place whatever the order.

I'm 20 years from retiring. Does this matter yet?

It matters less for your balance today than for decisions you can make now. It's easier to build flexibility in now, in how you'd cut spending, how you'd hold some money outside stocks and how firm your date is, than to find it in a bad year.

Does an annuity remove the risk?

For the part of your spending it covers, largely yes: the insurer takes on the market risk and pays the same amount whatever happens. The costs are giving up access to that money and, unless you pay extra for it, protection from inflation.

Is 4% safe, then?

It held up through the worst sequences in US history over thirty years, which is what it was built to do. Whether it suits you depends on how long your retirement could last, how much you hold in stocks and how flexible your spending is. The 4% rule lesson covers it in detail.

In short

  • Without withdrawals, the order of returns doesn't matter. With them, it can decide whether the money lasts.
  • The risk is highest in the few years before retiring and the first decade after.
  • Historically, the worst retirements came from bad starts, not low long-run averages.
  • Flexible spending, a buffer outside stocks, steady income and some give in the date all make a plan less sensitive to a bad start.
