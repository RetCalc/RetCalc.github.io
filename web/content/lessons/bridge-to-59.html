Most retirement savings sit in 401(k)s and IRAs, and the tax code charges an extra 10% on most withdrawals before age 59½. Retire at 50 and you have nine and a half years to pay for some other way. How you do it depends on what you hold outside those accounts and on a few rules that open them early, and it works best laid out year by year.

The 10% penalty

The penalty is officially an additional tax on early distributions, and it comes from section 72(t) of the tax code. It applies to withdrawals before 59½ from traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s and similar plans, and it's charged on the part of the withdrawal that counts as taxable income. You pay it on top of the ordinary income tax, which you owe anyway.

Take a single person, 52 and retired, who withdraws $40,000 from a traditional IRA in 2026 with no other income. Income tax on it, after the $16,100 standard deduction, is about $2,620. The penalty adds $4,000. Together that's $6,620 of federal tax on $40,000, a rate of about 17%, where the same withdrawal at 60 would cost $2,620.

A few details decide what the penalty touches:

  • It applies from the date you reach 59½, not the year. Someone born on March 10, 1977 reaches 59½ on September 10, 2036.
  • For a couple, each person's own age counts for their own accounts. An older spouse's IRA opens first.
  • Only taxable money is penalized. What you contributed to a Roth IRA comes back out with no tax and no penalty at any age.
  • A SIMPLE IRA charges 25% instead of 10% in the first two years after you join the plan.
  • A governmental 457(b), the plan many state and local government workers have, has no 10% penalty at all, except on money rolled into it from another kind of plan or IRA.

The exceptions, and which ones can carry you

The tax code lists many exceptions to the penalty. Most are for one-off events. Only a few can pay for years of living, and those are the ones an early retirement is built on. Some apply only to employer plans like a 401(k), some only to IRAs, so where the money sits matters.

The newer exceptions from SECURE 2.0 help in an emergency, but they're small and capped. For the years between retiring and 59½, the realistic options are the rule of 55, 72(t) payments, and money that was never subject to the penalty in the first place.

The rule of 55

If you leave your job in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) or 403(b) have no 10% penalty. You don't have to be 55 on the day you leave. Leave in February of the year you turn 55 in November and it still applies. Nor do you have to stop working altogether: a new job elsewhere doesn't undo it.

The details catch people out:

  • It covers only the plan of the employer you're leaving. A 401(k) from a job you left at 48 doesn't qualify. If your current plan accepts roll-ins, moving an old 401(k) into it before you leave brings that money under the rule too.
  • It doesn't apply to IRAs. Roll the 401(k) into an IRA after you leave and the money loses the exception until 59½, which is easy to do by accident when you consolidate accounts.
  • Plans set their own withdrawal rules. Some allow only a full payout or a few withdrawals per year; check before you rely on it.
  • A withdrawal paid to you from a 401(k) usually has 20% withheld for federal income tax. If that's more than you owe, you get the difference back when you file.

For public-safety workers the age is lower. State and local police, firefighters, emergency medical workers and corrections officers in a governmental plan qualify from age 50, or after 25 years of service under the plan if that comes first. The same applies to certain federal workers, including federal law enforcement officers, federal firefighters, customs and border protection officers and air traffic controllers, and, since SECURE 2.0, to private-sector firefighters.

Money you can reach at any age

Before using any exception, look at the money the penalty never touches. For most early retirees, this is what pays for the first years.

The taxable account

A regular brokerage account has no age rules at all. When you sell, only the gain is income; the part that's your original investment, your cost basis, comes back untaxed. Long-term gains are taxed at 0% while your taxable income, gains included, stays under $98,900 for a married couple in 2026 ($49,450 single). A couple selling $60,000 of shares that are half gain has $30,000 of income from it. After the $32,200 standard deduction, with no other income, they owe no federal income tax at all.

That makes the taxable account the cheapest money most early retirees have, and the one whose size decides how much freedom the rest of the plan has. People planning to retire early often keep saving into it alongside their 401(k), even though it has no tax break on the way in.

Roth contributions

Roth IRA withdrawals come out in a fixed order: your regular contributions first, then converted amounts, oldest first, then earnings. Because contributions come first and were already taxed, you can take out everything you've contributed, at any age, with no tax and no penalty. Someone who put $7,000 per year into a Roth IRA for ten years has about $70,000 they can reach today, whatever the account is worth now.

A Roth 401(k) is different. An early withdrawal from it comes out partly as contributions and partly as earnings, in proportion, and the earnings are taxed and penalized. Rolling it into a Roth IRA after you leave the job generally lets the contributions count as Roth IRA contributions, which you can then take first.

An HSA's old receipts

A health savings account can repay you, tax-free, for medical bills you paid out of pocket in any earlier year since the account was opened, as long as you kept the receipts. Someone who paid for care from other money for years while the HSA stayed invested can draw on those receipts in early retirement. The HSA lesson covers the rules.

Routes into the pre-tax money

If the money above won't last until 59½, there are three ways to reach a traditional IRA or 401(k) early.

  • 72(t) payments: take a fixed yearly payment, set by an IRS formula, for five years or until 59½, whichever is later. No penalty, but changing the payment before then brings the penalty back on every payment so far. It pays from the first year, which is its advantage. The 72(t) lesson covers the three methods and how to size a plan.
  • A Roth conversion ladder: each year, convert about a year's spending from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA and pay income tax on it. Five years later that conversion can come out with no penalty. It's flexible, and in low-income years the tax can be small, but you need five years of other money to live on while the first conversions wait. The conversion ladder lesson walks through it.
  • Paying the penalty: for a gap of a year or two, 10% can cost less than committing to years of fixed payments. Taking $20,000 for one year before 59½ costs $2,000 in penalty.

Which money pays for which years

A bridge plan works best as a timeline: which source pays for each year, and what you do with the other accounts in the meantime. Here's a hypothetical example. Mia and Theo are both 52 and stop working this year. They spend $60,000 per year, with taxes and health premiums included. They have $30,000 in cash, $250,000 in a brokerage account (about half of it gain), $80,000 in Roth IRAs, $50,000 of it their own contributions, and $900,000 in traditional 401(k)s and IRAs.

Cash, the brokerage account and part of the Roth contributions cover the first five years, which is exactly the wait the first conversion needs. Each conversion of $60,000 costs them about $2,840 of federal income tax at 2026 rates. They keep $30,000 of Roth contributions in reserve, and the $720,000 or so left in traditional accounts after three conversions is untouched until 59½.

The weak spot is their income in the conversion years. It's the $60,000 converted plus the gain on the shares they sell, about $30,000, so roughly $90,000. For 2026, marketplace premium credits stop above $84,600 for a household of two, and for a couple of 52 to 54 in a state with typical premiums, losing them would cost about $14,500 to $16,600 per year. Converting a little less, selling shares with less gain, or spending more of the cash in those years keeps them under the line. The conversion ladder lesson goes into how to size each year.

Other households would build it differently. Someone leaving a job at 55 with a large 401(k) might use the rule of 55 and nothing else. Someone with almost everything in an IRA and little in a brokerage account might start a 72(t) plan on part of the IRA to pay for the first years, and convert alongside it.

Common questions

Does the rule of 55 work if I'm laid off?

Yes. It depends on leaving that employer in or after the year you turn 55, for any reason. If you leave at 54 and turn 55 the next year, it doesn't apply to that plan.

Should I roll my 401(k) into an IRA when I retire early?

An IRA usually has more investment choices, and it makes it easy to split off an account for 72(t) payments or to convert part of it to a Roth. But rolling over gives up the rule of 55 for that money, if you'd have qualified. Some people roll over part and leave the rest in the plan.

Can I take out Roth IRA earnings early?

Earnings come out last, after all contributions and conversions. Before 59½ they're usually both taxed and penalized, so most plans leave them alone until then.

Is the 10% penalty ever worth paying?

Sometimes. For a short gap or a one-time need, 10% on a small amount can cost less than a ladder's taxes or a 72(t) plan's lock-in. The Bridge includes paying the penalty as one of its routes so you can compare the cost.

In short

  • Most withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts before 59½ carry a 10% penalty on top of income tax.
  • Only a few routes into pre-tax money can carry you for years: the rule of 55, 72(t) payments, a governmental 457(b) and a Roth conversion ladder.
  • A taxable account, cash and Roth IRA contributions can be used at any age and are usually the cheapest money to spend first.
  • A conversion ladder needs five years of other money before the first conversion can be spent.
