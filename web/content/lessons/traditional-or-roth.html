Almost every retirement account comes in two versions. With a traditional account, you skip the income tax on what you put in and pay it on what you take out. With a Roth, you pay the tax now and everything comes out tax-free later. Which one leaves you with more depends mostly on one comparison: your tax rate today against your tax rate when you withdraw.

The same money, taxed at a different time

Take $10,000 of pay. Put it in a traditional 401(k) and all $10,000 is invested, because it comes off your taxable income. Put it in a Roth 401(k) and you pay income tax on it first; in the 22% bracket, that leaves $7,800 to invest.

Now let both grow for thirty years at 5% per year after inflation. The traditional account grows to $43,219 and the Roth to $33,711. But the traditional account still owes tax. If you withdraw it at 22%, you keep $33,711, exactly what the Roth holds.

The order of the two steps makes no difference: multiplying by the growth and then taking 22% off gives the same answer as taking 22% off and then multiplying by the growth. When your tax rate is the same at both ends, the two accounts come out level. Everything that follows is about when, and why, the rates differ.

Set the two rates yourself. When they match, the bars match; the further apart they are, the more the choice is worth.

If your rate in retirement is lower than today, traditional wins: at 12%, it leaves $38,033 against the Roth's $33,711, about 13% more. If your rate is higher, Roth wins. The gap is the difference in rates, applied to the whole balance.

Your rate now and your rate later

The rate now is your top rate

A traditional contribution comes off the top of your income, so the tax it saves is at your marginal rate, the rate on your last dollars. For someone single earning $100,000 in 2026, that's 22% federal, plus state income tax in most states. A $10,000 traditional 401(k) contribution saves about $2,200 of federal tax. It doesn't save Social Security or Medicare tax, which apply to 401(k) contributions either way.

The rate later is often lower than people expect

In retirement, traditional withdrawals stack up from zero. The first dollars each year are covered by the standard deduction and come out tax-free; the next are taxed at 10%, then 12%. So the average rate on all your withdrawals is lower than the rate on the last dollar.

A married couple, both over 65, with $60,000 of traditional IRA withdrawals and $48,000 of Social Security pays $5,804 of federal income tax for 2026. That's helped by the larger standard deduction at 65 and the temporary senior deduction for 2025 through 2028. Their top bracket is 12%, and the whole bill comes to under 10% of the IRA withdrawals.

The next dollar can cost more than the bracket suggests. For that couple, another $1,000 from the IRA would also make $800 more of their Social Security taxable, so it would cost $216 in federal tax, an effective 21.6%, though their bracket says 12%. Past that point 85% of their benefit is taxable, the most there can be, and the rate drops back to 12%. How retirement income is taxed explains why.

The average line is what the couple pays on the withdrawals as a whole: even at $100,000 per year, it's under 11%. For the next contribution, the rate that matters is the one on the withdrawals it will eventually pay for, which sit on top of everything else you'll have coming in. If this couple's other savings already produce $50,000 per year of withdrawals, a further contribution today comes out later at 22.2% or 12%, and the 0% and 10% layers are already used up.

What usually decides it

For most people, the comparison comes down to where they are in their career and how much they'll have saved.

  • Early in a career, in the 10% or 12% bracket, the tax saved by a traditional contribution is small, and income will probably be higher later. Roth usually wins.
  • In peak earning years, at 24% or above, the tax saved is large, and most people won't withdraw enough in retirement to be taxed at that rate. Traditional usually wins.
  • In the 22% bracket, it's close, and the details below tip it.

What tips the balance

Toward Roth

  • Large savings or a pension. Big traditional balances bring big required minimum distributions from 73 or 75, which can push you into a higher bracket than you're in now. A pension does the same, since it fills the low brackets first.
  • Ending up single. After one spouse dies, the survivor usually files as single from the year after the death (a survivor with a dependent child at home keeps the joint brackets for two more years), with brackets about half as wide, on not much less income. The same withdrawals then cost more tax.
  • Income-based costs in retirement. Roth withdrawals don't count as income for the taxation of Social Security, Medicare's IRMAA surcharges or ACA premium credits. Traditional withdrawals count for all three.
  • Heirs. Most non-spouse heirs must empty an inherited account within ten years. Traditional money is taxed at their rate, often in their own peak earning years; Roth money passes tax-free.
  • Moving to a higher-tax state after you retire, which would tax withdrawals more than your current state taxes your pay.

Toward traditional

  • A gap before Social Security. If you retire at 60 and claim Social Security at 70, the years in between can have very little taxable income. Those are good years to move traditional money into a Roth at 10% or 12%, which is often cheaper than paying 22% or 24% on a Roth contribution today. Roth conversions covers how.
  • Moving to a lower-tax state, or living in one that exempts retirement income. Illinois, for example, taxes pay at 4.95% but doesn't tax 401(k) or IRA withdrawals at all.
  • Income-tested benefits now. A traditional contribution lowers your adjusted gross income, which can raise ACA premium credits, the Saver's Credit or other income-tested breaks this year.
  • Modest expected retirement income, mostly covered by Social Security, so withdrawals land in the 0%, 10% and 12% layers.

Future tax law can't be known. The 2017 tax law lowered the brackets through 2025, and a 2025 law made those rates permanent, meaning they no longer have an end date. Congress can still change them. Nobody knows the rates of 2050, which is one reason many people hold some of each.

A Roth holds more under the same limit

The limits are set in dollars, not in after-tax value. For 2026, you can put $24,500 into a 401(k), traditional or Roth, and $24,500 in a Roth is worth more, because the tax on it is already paid.

If the traditional saver invests the $5,390 of tax they saved, that money, in a taxable account, would close most of the gap. In practice it usually gets spent. So for anyone who already saves the maximum, Roth contributions put more money in the tax-free column. It's another reason people who fill their 401(k) often lean Roth even in the 22% or 24% bracket.

Having some of each

You don't have to choose once. Many plans let you split contributions between traditional and Roth in any proportion, and you can change the split each year as your income changes. Some people put everything into traditional in high-income years and everything into Roth in low ones. Most workplace plans now offer the choice: 98% of the plans Vanguard administered had a Roth option at the end of 2025, partly because of the new catch-up rule below, yet only 18% of participants in those plans used it.

Holding both kinds of money in retirement gives you a choice each year. You can take traditional withdrawals up to the top of a low bracket and pay for anything above that from the Roth, keeping your taxable income below a line you care about: a bracket, an IRMAA threshold or the ACA subsidy cliff. A taxable brokerage account adds a third option, since only the gain on what you sell is taxed. Planners call this tax diversification.

Roth 401(k) or Roth IRA

Both are Roth accounts, taxed the same way on the way out, but the rules around them differ.

The Roth 401(k) is the only way to make large Roth contributions at a high income, since it has no income limit. The Roth IRA is the more flexible of the two: you can take your contributions back out at any time, which makes it a reasonable second line behind an emergency fund. IRAs covers its rules in detail.

One rule change affects people 50 and over with higher pay. From 2026, if your wages from your employer were over $150,000 in 2025, any catch-up contributions you make to that employer's plan must go in as Roth. The wages that count are the Social Security wages on your W-2 from that one employer, and the threshold rises with inflation each year. Your regular contributions up to $24,500 can still be traditional. If the plan has no Roth option, people over the threshold can't make catch-up contributions at all, which is one reason nearly every plan now has one. This year's limits has the details.

Seeing it in your own plan

Income Tax shows both sides of the trade. In normal mode, enter your pay and add a traditional 401(k) or IRA contribution under pre-tax deductions: the tool shows how much tax it saves this year and your marginal rate, the rate a traditional contribution saves. In retirement mode, enter the withdrawals and Social Security you expect and it shows the tax on each kind of income and the rate on the next dollar, the rate the same money would pay coming out.

If the rate in retirement comes out lower than today's, traditional contributions are probably saving you tax. If it's close or higher, Roth contributions, or Roth conversions in the low-income years before Social Security, deserve a look; the Roth Conversions tool tests a conversion schedule against doing nothing. Common questions

Does choosing Roth reduce my take-home pay?

Yes, at the same contribution rate. A 6% Roth contribution comes out of pay after tax, so take-home drops by the full 6%, while a 6% traditional contribution drops it by less. To keep take-home pay the same, a Roth contribution has to be smaller, which is the trade the worked example above shows.

Is Roth worth it if I'm close to retiring?

It can be. Roth money has a five-year clock for tax-free earnings (one clock for all your Roth IRAs, a separate one for each workplace plan), but for most people over 59½ that's the only wait, and your contributions themselves are never taxed again. What matters more is your rate now against your rate in retirement, which near retirement you can estimate quite well.

What if I can't decide?

Splitting is a reasonable answer when the rates are close. Half and half means that whichever way tax rates go, half your money was in the right place.

Do state taxes change the answer?

They can. A traditional contribution saves your state's tax now, and whether withdrawals are taxed later depends on the state where you retire. Pennsylvania doesn't let 401(k) contributions off its state tax at all, and Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Massachusetts don't let traditional IRA contributions off either. State taxes covers the rules.

In short

  • Traditional means tax later; Roth means tax now. At the same rate, they end up exactly level.
  • Compare your top rate today with the rate your withdrawals will pay, which is often lower for modest retirement incomes.
  • Large savings, a pension, ending up single or heirs in high brackets tip it toward Roth; peak earnings and low-income years ahead tip it toward traditional.
  • A Roth holds more after-tax money under the same contribution limit.
  • Holding some of each lets you control your taxable income in retirement.
