Every way of spending from savings makes the same trade. Keep your income perfectly steady and you accept some chance the money runs out; let your income move with the markets and the money can last, but some years you'll live on less. Where you sit between the two decides how much you can start with.

Two ways to get through a bad decade

A retirement can respond to a bad market in only two ways. Either spending stays the same and the portfolio absorbs the whole loss, or spending gives a little and the portfolio absorbs less. There's no third option that avoids both, unless some of your income doesn't depend on markets at all.

The clearest way to see it is to follow one hard retirement three ways. The chart starts with $1,000,000 in 60% US stocks and 40% bonds in January 1966, the start that set the 4% rule, and plots what each approach let you spend each year, in today's dollars.

The fixed withdrawal, the 4% rule, pays $40,000 every year for 26 years and then stops. Taking 4% of whatever the balance is each year never runs out, but by year 17 it's paying $16,718. Guardrails, a rule that starts higher and cuts 10% at a time when withdrawals grow too large against the balance, start at $50,000, cut ten times, and bottom at $17,434.

The flexible approaches spend more over a lifetime in the typical case, and they get there by letting the bad starts spend less.

Two different kinds of failure

A fixed plan fails in one obvious way: the money runs out. That's what a success rate measures, and it's why "94.3% of starts lasted" is a fair summary of the 4% rule.

A flexible plan almost never fails that way. Any rule that takes a share of the balance can't reach zero, and rules like guardrails cut before it gets close. So for flexible plans the success rate is close to useless: guardrails starting at 5% lasted in every one of the 70 starts, and so would much higher rates. The failure you're exposed to is different. It's a year, or a decade, of spending less than you can live on.

That's why the useful question for a flexible plan is how low spending went and for how long. In the 5% guardrails test, every one of the 70 starts cut spending at least once, and the typical start cut 4 times. 51 of the 70 spent less than the starting $50,000 at some point, and 37 dipped below $40,000, what the plain 4% rule would have paid every year.

The Drawdown Simulator handles this with a comfort line: the spending you'd hate to fall below. A fixed plan fails if the money runs out; a flexible plan fails if spending dips under the line. Measured that way, the two can be compared fairly.

How much flexibility is worth

Suppose you decide in advance how low you'd let spending go, and the plan holds that level as a hard minimum, cutting no further. How much higher could you start? Reading down the table:

  • The first bit of flexibility buys the most. Agreeing that spending might drop to $35,000, about 8% under the fixed amount, raises the safe start from $37,900 to $48,461, more than a quarter higher, and lifetime spending in the typical start rises by about 40%.
  • After that, returns shrink. Going from a $30,000 minimum to $20,000 raises the start by $15,000, but typical lifetime spending barely moves, from $1.69 million to $1.74 million. A lower floor mostly moves spending earlier: more in the first years, more risk of lean years later.
  • The wider the range, the more you spend early and the less you leave. Which you'd prefer depends on whether you want to spend more in your 60s or keep a cushion for your 80s and your heirs.

The same pattern holds for other flexible rules. Taking a fixed percentage of the balance, held to a $32,000 minimum, could start at $63,466. Vanguard's dynamic spending rule, which limits each year's change, could start at $49,411.

Floors and ceilings

Most flexible plans have two limits, and they do different jobs.

A floor is the least you'll spend in any year, however markets go. It should be set by what you can't do without: housing, food, insurance, utilities, taxes, the car. A plan with a floor can still run out of money, because holding the floor in a long bad stretch means selling more than the rule would. The table above shows the price: the higher the floor, the lower the start.

A ceiling is the most you'll spend, however well markets do. It stops a few strong years from setting a lifestyle that a later bad decade can't support, and keeps a cushion. The cost is spending less in good times than you could have.

Some rules limit the change from one year to the next instead of the level. Vanguard's dynamic spending lets spending rise at most 5% or fall at most 2.5% from one year to the next, after inflation. That makes income smooth, but a long decline can pull it down a little every year for a long time: retiring in 1966 with a 5% target, spending slid 2.5% every year, to $23,994 by year 30. Spending a percentage covers these rules.

Building a floor from guaranteed income

Retirement income planners often describe two schools of thought on this. The probability-based school spends from a diversified portfolio at a rate that history or simulations say is very likely to last, and adjusts if things go badly. The safety-first school covers essential spending with income that can't fall with markets, such as Social Security, a pension, an annuity or a ladder of inflation-protected Treasury bonds, and lets the portfolio pay for everything else. In practice most plans mix the two.

Guaranteed income makes flexibility much easier to live with. Suppose Sam and Jordan spend $80,000 per year, Social Security pays them $40,000, and the other $40,000 comes from a $1,000,000 portfolio. If a guardrail cuts the portfolio withdrawal by 10%, that's $4,000, which is 5% of what they actually spend. Two cuts in a row take about $7,600 out of an $80,000 budget, under 10%. The bigger the share of spending covered by income that doesn't move with markets, the smaller each cut feels.

Which spending can flex

A flexible plan only works if you can make the cuts it asks for. That depends less on your total budget than on how much of it is optional.

Split your spending into essentials and wants. Essentials are the bills that come whatever happens. Wants are travel, dining out, gifts, hobbies, a newer car, help for grown children. If you spend $80,000 per year and $56,000 of it is essential, the $24,000 of wants is your room to cut. A 10% cut in total spending, $8,000, means giving up a third of the wants. A 20% cut takes two thirds of them. The table above showed guardrails reaching cuts far deeper than that in the worst starts, which is why a floor matters.

A few other things change how flexible you really are:

  • Timing. In the worst starts, cuts came in the first decade, when you're youngest and most likely to want to travel. A plan that cuts in your late 60s costs more in enjoyment than one that cuts at 85.
  • Fixed costs that creep. Health care and insurance have tended to rise faster than general inflation, so the essential share of a budget often grows with age.
  • Spending that falls on its own. Many retirees spend less in real terms as they age, which gives a fixed plan some built-in slack. Spending changes with age covers what the research shows.
  • Other income. Part-time work, a pension or a later Social Security claim can replace a cut, at least for a while.

What researchers have found

Bengen's 1994 study, the source of the 4% rule, assumed spending never adjusts. The flexible rules that followed were built to relax that. In 2006 the planner Jonathan Guyton and William Klinger published decision rules, now called guardrails, and found that with them a retiree with at least 65% in stocks could start at 5.2% to 5.6% and keep the money for 40 years in 99% of simulated cases. The catch, which their paper says plainly, is that the higher start is paid for with cuts. Guardrails goes through the rules in detail.

Later work looked hard at the size of those cuts. Karsten Jeske of the Early Retirement Now blog showed in 2017 that guardrail rules can force cuts much deeper than people expect, more than half from the starting level in the worst historical starts. In 2024 Derek Tharp and Justin Fitzpatrick, writing on Kitces.com, made a similar case and argued for guardrails set by the odds of the plan lasting, which in their tests called for much smaller cuts in the same bad periods.

Taken together, the research says flexibility raises what you can spend, and the gain is largest for the first few percent of give. Flexibility without a floor can mean a long, deep cut in the worst decades, and a plan should be judged by the worst path you'd be willing to live through.

Choosing where you sit

The right point on the line depends on your situation. Some signs point one way or the other:

  • Lean toward fixed spending if most of your budget is essential and not covered by Social Security or a pension, if you'd find cuts very hard, or if you value a predictable income enough to start lower for it.
  • Lean toward flexible spending if guaranteed income covers your essentials, a large part of your budget is optional, you'd rather spend more in your early retirement years, or you can earn some income in a bad stretch.
  • Use a floor if you're flexible but have a level you won't go below. Set it from your essential budget, minus guaranteed income, and accept a lower start in return.

Many retirees end up with a mix: guaranteed income for the essentials, and a flexible rule on the portfolio for everything else. Choosing a strategy compares the specific rules, and Annuities and pensions covers building more of the floor from income.

Common questions

Isn't a flexible plan riskier than a fixed one?

It moves the risk. A fixed plan risks running out late in life; a flexible one risks spending less for a while. Historically, flexible plans with sensible starting rates almost never ran out, but in the worst starts their spending fell a long way.

Does a 10% cut really make much difference?

Yes, especially early. Money not withdrawn after a fall stays invested for the recovery. In the site's test, accepting a minimum about 8% under the fixed amount raised the safe start by more than a quarter.

What if I can't cut at all?

Then a fixed amount at a conservative rate is the honest plan, a little under 3.8% for 30 years with a balanced mix in the US record. You can also make more of your spending guaranteed, by delaying Social Security or buying an annuity, so less depends on the portfolio.

How do I pick a comfort line?

Start from your essential spending and subtract Social Security and any pension. What's left is the least the portfolio has to provide. Add a margin if you'd hate to live at exactly that level for years.
