Most retirees hold money in more than one kind of account: a traditional 401(k) or IRA, a Roth, a regular brokerage account. Each one is taxed differently when you take money out, so the order you draw on them decides how much of your savings goes to tax. With the same balances and the same spending, two orders can differ by tens of thousands of dollars over a retirement.

Three kinds of account, three kinds of tax

A dollar from a traditional account is the most expensive dollar you can spend, because all of it is income. A dollar from a brokerage account is often cheap: if you bought a fund for $60,000 and sell it for $100,000, only $40,000 is a gain, and for a married couple with taxable income under $98,900 in 2026, long-term gains are taxed at 0%.

The cost of a traditional dollar also depends on when you take it. Income tax is progressive, so the first dollars of income each year are taxed lightly or not at all, thanks to the standard deduction and the 10% and 12% brackets. A traditional withdrawal taken in a year with little other income can be taxed at almost nothing. The same withdrawal stacked on top of Social Security and a required distribution can be taxed at 22% or more.

The conventional order, and where it goes wrong

The usual advice is simple: spend the taxable account first, then the traditional account, and the Roth last. The logic is sound as far as it goes. Spending the taxable account first leaves the tax-deferred and tax-free accounts growing untouched for longer, and the Roth, which never creates taxable income, is the best account to leave to grow or to heirs.

The trouble is what it does to your taxable income over time. Picture a couple who retire at 65 and follow it. For the first few years they live on the brokerage account, so their taxable income is close to zero: the standard deduction and the low brackets go to waste. Once the brokerage money runs out, they start on the traditional account. At 70 Social Security starts, and up to 85% of it becomes taxable. At 75 required minimum distributions begin, from a traditional balance that has been growing untouched for ten years. Their income goes from almost nothing in their sixties to the highest it has been since they stopped working, in their eighties.

A tax system with rising brackets rewards the opposite: income spread as evenly as possible across the years, so more of it is taxed at the lowest rates.

Two orders, thirty years

Sam and Jordan are both 65, married, and retire this year in Florida, which has no state income tax. They have $1.2 million in traditional IRAs, $400,000 in a brokerage account (of which $160,000 is gain) and $100,000 in Roth IRAs. They spend $100,000 per year after tax, and they'll claim Social Security at 70, for $70,000 per year between them. Their investments earn 4% per year after inflation, and everything is in today's dollars.

The conventional order spends the brokerage account first, then the IRAs, then the Roth, taking RMDs from 75 and reinvesting any surplus. The second plan, bracket filling, takes enough from the IRAs every year to bring their taxable income to the top of the 12% bracket, $100,800 for a married couple in 2026. They spend what they need and move the rest into their Roth IRAs as a conversion. The brokerage account and the Roth cover anything more.

Following the conventional order, they pay no federal tax at all for four years, which feels like a win. But it leaves the IRAs growing, and from 75 the RMDs, on top of Social Security that is now 85% taxable, push their taxable income to about $75,000 at 75 and past $100,000 from 82 on. Bracket filling pays $11,600 in tax every year from 65 to 79, then almost nothing once the IRAs are empty.

Filling the 12% bracket cuts their lifetime federal tax by $95,872, more than a third. What's left at 95 looks about the same, but under the conventional order $846,676 of it is still in IRAs, and whoever inherits it will owe income tax on every dollar. Filling the 22% bracket instead goes too far for this couple: it pays 22% now on money that would have been taxed at 12% later, and ends with less.

The best line for someone else could be higher or lower. It depends on how large the traditional balance is compared with your spending, when Social Security starts, and your state. A bigger IRA can justify filling the 22% or even the 24% bracket; a smaller one may need little more than the standard deduction filled.

How Social Security bends the brackets

Social Security makes the tax on an extra traditional dollar higher than your bracket suggests. How much of your benefit is taxed depends on your "provisional income": your other income plus half your benefit. Above $25,000 for a single filer ($32,000 married), up to half of the benefit becomes taxable; above $34,000 ($44,000 married), up to 85%. These thresholds were set in 1983 and 1993 and have never been raised for inflation, so each year they catch more retirees.

In the range where benefits are being phased in, each extra $1 of IRA income makes up to $0.85 of Social Security taxable as well, so $1.85 of income gets taxed. In the 12% bracket that's a real rate of 22.2%. In the 22% bracket it's 40.7%. Planners call this the tax torpedo.

Traditional money taken out before Social Security starts misses the torpedo entirely, which is one reason bracket filling and delayed claiming work well together. Once benefits are 85% taxable, the extra cost stops, and a traditional dollar costs just the bracket rate again. Michael Kitces described the phase-in in 2013 as, in effect, a surtax that falls mostly on retirees with fairly modest incomes.

The moves that make up a withdrawal plan

Fill the low brackets with traditional money

In any year with room in a low bracket, take traditional money up to the top of it, whether you need to spend it or not. Whatever you don't spend can be converted to a Roth, where it grows tax-free and never creates an RMD. This works best in the gap years: after the paycheck stops, before Social Security and RMDs start. Roth conversions covers the mechanics, including the five-year rule.

Harvest gains at 0%

If your taxable income stays under the 0% line for long-term gains ($98,900 married, $49,450 single in 2026), you can sell investments at a gain, pay nothing, and buy them straight back. Your cost basis resets higher, so those gains are never taxed. This competes for the same room as bracket filling: traditional income fills the brackets first and gains stack on top, so a conversion that fills the 12% bracket uses up the whole 0% band. Couples with large unrealized gains and smaller IRAs often do better harvesting gains than converting.

Draw proportionally

Some firms suggest taking a share from each account every year, in proportion to its size. It spreads taxable income more evenly than the conventional order without needing a bracket target. In Fidelity's illustration, a 62-year-old drawing this way paid about $34,000 of tax over retirement instead of just over $56,000, and the money lasted about one more year. It's a reasonable rule of thumb, but it doesn't respond to your actual brackets the way bracket filling does.

Use the Roth for spikes

A new roof, a car or a big medical bill in one year can push income across a bracket line or a Medicare surcharge line. Taking that money from the Roth, or from cash, keeps the year's income where you planned it. That's one reason not to empty the Roth early, and one reason the Roth usually comes last.

Keep some traditional money for late in life

Large medical and long-term care costs late in life can be deductible as itemized medical expenses. A traditional IRA is a good account to pay them from, because the deduction can cancel much of the tax on the withdrawal.

Income lines to watch

Brackets aren't the only lines that matter. Several costs jump at particular incomes, and a withdrawal plan should usually stay just under them:

  • The ACA subsidy cliff, before 65. With the enhanced premium credits having ended after 2025, marketplace subsidies stop entirely above 400% of the poverty line. A big conversion before Medicare can cost thousands in premiums. See Health cover before 65.
  • IRMAA, from 65. Medicare charges more for Parts B and D once income passes $109,000 single or $218,000 married in 2026, based on your return two years earlier. One dollar over a line costs the whole surcharge for that tier. See IRMAA.
  • The senior deduction. From 2025 through 2028, each person 65 or older gets an extra deduction of up to $6,000, reduced by 6% of income over $75,000 single or $150,000 married.
  • The net investment income tax: 3.8% on investment income, including capital gains, above $200,000 single or $250,000 married.
  • State taxes. Some states don't tax Social Security or give retirees an exclusion for pension and IRA income. State taxes covers the differences.

What the research says

A widely cited study is "Tax-Efficient Withdrawal Strategies" by Kirsten Cook, William Meyer and William Reichenstein, published in the Financial Analysts Journal in 2015. They tested the conventional order against strategies that draw traditional money to fill low brackets and convert to Roth in low-income years. In their examples, the better strategies made a portfolio last more than three years longer than the conventional order. The reason is the one in the example above: the conventional order leaves the low brackets empty early and piles taxable income into the later years.

If you'd pay the same tax rate on every dollar in every year, as under a flat tax, it wouldn't matter whether you drew from a traditional account or a Roth first. Withdrawal order pays off only because rates differ between years. That's also why the gains are largest for people whose income would otherwise swing the most: early retirees with big traditional balances, and couples who delay Social Security.

Common questions

Should I always spend my taxable account first?

Often it makes sense to spend from it early, especially if the gains are small or fall in the 0% band. Spending only from it, while your traditional balance grows untouched, is what causes trouble later. Many good plans spend taxable money and fill a low bracket with traditional money in the same years.

Is it ever right to spend the Roth first?

Rarely as a rule. The main exceptions are a single high-income year, where Roth money keeps you under a bracket or IRMAA line, and the years before 65 when you're managing income for ACA subsidies.

What about leaving money to heirs?

Heirs pay income tax on inherited traditional money, usually within ten years. A Roth passes tax-free, and a taxable account gets a step-up in basis at death, so the gains up to then are never taxed. If your heirs earn a lot, converting at your lower rate can save the family tax. If they earn little, leaving traditional money can be cheaper.

Does this matter if all my savings are in a 401(k)?

Less, but still some. You can't choose between accounts, but you can choose how much traditional money to take each year, and whether to convert some to a Roth in the low-income years before Social Security and RMDs.

In short

  • Traditional withdrawals are taxed as income, Roth withdrawals aren't, and taxable accounts are taxed only on gains, often at 0%.
  • The conventional order wastes low brackets early and piles income into later years, when Social Security and RMDs arrive.
  • Filling a low bracket with traditional money each year, and converting what you don't spend, evens out taxable income.
  • While Social Security is being phased into tax, an extra IRA dollar can cost 22.2% in the 12% bracket and 40.7% in the 22%.
  • The ACA cliff, IRMAA lines and the senior deduction set limits on how much to take in any year.
