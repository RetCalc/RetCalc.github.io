"Financial advisor" isn't a license. People who use the title can be registered in three different ways, held to different legal standards and paid in ways that pull in different directions. This lesson explains who is who, how to look up a person's record, how advisors charge, what a 1% fee adds up to over a working life and a retirement, and what the money can buy that's hard to get on your own.

Who calls themselves an advisor

Almost everyone who gives financial advice for a living falls into one of three groups, and the group decides which regulator oversees them and what they owe you. The title on the business card won't tell you which one you're talking to.

The law spells it "investment adviser"; most of the industry writes "advisor". The spelling tells you nothing.

Many firms are registered as both a broker and an adviser, and their people can act as either depending on the account. The SEC calls them dual registrants. With one, ask which role the person is in for each account and each recommendation, because the standard changes with the role.

What each one owes you

An investment adviser is a fiduciary under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940. In an interpretation published in June 2019, the SEC described that duty as applying to the entire relationship between the adviser and the client. In practice it's usually described as two duties: care, meaning advice that suits your situation and goals, and loyalty, meaning the adviser doesn't put its own interest ahead of yours and tells you about conflicts it has.

A broker works under Regulation Best Interest, which the SEC adopted on the same day and which has applied since June 30, 2020. It requires a broker to act in a retail customer's best interest when recommending a security or a strategy, and not to put the firm's interest first. The rule has four parts: disclosure, care, handling conflicts of interest, and compliance procedures. The main difference from the adviser's duty is timing. The broker's obligation attaches to each recommendation; unless the broker agrees to it, there's no duty to keep watching the account afterwards.

Insurance agents are licensed by their state. For annuities, the state insurance commissioners' model rule was revised in 2020 to require agents to act in the buyer's best interest, and most states have adopted a version of it. Other insurance sales are governed by each state's own rules.

All three standards leave conflicts of interest in place and require them to be disclosed and managed, so how a person is paid deserves as much attention as the standard they work under.

Fee-only, fee-based and commission

  • Fee-only means the advisor's only pay is what clients pay directly: a percentage, a flat fee or an hourly rate. No commissions from anyone. That's the definition used by NAPFA, the association of fee-only advisors.
  • Fee-based sounds the same and means something else. It's generally used for advisors who charge fees and also take commissions on some products.
  • Commission means the advisor is paid by the fund company or insurer when you buy. You don't write a check, but the cost is built into the product's price or its yearly charges.

Commissions aren't wrong in themselves. Someone who needs one term life policy, or who buys a few funds and holds them for decades, can pay less through a commission than through a fee charged every year. What they do is give the seller a reason to prefer one product over another, so it helps to know when one is being paid.

Checking a person's record

Looking someone up is free and takes a few minutes. The SEC says that unlicensed, unregistered people commit much of the investment fraud in the US, so the first thing to confirm is that the person is registered at all.

  • BrokerCheck, run by FINRA, covers brokers. It shows where the person works, ten years of employment history, licenses and exams passed, and any customer complaints, arbitration awards, regulatory actions, terminations and bankruptcies.
  • The SEC's adviser search, called Investment Adviser Public Disclosure, covers adviser firms and their representatives, including most state-registered firms, and links across to BrokerCheck. The search box on Investor.gov leads to the same records.
  • Form ADV Part 2 is the adviser's brochure: a plain-English description of its services, its fees, its conflicts of interest and any disciplinary history. Advisers must give it to clients, and it's posted on the adviser search. A supplement covers the individual people who'll advise you.
  • Form CRS, the relationship summary, is a short document that both brokers and advisers must give retail investors. It covers services, fees, conflicts, the standard of conduct that applies and whether the firm or its people have a disciplinary history, and it ends with questions to ask.

One old complaint doesn't have to rule someone out, since complaints can be filed and later denied. A pattern does matter: several complaints about the same kind of product, or a string of short stays at different firms.

What 1% costs over a lifetime

A yearly percentage comes out of the whole balance every year, so its cost compounds along with the savings. Take a saver who starts with $10,000 and adds $500 per month for 30 years, earning 5% per year after inflation before costs. After 30 years the saver paying 1% has $375,069 instead of $450,907. That's $75,838 less, or 16.8% of what the no-fee balance would have been. At 0.25% the gap is $20,490.

The fee doesn't stop at retirement. In the US record since 1926, the highest starting withdrawal that lasted 30 years in every case, for a mix of 60% stocks and 40% bonds, was 3.79% of the balance with no fee and 3.36% with a 1% fee. Paying 1% through both stages leaves $12,602 per year to spend instead of $17,089, about a quarter less, from the same $190,000 of saving. Against 0.25%, a typical price for automated management, 1% leaves about a fifth less. Whether the fee is worth paying depends on what the advice adds, and for some people it adds more than this.

The low-cost end: robo-advisors and target-date funds

Two products do the investment-management part of an advisor's job for much less.

A robo-advisor is an online service that asks about your goals and tolerance for risk, puts you in a portfolio of low-cost funds, and rebalances it automatically. Morningstar's 2025 review of 16 of them found a median advisory fee of 0.25% per year, on top of the funds' own costs. Some include access to human planners at a higher price or a higher minimum.

A target-date fund is a single fund that holds a diversified mix and shifts it from stocks toward bonds as its target year approaches. Morningstar's 2026 report put the average expense ratio, weighted by assets, at 0.27% for 2025, with the cheapest at 0.04%. Many 401(k) plans offer one as the default. How much in stocks explains how their glide paths work.

Neither one knows about your spouse's pension, your tax bracket or the house you plan to sell. They manage a portfolio. They don't decide when to claim Social Security, which account to draw from first or how much to convert to Roth, and those decisions are where a planner's time usually goes.

What an advisor is good for

An advisor is unlikely to earn the fee by picking investments that beat the market. Most professional fund managers fall short of their index after costs, and the advisory fee comes on top. The cases where advice pays are these.

  • Behavior in a crash. The most expensive mistake available to an investor is selling after a large fall and waiting to get back in. Someone you trust who talks you out of it once can pay for years of fees. When markets fall covers what's at stake.
  • Tax and withdrawal planning. Which accounts to draw from, how much to convert to Roth in the low-income years, when to claim Social Security, how to stay under the Medicare and health-insurance income lines. These are worth real money and are easy to get wrong. Which account to draw from first and Roth conversions show the size of it.
  • A one-time review. A second pair of eyes on a plan before you retire can catch a gap in health cover, a pension election or a beneficiary form you'd have missed. The five years before you retire lists what such a review should cover.
  • Getting it done at all. A plan that exists and is followed beats a better plan that's never made, and for a couple it means the partner who doesn't follow the money has someone to call.

Vanguard's Advisor's Alpha

The best-known attempt to put a number on this is Vanguard's. It introduced the idea of "Advisor's Alpha" in 2001 and in 2014 estimated that an advisor following its framework could add "up to, or even exceed, 3% in net returns". Its 2025 edition breaks the estimate into parts.

Vanguard's own caveats are worth as much as the headline:

  • The 3% is not a yearly figure. Vanguard writes that it means 3 percentage points of extra net return "over an unspecified period of time", and that the largest gains arrive intermittently, mostly in periods of panic or euphoria.
  • It's measured against what Vanguard calls the "average" client experience: a portfolio run without these practices, in costlier funds, left to drift, and sold in a fall. Someone already in low-cost index funds who stays put has collected several of the rows before paying anyone.
  • The parts can't simply be added. Several are zero for a given person: asset location for someone with only a 401(k), tax-loss harvesting for someone with no taxable account.
  • The paper is written for advisors, who are Vanguard's customers. It's a framework for what good advice consists of, and it was never a measured average of what clients received.

The largest row is behavior, which is the one a fund can't supply. The estimate also argues for a particular kind of advisor: one who uses low-cost funds and spends the time on planning, taxes and keeping clients steady.

Questions to ask before hiring

  • Are you a fiduciary for everything you do for me, on every account, and will you put that in writing?
  • How are you paid, and by whom? Do you or your firm receive anything from the products you recommend?
  • What would I pay in total each year, in dollars, including the funds' own costs?
  • Are you registered with the SEC, a state or FINRA? Can I have your Form CRS and your Form ADV Part 2, including the supplement about you?
  • Have you or your firm ever been disciplined by a regulator or had a customer complaint? (Then check the answer.)
  • What exactly is included: tax planning, Social Security, a withdrawal plan, insurance, estate basics? How often will we meet?
  • Which firm will hold my money, and will the statements come to me directly from it?
  • What does it cost to leave?

A good advisor answers all of these without discomfort, in plain words and in dollars.

Warning signs

The SEC's list of red flags for investment fraud starts with the ones below. Any of them is reason to stop.

  • The person or firm doesn't appear in BrokerCheck or the SEC's adviser search.
  • Guaranteed returns, "risk-free" investments, or results that sound too good to be true.
  • Pressure to decide today.
  • Credentials you can't verify with the body that issues them.
  • A request to pay by wire to a personal account, by gift card or by credit card.

Others aren't fraud but deserve a pause:

  • The fee can't be stated in dollars, or the answer to "how are you paid?" changes the subject.
  • A first meeting that leads straight to one product, especially one with a surrender charge for leaving in the first several years.
  • A recommendation to move an old 401(k) with no comparison of the costs before and after. The old 401(k) lesson covers what to compare.
  • Past returns offered as the reason to hire them.

When doing it yourself is reasonable

Plenty of people manage their own retirement saving well. It tends to work when the situation is ordinary (a workplace plan, an IRA, perhaps a taxable account), when the investments are a target-date fund or a few broad index funds, and when you've already lived through a large fall without selling. The work in the saving years is mostly a matter of contributing steadily and leaving the portfolio alone, and the lessons in the Saving and Investing tracks cover it.

Help earns more at particular moments: the years around retirement, when a dozen linked decisions arrive together; an inheritance, a business sale or stock options; a divorce or a spouse's death; or any time you notice you're avoiding the subject. Those are also the cases where paying by the hour or for a single plan fits best, because the need is a set of decisions that get made once.

So there's a middle path between full service and none: manage the portfolio yourself in low-cost funds, and pay a fee-only planner a few thousand dollars for a plan before retiring and a check every few years. Whether full service at 1% is worth it instead depends on how much of the list above you'd really do on your own, and only you can judge that.

Common questions

Is an advisor at my bank or my 401(k) provider a fiduciary?

It depends on how the person is registered and which service you're using, and one person can be an adviser for one account and a broker for another. The firm's Form CRS says which standards apply. Ask directly, and ask for it in writing.

Can I negotiate the fee?

Often, yes. The SEC notes that what you pay an adviser depends on the contract you negotiate. Published fee schedules step down with size, and many firms will discuss the rate or offer planning without investment management.

Does a higher fee buy better returns?

There's no reason to expect it. The fee comes straight out of the return, so a higher-cost advisor has to add more just to match a cheaper one. Judge an advisor by planning, tax work and service, which you can observe, more than by promised performance.

Is my money safe if the advisor's firm fails?

Your investments should be held by a separate custodian, usually a large brokerage, in your own name, so they aren't the advisor's property. If a brokerage firm itself fails, the Securities Investor Protection Corporation works to return customers' securities and cash. It doesn't cover a fall in the value of your investments.

I only want a retirement plan checked. Who do I look for?

A fee-only planner who works hourly or per plan, usually a CFP, and for tax-heavy questions a CPA or enrolled agent. Say at the start that you want a one-time review and no ongoing management, and ask for the price before the first meeting.

  • "Advisor" covers adviser representatives, brokers and insurance agents. Advisers are fiduciaries for the whole relationship; brokers must act in your best interest when they recommend something.
  • Look everyone up on BrokerCheck or the SEC's adviser search, and read Form CRS and Form ADV Part 2.
  • Median 2024 prices: 1% of assets per year, a $4,500 yearly retainer, $300 per hour, or $3,000 for a plan.
