Your net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe. It's the one figure that takes in your whole financial life at once: savings, home, loans, all of it. It's also easy to misread, because a dollar of home equity, a dollar in a 401(k) and a dollar in a checking account are not worth the same to a retirement plan.

What net worth is

Add up what you own at today's values. Those are your assets. Add up what you owe. Those are your liabilities, or debts. Subtract the second total from the first.

Here's a made-up household to follow through the lesson. Sam and Jordan are in their early 40s, own a home, and have been saving through work for about fifteen years.

Net worth is a snapshot of one day. Income and spending are flows, measured per month or per year; net worth is the pool they fill or drain. Someone can earn a lot and have a small net worth, and a retiree with almost no income can have a large one.

It can also be negative. A new graduate with $30,000 of student loans and $4,000 in the bank has a net worth of −$26,000. That's common early on, and it says more about age than about habits. What matters at that stage is which way the figure is moving.

What counts, and what doesn't

The test for an asset is whether you could sell it or spend it. The test for a debt is whether someone could make you pay it.

Assets to include

  • Cash: checking, savings, money market funds, certificates of deposit.
  • Investments in taxable accounts: stocks, bonds, mutual funds and ETFs at today's market value.
  • Retirement accounts at their current balance: 401(k), 403(b), IRAs, Roth accounts, HSAs. Count only the vested part of an employer match; The employer match explains vesting.
  • Real estate: your home and any other property.
  • A business you own, or a share of one, at a cautious estimate of what a buyer would pay.
  • The cash value of a whole life policy. The death benefit isn't yours to spend, and term insurance has no cash value at all.

Debts to include

  • Mortgages and home equity loans or lines.
  • Car loans, student loans and personal loans.
  • Credit card balances, including one you plan to pay off this month and one on a 0% offer.
  • Taxes you owe and haven't paid, and medical bills on a payment plan.

What stays off the list

  • Your salary and future raises. Earning power is valuable, but you can't sell it.
  • An inheritance you expect. It isn't yours yet, and plans change.
  • Stock options or shares that haven't vested.
  • Furniture, clothes and electronics. They cost a lot to buy and sell for very little.
  • Money set aside for someone else. A 529 account for a child is legally yours, so it belongs in the total, but many people list it on its own line because it's already spoken for.

One trap with retirement plans: if you've borrowed from your 401(k), check whether the statement balance already leaves out the borrowed money. If it does, listing the loan as a debt as well would subtract it twice.

Putting a value on a home, a car and a pension

Bank and brokerage balances are easy, since the statement gives the number. The hard items are the ones with no statement.

A home

Use what similar homes nearby have sold for recently. Listing prices, online estimates and what you paid plus what you spent on the kitchen are all weaker guides. Lean low. A home's value on paper is a guess until someone writes a check, and a sale comes with agent commissions and closing costs that come out of the price. Your home equity is the value minus the mortgage and any other loan secured by the house: for Sam and Jordan, $430,000 − $268,000 = $162,000.

Pick one method and keep to it. If you update the home's value once per year from the same kind of source, the year-to-year change means something. If you revise it whenever a neighbor's sale flatters it, you're only measuring your mood.

A car

Use the price a private buyer would pay today, which a used-car pricing guide will estimate from the model, year and mileage. Expect it to fall every year. Many people leave cars out altogether, on the reasoning that they'll always need one and can't spend it. Either choice is fine if you make it once and stay with it. If you leave the car out, you can leave its loan out too, but that flatters the total when the loan is larger than the car is worth.

A pension or Social Security

A pension that will pay $2,000 per month for life is plainly worth something. One rough way to size it borrows the 25 times rule from From spending to a target: income of $24,000 per year does the work of about $600,000 of savings. On the same arithmetic, a Social Security benefit of $30,000 per year stands in for about $750,000. The rule assumes income that rises with prices, which Social Security does and most private pensions don't, so a pension with no inflation adjustment is worth less than the multiple suggests.

Even so, most people leave both off the balance sheet, and so does the Federal Reserve when it measures household wealth. There are good reasons:

  • You can't sell either one, borrow against it, or leave it to your children. Payments stop when you die, or when your spouse does if there's a survivor benefit.
  • The value depends on guesses about how long you'll live, future inflation and interest rates. Change one assumption and the "asset" moves by six figures.
  • The benefit isn't final until you claim. It depends on how long you keep working and when you start, which How your benefit is figured covers.

A retirement plan handles them a better way: as income. It subtracts the pension and Social Security from what you'll spend each year and asks your savings to cover the rest. They count in full that way without ever appearing in net worth. Annuities and pensions goes into how to compare a pension with a lump sum.

Total, investable and liquid

The $492,000 at the top of this lesson is total net worth. Two narrower versions answer different questions.

  • Investable assets are the money you have in cash and investments, in any kind of account. For Sam and Jordan that's $28,000 + $40,000 + $210,000 + $45,000 = $323,000. The home and the cars are left out, because they're in use.
  • Liquid net worth is what you could reach within a few days without a penalty or a forced sale: cash and the brokerage account, $68,000 here. Definitions vary. Some people subtract short-term debts, and some count retirement accounts at a discount. The point is to know how much is within reach if you lost your income, which is the subject of An emergency fund comes first.

Which one a retirement plan runs on

A plan runs on investable assets, and usually on a little less than all of them. Retirement spending is paid from accounts you can draw down year after year. Home equity pays for nothing while you live in the house. It can be turned into money later by moving somewhere cheaper or borrowing against it, and Your home in retirement covers those routes, but a careful plan treats it as a reserve and leaves it out of the main sum.

It's also usual to hold the emergency fund apart, since it has its own job. Take the $28,000 of cash out of Sam and Jordan's $323,000 and their plan starts from $295,000: the 401(k)s, the Roth IRAs and the brokerage account. That's 60% of their net worth. Two households with the same total can be in very different shape for retirement, depending on how much of it is the house.

What taxes do to each account

A balance sheet lists every account at face value, but the tax still owed on each one is different.

  • A traditional 401(k) or IRA has never been taxed. Every dollar that comes out is taxed as income, so part of the balance belongs to the government. Take money out before 59½ and there's usually a 10% additional tax on top.
  • A Roth account was taxed on the way in. Withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, so a Roth dollar is a whole dollar.
  • A taxable brokerage account sits in between. You already paid tax on the money you put in. Only the gain is taxed when you sell, and for investments held more than a year the federal rate is 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on your income. For 2026 the 0% rate reaches up to $49,450 of taxable income for a single filer and $98,900 for a couple.
  • Cash has no tax left to pay.

Nobody knows their future tax rate exactly, so this is an estimate. A reasonable one for a traditional account is the average rate you expect to pay on withdrawals in retirement, which for many households is lower than the bracket they're in while working, because the first slice of income each year falls under the standard deduction and the lowest brackets. Brackets: marginal and effective explains the difference between the two rates.

Suppose Sam and Jordan expect to pay about 15% on what they withdraw. Their $210,000 of traditional money is worth about $178,500. The brokerage account holds $12,000 of gain, so at a 15% rate it's worth $38,200. The Roth IRAs and the cash count in full. Their $323,000 of investable assets comes to about $289,700 after tax, roughly a tenth less than the statement total.

You don't need to redo your balance sheet this way every time. Two things are worth keeping in mind. A household whose savings are nearly all traditional has less to spend than its total suggests. And two savers with the same balance aren't equally well off if one holds Roth money and the other traditional, which is part of the choice covered in Traditional or Roth.

Tracking it, and what the trend shows

A single net worth figure tells you little. A column of them, taken the same way over years, tells you a good deal.

Once or twice per year is enough for most people; some like every quarter. Pick a date, write down every balance as of that day, and use the same method each time: the same source for the home's value, the same choice about the cars. A spreadsheet with one row per date works. Checking every week adds nothing, since over short stretches almost all of the movement is the market.

Net worth changes for four reasons, and it helps to keep them apart:

  • What you saved: new money into savings and investment accounts.
  • Debt you paid down. Each mortgage payment is part interest, which is gone, and part principal, which raises your net worth as surely as a deposit does.
  • What the markets did to your investments.
  • Changes in what your home and other property are worth.

Three things show up in a record like this that no single figure can tell you.

The first is whether you're really saving what you think. Take the change in net worth over a year and set aside the market's part. What's left should be close to your contributions plus the debt you paid off. If it's well short, money is going somewhere your budget doesn't show. Your savings rate is the lever covers how to measure the saving itself.

The second is how to read a bad year. In year 3 of the chart the household's net worth fell by about $4,600 while they put in $24,000. Seen alone, that looks like failure. Next to the dotted line, it's a market fall that their saving nearly cancelled.

The third is the handover from saving to growth. In the early years the solid line stays close to the dotted one, because contributions are most of the change. By year 10 the markets have added about $127,000 on top of the $240,000 the household put in, and that share keeps rising. Time does most of the work explains why.

Level and trend can also disagree. A net worth of $500,000 that was $500,000 five years ago and one that was $250,000 five years ago are the same figure and very different situations.

Typical figures by age

The best measure of what American households own is the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, taken every three years. The latest, the 2025 survey, was published in October 2026 and interviewed 4,367 families. It counts homes, vehicles, businesses, and financial and retirement accounts, less all debts. It leaves out Social Security and traditional pensions, for the reasons given earlier.

Across all families the median net worth was $215,900 and the mean was $1,241,500. The median is the family in the middle: half have more, half have less. The mean is the total divided by the number of families.

Why the mean is so much higher

The mean is almost six times the median because wealth is very unevenly spread. Picture ten households: nine have $100,000 each and one has $10 million. The median is $100,000. The mean is $1,090,000, a figure that describes none of them. A few very large fortunes pull the mean far above what a typical family has, and the same thing happens in every age group. Among families headed by someone 55 to 64, the median is $411,900 and the mean is $1,878,000.

So compare yourself with the median for your age if you compare at all. A few other points help in reading the chart:

  • Much of the middle family's wealth is its home. Homeowners had a median net worth of $458,900; renters and others, $10,600. Among owners, the median home was worth $375,000 and the median equity in it was $230,000.
  • The figures are per family, so a couple's combined wealth is being compared with a single person's.
  • The under-35 median, $33,000, was 23% lower than in 2022 after inflation, while the 75-and-over median rose 37% to $504,900, the highest of any age group.

These medians describe what people have, which isn't the same as what they need. A household that spends $40,000 per year and one that spends $120,000 need very different sums. A better yardstick is your own investable assets against your own target, which What a retirement plan answers walks through.

Common mistakes

  • Valuing the home at a hopeful price. Use recent sales, lean low, and remember the cost of selling.
  • Reading total net worth as retirement readiness. If most of it is the house, the plan has less to work with than the total suggests.
  • Treating traditional balances as spendable in full. Some of that money is tax you haven't paid yet.
  • Leaving debts off: the car loan, the 0% card, the loan from a relative.
  • Changing the method from one year to the next. Adding the cars in one year or switching the source for the home's value creates jumps that have nothing to do with your finances.
  • Checking too often. Daily and weekly changes are market noise, and watching them invites selling at the wrong moment. When markets fall covers that.
  • Measuring against the mean, which a handful of very wealthy families set.
  • Chasing the figure itself. Paying extra on a low-rate mortgage and investing the same money both raise net worth today, and which leaves you better off later is a separate question. Paying down debt covers it.

Common questions

Should I count my home?

In total net worth, yes, at a cautious value less the mortgage. In the figure a retirement plan runs on, usually not, because you can't spend the house while you live in it. Count it there only if you have a firm plan to sell and move somewhere cheaper, and then count only the difference.

Is it bad to have a negative net worth?

Not by itself. Student loans or a new mortgage with a small down payment can put a young household below zero for years. What matters is the direction. If the figure rises every year because debts are shrinking and savings are growing, the plan is working.

Do I count my 401(k) at its full balance?

On an ordinary balance sheet, yes, counting only the vested part of any employer money. Just remember that a traditional balance comes with a tax bill, so the amount you can spend is lower, by 12% to 22% in the examples above, depending on your rate in retirement.

How often should I work it out?

Once or twice per year suits most people. That's often enough to catch a problem and seldom enough that the change reflects your saving more than last week's market.

My net worth fell this year even though I saved. What went wrong?

Probably nothing you did. Once investments are several times your yearly saving, a market fall can outweigh a year of contributions. Check the part you control, what you put in and what you paid off, and judge the year on that.

In short

  • Net worth is what you own minus what you owe, on one day. It can be negative early on.
  • Count what you could sell or spend, at cautious values, and every debt. Most people leave out pensions and Social Security and treat them as income.
  • A retirement plan runs on investable assets, not the total. Home equity is a reserve.
  • A traditional dollar is worth less than a Roth dollar, because tax is still owed on it.
  • Track it once or twice per year the same way each time, and watch the part you control: saving and debt paid down.
