Most households get little or no tax break for what they give, because they take the standard deduction. In retirement that changes: you may hold shares with decades of gain in them, and from 70½ you can give straight from an IRA. This lesson covers the rules as they stand for 2026, when three changes from the 2025 tax law took effect, and what each way of giving is worth.

Why most gifts earn no deduction

A gift to charity is an itemized deduction. You only itemize when your itemized total (state and local taxes, mortgage interest, medical costs over a floor, and gifts) is larger than your standard deduction, and for 2026 that's $32,200 for a married couple and $16,100 for a single filer, with more at 65. About nine in ten households take the standard deduction. Deductions and credits explains the choice.

Retirees are even less likely to itemize. The mortgage is often paid off, state income tax falls with income, and a couple who are both 65 have a standard deduction of $35,500 before the senior deduction. A retired couple with $9,000 of state and property taxes and no mortgage would have to give more than $26,500 in a year before the first dollar of giving lowered their tax under the old rules.

What changed in 2026

The 2025 tax law, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, changed three things about the charitable deduction, all starting with the 2026 tax year.

Each has details that matter.

The deduction for non-itemizers is for cash only (checks and card payments count; shares, clothing and household goods don't), given to a public charity in that tax year. Gifts to a donor-advised fund or a supporting organization don't qualify. It comes off taxable income after AGI is figured, so it doesn't help with anything that keys off AGI, such as Medicare premiums or how much of your Social Security is taxable. Unlike the temporary version Congress allowed in 2020 and 2021, it has no end date.

The 0.5% floor applies to an itemizer's giving as a whole, once per year. The part under the floor is generally lost: it carries forward only in a year when your gifts are also large enough to run into the AGI limits covered below.

The 35% cap works by trimming the itemized total by 2/37 of the amount that falls in the 37% bracket. It applies to all itemized deductions, charity included.

The same law made permanent the higher ceiling for cash gifts, 60% of AGI, which had been due to drop back to 50%.

Bunching, and donor-advised funds

If your itemized total sits a little below the standard deduction every year, steady giving earns you only the $1,000 or $2,000. Bunching means giving two or three years' worth in one year, itemizing that year, and taking the standard deduction in the others. Deductions and credits works through an example in which a couple giving $8,000 per year deducts $5,000 more over two years by giving $16,000 every other year.

The 2026 rules add two wrinkles. The 0.5% floor comes off in each year you itemize, so itemizing every other year means losing it half as often. And in the off years the non-itemizer deduction is still there, as long as you make that year's gifts in cash and directly.

The obvious problem with bunching is that the charities you support would rather have steady income. A donor-advised fund solves that. It's an account at a sponsoring charity, which most large brokerages and community foundations run. You contribute cash or shares and take the deduction that year. The money is invested, and you recommend grants from it to charities whenever you like. The gift to the fund is final. Legally the sponsor controls the money and you only advise, though sponsors follow recommendations to eligible charities as a matter of course.

A donor-advised fund costs something to run, usually an annual fee on the balance plus the costs of the funds inside it. And it's shut out of both of the breaks aimed at people who don't itemize: gifts to it don't count for the $1,000 or $2,000 deduction, and an IRA can't make a qualified charitable distribution to it.

Giving shares instead of cash

If you itemize and you own shares in a taxable account that have risen a lot, giving the shares is better than giving cash. For shares held more than one year, you deduct their full market value, and neither you nor the charity pays tax on the gain. (Capital gains and how to harvest them covers how that gain would otherwise be taxed.)

Take Sam and Jordan, who itemize, have $200,000 of AGI and want to give $10,000. They own fund shares worth $10,000 that they bought years ago for $2,000. They're in the 22% bracket, and their long-term gains are taxed at 15%.

They don't have to give up the investment, either. They can use the $10,000 of cash they'd have given to buy the same fund back the same day. They own what they owned before, with a basis of $10,000 where it used to be $2,000. The wash-sale rule doesn't come into it, because nothing was sold at a loss.

A few rules to know:

  • The shares must have been held more than one year. For shares held one year or less, the deduction is limited to what you paid.
  • Don't give shares that are below what you paid. Sell them, keep the capital loss for your own return, and give the cash.
  • The benefit depends on the tax you'd otherwise pay on the gain. If your gains would be taxed at 0% anyway, giving shares saves no more than giving cash.
  • If you take the standard deduction, giving shares gets you no deduction, since the non-itemizer deduction is for cash only. It does still remove the gain, which is worth having if you'd have sold those shares one day.
  • The transfer goes from your brokerage to the charity's. Allow a couple of weeks before year-end. A donor-advised fund is often the easiest way to give shares to small charities that can't accept them.

Giving from an IRA: the QCD

From the day you turn 70½, you can have your IRA custodian send money directly to a charity. This is a qualified charitable distribution, or QCD. The amount never enters your income. You get no deduction for it, and you don't need one: leaving the money out of income is worth at least as much as deducting it, and it works whether you itemize or not.

Required minimum distributions lists the basic rules. In brief:

  • The limit for 2026 is $111,000 per person, indexed to inflation. Each spouse has their own limit, from their own IRAs.
  • The age is 70½ on the day of the distribution, not just by the end of the year, even though required distributions now start at 73 or 75.
  • A QCD counts toward that year's required minimum distribution.
  • The payment must go from the custodian to the charity. A check from the IRA made out to the charity counts, even if it's mailed to you to pass on. A withdrawal paid to you and then given away doesn't.
  • The charity must be a public charity. Donor-advised funds, supporting organizations and most private foundations are excluded.
  • You can't receive anything in return. The law requires that the whole amount would have been deductible as a gift, so a QCD that buys a gala table or an auction item fails entirely.
  • It works from a traditional IRA, including an inherited one, but not from a 401(k), and not from a SEP or SIMPLE IRA that's still receiving employer contributions.

A QCD compared with a cash gift

Take a married couple, both 74, with $50,000 of Social Security and $60,000 of required distributions from their IRAs. They give $10,000 per year and take the standard deduction.

The QCD saves $2,220, more than nine times what the cash gift saves. Two things are at work. The cash gift's deduction covers only $2,000 of the $10,000, while the QCD takes the whole gift out of income. And taking $10,000 out of AGI also makes $8,500 less of their Social Security taxable, so their taxable income falls by $18,500. How retirement income is taxed explains why an IRA dollar can count nearly twice in that range.

The size of the saving depends on where your income sits. The chart runs the same $10,000 gift for the same couple at different levels of IRA withdrawals.

Below about $30,000 of withdrawals this couple owes no federal tax, so no kind of gift saves any. Above that the QCD is ahead at every income.

Lower AGI helps outside the income tax as well. Medicare's income surcharge, IRMAA, charges a whole tier for being a dollar over a line. At 2026 rates, a single retiree whose income would be $113,000 pays about $1,148 per year more than one at $109,000 or less. A $5,000 QCD in place of a $5,000 withdrawal would bring them under. (IRMAA looks at income from two years earlier, so this takes planning ahead.) Lower AGI also lowers the 7.5% floor on medical deductions and leaves more room under the thresholds for the senior deduction and the 3.8% investment income tax.

Details that trip people up

Order matters within the year. The first money out of an IRA each year counts toward the required distribution. If you take the full distribution in January and send a QCD in November, the QCD is still left out of income, but you've already been taxed on the full required amount. Make the QCDs first, or have them make up the distribution.

Your custodian's Form 1099-R reports a QCD like any other withdrawal; a code that marks it is optional for custodians in 2026. Telling the IRS is up to you: on your return you enter the full distribution on the IRA line and the smaller taxable amount beside it, and check the QCD box on that line. People who forget pay tax on their own gift.

If you kept working past 70½ and deducted IRA contributions, there's an offset. Deductible contributions made for the year you turned 70½ or any later year reduce the amount of your QCDs that can be left out of income, dollar for dollar, until they're used up. Say Jordan deducted $7,000 at 71 and $8,000 at 72, then makes a $10,000 QCD at 73. None of it is excluded, and $5,000 of the offset remains. The next year's $10,000 QCD is excluded only as to $5,000. The part that isn't excluded is taxable income, though it can still be claimed as an ordinary gift by someone who itemizes.

There's also a one-time election to send up to $55,000 (the 2026 figure) as a QCD to a charitable remainder trust or a charitable gift annuity, arrangements that pay you or your spouse an income for life and leave the rest to charity. It can be used in one year only, the trust or annuity can hold nothing but QCD money, a gift annuity must start paying at least 5% within a year, and the payments you receive are fully taxable. At that size it's usually a gift annuity, since a trust costs more to set up and run.

Leaving an IRA to charity

If you plan to leave something to charity at death, which account it comes from matters more than the amount. A charity pays no income tax, so it receives a traditional IRA whole. A person who inherits the same IRA pays income tax on every dollar withdrawn, usually within ten years. Shares in a taxable account are the reverse: heirs receive them with the basis stepped up to the value at death, so they owe nothing on the gain to that point.

Suppose you have a $200,000 traditional IRA and $200,000 of shares in a taxable account, and want to leave half to a charity and half to your daughter, who is in the 24% bracket.

To do this, name the charity on the IRA's beneficiary form, as the sole beneficiary or for a percentage. Don't rely on a will: the beneficiary form controls who gets the account, and routing the IRA through the estate complicates the tax treatment. Beneficiaries and estate basics covers the forms. The same logic puts Roth accounts last in line for charity, since a Roth is tax-free to a person and that's wasted on an organization that pays no tax anyway.

The reasoning also bears on Roth conversions. Converting means paying tax now on money that, if it's headed to a charity, would never have been taxed at all. And for the order you draw on accounts, it's a reason not to run a traditional IRA down to nothing if a bequest is part of the plan.

AGI limits and carryforwards

For itemizers, a year's deduction for gifts can't exceed a share of AGI. The limits rarely matter for ordinary giving, but they do in a bunching year, or when a large block of shares goes into a donor-advised fund.

For example, with $150,000 of AGI, a gift of $70,000 of long-held shares to a donor-advised fund is capped at $45,000 this year. The other $25,000 carries forward, and it's subject to the same 30% limit in each later year. A carryforward not used within five years is lost, and it only helps in years when you itemize. QCDs sit outside all of this, with their own $111,000 limit.

The records you need

A charitable deduction can be denied for missing paperwork even when nobody doubts the gift was made. What's required depends on the gift.

The acknowledgment has to exist by the time you file, or by the return's due date if that's earlier. One obtained later doesn't count. If you got something in return, such as a dinner, only the amount above its value is a gift.

Seeing it in your own plan

Income Tax, in retirement mode, will show what a QCD is worth to you. Enter your income as it would be with no giving, note the federal tax, then lower the traditional withdrawal by the amount you'd send to charity from the IRA. The drop in tax is the QCD's saving, including its effect on how much of your Social Security is taxed. For a cash gift, compare the standard deduction with your itemized total including the gift.

The RMD calculator shows how large your required distributions will be at each age, which is the pool QCDs can draw on. Roth Conversions is the place to test converting less when part of the IRA is meant for charity.

Common questions

Can I make a QCD from my 401(k)?

No. QCDs come only from IRAs. If the money is in a 401(k), roll it to an IRA first. A required distribution from the 401(k) for that year can't be rolled over, so plan the rollover for an earlier year.

Can I take both the QCD and a deduction for the same gift?

No. A QCD is left out of income, and that's the whole benefit. But QCDs don't use up the non-itemizer deduction, so a couple could give through QCDs and still deduct $2,000 of separate cash gifts.

I'm 66 and take the standard deduction. What are my options?

Until 70½ there's no QCD. The $1,000 or $2,000 deduction for cash gifts is available every year. For larger giving, bunching several years' gifts into one, often through a donor-advised fund and with appreciated shares, is the usual way to get over the standard deduction. Some people in their sixties simply wait and do their larger giving by QCD from 70½.

Does a QCD have to be part of my required distribution?

No. You can make QCDs from 70½, before required distributions start, and you can give more than the required amount, up to the annual limit. Before RMDs begin, a QCD still shrinks the IRA that later distributions will be figured on.

Do gifts to a relative or a crowdfunding page for someone count?

No. Only gifts to qualified organizations are deductible, and a gift earmarked for one person isn't. The IRS has a search tool for checking whether an organization qualifies.

In short

  • From 2026, people who take the standard deduction can deduct $1,000 of cash gifts ($2,000 joint); itemizers lose the first 0.5% of AGI, and the top bracket saves 35% at most.
  • Bunching several years' gifts into one, often through a donor-advised fund, is how a household near the standard deduction gets a deduction at all.
  • Giving long-held shares that have risen, when you itemize, earns the same deduction as cash and removes the capital gain.
  • From 70½, a QCD of up to $111,000 per person in 2026 goes from an IRA to charity without entering income. It counts toward the RMD and lowers AGI.
